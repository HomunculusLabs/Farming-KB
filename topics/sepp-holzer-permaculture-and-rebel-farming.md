# Sepp Holzer Permaculture and Rebel Farming

Sepp Holzer (born 1942) is an Austrian farmer who has spent over five decades
developing a comprehensive system of ecological agriculture on his family's
steep mountain property, the Krameterhof, in Lungau, Salzburg. Situated at
elevations between 1,100 and 1,500 metres in a region nicknamed the "Austrian
Siberia," the Krameterhof demonstrates that productive, profitable farming is
possible in marginal climates without chemical inputs, irrigation, or
specialised equipment. Holzer's approach predates the formal permaculture
design system codified by Bill Mollison and David Holmgren in the 1970s; he
arrived at his methods independently through childhood observation and decades
of hands-on experimentation, and his work represents one of the most thoroughly
tested large-scale applications of permaculture principles anywhere in the
world.

What distinguishes Holzer from many permaculture practitioners is his
willingness to work at commercial scale using mechanical earthworks where
appropriate, his emphasis on old domesticated breeds and crop varieties rather
than exclusively wild species, and his insistence that ecological farming must
be economically viable. He does not reject technology outright but rejects
dependency on it. An excavator is a tool; a chemical fertiliser company is a
chain. The Krameterhof operates profitably across 45 hectares, with diverse
revenue streams including fruit, livestock, mushrooms, fish, aquatic plants,
tours, and training courses. By eliminating input costs and creating multiple
income channels, the farm achieves financial independence that conventional
operations, burdened by rising chemical costs and concentrated market risk,
cannot match.

Holzer's philosophy extends well beyond agronomic technique into a broader
critique of modern industrial agriculture, agricultural policy, and the
relationship between humans and the natural world. He calls himself the
"Rebel Farmer," a title reflecting his lifelong defiance of agricultural
authorities, EU regulations, and the subsidy structures he believes have
enslaved farmers and degraded the land. His critique encompasses the
destruction of soil fertility through chemical farming, the loss of crop
biodiversity through corporate seed monopolisation, and the erosion of
traditional farming knowledge through agricultural education that serves
industry rather than farmers. This article synthesises Holzer's system from
key concept pages of the LLM Wiki, covering his philosophy, landscape design,
earthworks, water management, tree cultivation, livestock integration, seed
autonomy, mushroom cultivation, aquaculture, urban applications, and his
plan for addressing world hunger.

## Philosophy: Nature as Teacher

At the foundation of Holzer's system lies a deceptively simple principle:
work with nature rather than against it. This is not mere sentiment but a
rigorous methodology rooted in decades of observation. Holzer argues that the
belief that nature can be improved upon is a fundamental mistake. A healthy
ecosystem regulates itself. The farmer's role is to create conditions where
this self-regulation can occur, not to intervene at every sign of trouble.
This requires patience, observation, and a willingness to let natural
processes take their course.

Holzer's rule of thumb for all situations encapsulates his empathetic approach:
"I imagine I am the other -- the pig, the cow, the earthworm, the sunflower
or the other human being. Would I feel good if I was in their place? If not I
have to find out what is wrong." This practice of seeing the world from other
organisms' perspective is the starting point for "reading nature," the
foundational skill that precedes all design work. Reading nature means
learning to interpret what the landscape communicates through vegetation
patterns, erosion paths, animal behaviour, soil conditions, and microclimate
variations.

Every farming problem, in Holzer's view, is a symptom of an underlying
imbalance. Pests indicate weakened plants or disrupted predator populations.
Weeds indicate soil conditions such as compaction, acidity, or excess nitrogen.
Disease indicates stress from wrong location, wrong variety, or soil
degradation. Rather than fighting symptoms with chemicals, he addresses root
causes. Slugs are managed by creating habitat for toads through water
landscapes. Aphids are managed by providing habitat for ladybirds and
lacewings. Voles are managed by providing decoy plants rather than poisoning
them; the voles then aerate the soil and spread plants through their tunnels.

The philosophy rejects specialisation as dangerous. A diverse farm with many
income streams can react flexibly to market changes and unforeseeable events.
Holzer learned this when the Chernobyl disaster of 1986 wiped out his mushroom
income overnight. Specialisation creates only risk and dependence. He warns
that farm subsidies compound the problem: once a farmer adapts their operation
to subsidy criteria, they lose the ability to make independent decisions.
Nine out of ten farmers have given up in the last forty years, many losing
their farms to debt accumulated through systems that reward monoculture and
punish diversity.

## Reading the Land

Before any design work begins, the practitioner must develop what Holzer calls
the "aerial view." Looking down from an elevated position reveals patterns
invisible at ground level: water flow lines, contour patterns, vegetation
zones, erosion paths, and microclimate variations. The aerial view shows "the
dream of the landscape" -- what it would look like without human interference,
where water wants to go, where trees naturally grow, and how the land
organises itself.

The formal assessment process considers soil conditions, elevation, climate,
exposure, relief, drainage basins, previous land use, and existing plant
growth. Holzer emphasises walking the land extensively during different
seasons and weather conditions before making decisions. The aspect (the
direction the land faces) and elevation affect design enormously. At high
altitudes, from around 1,000 metres, the system must compensate for shorter
growing seasons and lower temperatures by maximising sunlight capture and
sheltering crops from wind through windbreaks of various fruit bushes, fruit
trees, and flowering shrubs at different heights.

Soil assessment uses simple, direct methods. The "finger test" determines soil
type by rolling fresh soil between the palms: sandy soil will not hold
together, medium soil can be rolled to pencil thickness, and heavy loam or
clay can be rolled even thinner. Holzer looks for crumbly topsoil with high
pore volume that holds water and nutrients like a sponge. Perhaps most
importantly, he recommends observing the land for at least a year before
making major design decisions, allowing the farmer to witness seasonal
variations, identify existing microclimates, and understand water movement.
No textbook can substitute for this direct, extended acquaintance with a
specific piece of land.

## Water: The Foundation of Life

Water is the single most important element in Holzer's permaculture system.
On the Krameterhof, a network of over 70 ponds, water gardens, wetlands, and
ditches spans the farm from 1,500 to 1,100 metres elevation. Most are
connected, with water used for multiple purposes as it flows downhill through
a cascade system. The principle is simple: keep water on the land as long as
possible, whether it arrives as rain, spring flow, or surface runoff. Where
there is wet soil, Holzer creates a pond, water garden, or wetland. In dry
places, he grows herbs that prefer semi-arid conditions like thyme, marjoram,
and sage.

Holzer's pond construction method is fundamentally different from conventional
approaches. He uses no plastic liners, arguing that they prevent natural pond
life from developing and cut the pond off from surrounding soil ecology.
Instead, he employs a "vibration method" in which water is filled to 30 to 40
centimetres depth and a small excavator bucket vibrates the subsoil 0.5 to 1
metre deep, compacting fine material into a watertight seal that works on
almost every subsoil type. Coarse excavated material stabilises walls and
banks, and islands and small biotopes are created within the pond for habitat
diversity.

Each pond serves multiple functions simultaneously. They produce fish
(including brown trout, arctic char, carp, tench, pike, wels catfish, and
koi) as well as crayfish and swan mussels. They provide gravity-fed
irrigation, fire protection water, microclimate moderation that protects
surrounding plantings from frost, wildlife habitat for amphibians and birds,
and reflective surfaces that bounce light into nearby gardens. The 100-metre
height difference across the farm also powers two Pelton wheel generators that
produce enough electricity for the entire farm, with water exiting cool and
oxygen-rich into lower fish ponds. A hydraulic ram pump operates without
external energy to supply drinking water to the mountain pasture at up to 15
bars of pressure. Every system cascades its output into the next, embodying
the principle that every element should serve multiple functions.

## Earthworks: Terraces, Raised Beds, and Hugelkultur

Earthworks are the physical infrastructure of Holzer's system. His approach is
pragmatic: mechanical diggers are used once during setup to loosen soil to
depth and introduce biomass, after which biological processes take over. The
excavated material builds terraces, raised beds, and water-retention
structures, with logs, branches, and green material buried deep to create
long-term fertility through slow decomposition.

Terraces are essential for working the steep slopes of the Krameterhof. They
prevent valuable humus from washing away, stop soil erosion, and contribute
to soil health. Holzer's terraces curve and meander following natural contours
rather than the straight lines of conventional terraces. This organic geometry
reduces erosion by eliminating concentrated flow paths, creates more edge
habitat (the most productive zone in any landscape), and makes the landscape
visually appealing. There should be no straight lines, corners, or steep
slopes. Large-scale projects are done gradually over multiple growing seasons,
with terraces created at the top, middle, and bottom of a slope first, then
filled in as stability is confirmed.

Raised beds are central to Holzer's growing system. A key breakthrough came
when he moved from using chipped wood (which compacts, acidifies soil, and
requires rebuilding every five to ten years) to incorporating whole logs and
branches into the beds. Bulky material rots down slowly, causing small shifts
that keep the structure loose and well-aerated. Tree trunks maintain balanced
moisture within the system for many years. These beds, essentially what modern
permaculturists call "hugelkultur," can last ten years or more without major
rebuilding. Beds are typically 1 to 1.5 metres high with sides angled at
least 45 degrees (60 to 70 degrees on heavy loam). On slopes, beds are placed
at an angle to the fall line so water distributes evenly.

The crater garden extends these principles further. Built as a bowl-shaped
depression surrounded by raised beds in a circular or semi-circular pattern,
the crater garden generates a humid microclimate ideal for growing a wide
range of crops. The sunken centre collects moisture while the raised edges
protect the interior from drying winds. In Burgenland, Holzer demonstrated
that a beneficial humid microclimate develops inside crater gardens during
summer, supporting plants that would not normally thrive in local conditions.
A pond or water garden in the centre combines raised bed cultivation with
moisture retention. Holzer explicitly rejects rigid templates, emphasising
that beds should vary according to function, location, and local conditions.

## Microclimate Creation

Creating microclimates is the technique that makes Holzer's most remarkable
achievements possible. At 1,100 to 1,500 metres elevation in one of Austria's
coldest regions, he grows kiwi, lemons, grapes, apricots, sweet chestnut,
cacti, and shiitake mushrooms. The principle is straightforward: by breaking
up the landscape with varied forms and features, the practitioner creates
special climatic conditions where plants that would otherwise not survive can
thrive. As the system develops, the land resembles a "rag rug" with many
irregularly shaped landforms producing numerous microclimates, and plant
diversity continues to increase.

Water bodies are the most powerful microclimate creators. During the day the
sun warms the water surface while deeper layers stay cool. At night heat is
slowly released, and through dew formation and evaporation the whole area is
kept moist. Proximity to water increases morning dew, reflects light onto
bank vegetation, provides frost protection, and supports plant growth through
humidity without irrigation. Holzer places ponds in front of niches and
suntraps so that the sun's rays reflect off the water surface into sheltered
areas, amplifying the heat-trapping effect.

Stones and rocks serve a similar thermal function. They store heat during the
day and release it at night. Growing herbs between rocks creates what Holzer
calls a "herb paradise" of different microclimatic niches. Wind management is
equally critical: Holzer builds earthworks against the prevailing wind, uses
deep-rooted trees as windbreaks, and creates curved shapes that deflect
rather than channel wind. He places plants in spots where it is just viable
for them to grow, making them stronger. The plants that survive in extreme
spots are the most valuable because they are the strongest and can acclimatise
to even more extreme locations, progressively extending the range of what is
possible.

## Fruit Forests and Tree Cultivation

Holzer's approach to fruit tree establishment is radically different from
conventional practice. Instead of planting grafted nursery trees in prepared
rows, he sows seeds directly onto the site, allows natural selection, and
grafts desired varieties onto the strongest seedlings. The best seed source is
pomace from pressing fruit, left to ferment for four to five weeks so that
germination inhibitors break down. Many seeds sprout; not all thrive. This
natural selection eliminates the weakest, leaving trees adapted to their
specific location from seedlinghood. After one to two years, only the best
trees are grafted.

Perhaps the most controversial element is Holzer's refusal to prune fruit
trees. Unpruned branches retain resilience, supporting themselves when weighed
down by fruit or snow. Trees stabilise themselves naturally, and when branches
are weighted by fruit, sunlight reaches the crown. Pruning causes trees to
put excess energy into water sprouts, creating a vicious cycle. Pruned
branches cannot withstand snow at altitudes up to 1,500 metres and the wounds
increase disease risk. Holzer also integrates fruit trees into forest systems,
creating multi-strata food-producing forests that combine timber with fruit and
nut crops. Multi-grafting several varieties onto a single tree minimises crop
failure risk, solves pollination problems, and creates unique collections
within one tree.

## Livestock Integration

Animals on the Krameterhof are not a separate enterprise but integral
co-workers in the farm ecosystem. Holzer keeps robust, hardy animals suited
to the terrain, favouring old domestic breeds and wild species. Old breeds
are significantly hardier, more intelligent, and more adaptable than modern
commercial varieties bred for rapid growth in confinement. Their produce is
of far higher quality. Animals are kept outdoors year-round in family groups
with species-appropriate diets and freedom of movement.

Pigs are Holzer's most valuable landscape workers. Their rooting behaviour
replaces mechanical ploughing on rough, stony terrain. They dig over and
loosen soil for planting, incorporate organic material, process windfall
fruit into manure, and disperse seeds across the landscape. Holzer keeps old
breeds such as Mangalitza, Swabian-Hall Swine, Duroc, and the rare Croatian
Turopolje, all of which withstand harsh alpine winters without heated housing.
The paddock system divides land into paddocks sown with cereals, legumes, and
root crops. Pigs move through sequentially, harvesting crops themselves. As
they move on, the disturbed soil is ready for sowing or planting, creating a
self-sustaining cycle of fertility that eliminates external inputs.

Beyond pigs, Holzer keeps Highland cattle for steep hillside pasture
management, free-range chickens that follow pigs in paddock rotation,
pheasants and guinea fowl for insect control, fallow deer for understory
management in forest gardens, and water buffalo in wetland areas. The core
principle is that animals are the most efficient mechanism for moving and
transforming nutrients across a landscape. When cattle graze on hillside
pastures and deposit manure in lower areas, they perform nutrient transport
that would otherwise require machinery, fossil fuel, and human labour. Poultry
following grazing animals scratch through dung, spreading it evenly and adding
concentrated nitrogen. The combined effect of multiple species creates
extraordinary nutrient cycling efficiency.

## Seed Autonomy and Biodiversity

Holzer's critique of corporate seed monopolisation is among the most forceful
aspects of his work. The statistics are staggering: 97% fewer fruit and
vegetable varieties today compared to 1900; India from 30,000 rice varieties
down to 12; the Philippines from several thousand to 2. Since the TRIPS
agreement of 1995, the World Trade Organisation has granted patent rights on
plant genetics to corporations. Hybrid varieties only produce for one
generation before degenerating, forcing farmers to buy new seeds annually. The
human cost includes 17,368 farmer suicides in India alone in 2009, with
families citing debt from high seed and fertiliser prices.

Against this backdrop, Holzer champions regionally adapted varieties and seed
saving as acts of resistance. His Siberian rye, given to him in 1957, grows
anywhere without artificial inputs, shows regional variation across continents,
and has continuously evolved through natural selection. His seed selection
method selects seeds from the strongest plants growing in the poorest soil,
because the plant giving the best crop under the most difficult circumstances
has the best genetic material. For biennial plants, the strongest specimen is
overwintered in a sand bed in a dark cellar, replanted in spring, and allowed
to set seed.

Holzer deliberately broke EU seed regulations by scattering his seeds
throughout his property. His position is unambiguous: "Laws must serve life. I
see it as my duty to stand up and protest if they do not." He recommends
never using F1 hybrid seeds, actively collecting seeds from traditional
plants, joining seed-saving groups, and sharing natural seeds with others.
Each person who saves and shares seeds becomes part of a decentralised network
of food sovereignty that corporate seed systems cannot easily control.

## Mushroom Cultivation and Aquaculture

Mushroom cultivation was one of Holzer's most important income sources in the
1980s. He grew button, oyster, shiitake, and king stropharia mushrooms on the
Krameterhof. His understanding of fungi goes beyond commerce: fungi are
essential decomposers, the visible mushroom being only the fruiting body of
a mycelium network that spreads through substrate absorbing nutrients. Many
fungi form mycorrhizae, symbiotic associations with plant roots that help
absorb water, nitrogen, and phosphates. Mycorrhizal networks connect different
plants, allowing nutrient exchange and communication.

For growing mushrooms on wood, Holzer prefers hardwood logs of 10 to 20
centimetres diameter. Logs are drilled, filled with spawn, sealed with wax or
clay, and placed in shaded, moist locations. After months of colonisation,
logs are "shocked" by soaking in cold water or banging to trigger fruiting.
Oyster mushrooms can be grown on urban balconies by inserting a trunk through
a drainage hole in a plant trough, serving as both climbing aid and mushroom
substrate. Holzer is sharply critical of the common advice to cut mushrooms
and leave the stem behind, arguing this damages the mycelium; the correct
method is to twist and pull the whole mushroom.

Holzer's aquaculture combines fish breeding, crayfish cultivation, aquatic
plant production, and wildlife habitat within pond complexes that mimic natural
water bodies. Multiple fish species -- tench, pike, wels catfish, zander,
koi, roach, and minnows -- reproduce naturally. Predatory species prevent
dominance by any single species. Fish are not fed commercial pellets; ponds
produce their own food through aquatic plants, algae, insects, and zooplankton.
Stocking rates are kept low (100 to 200 kg per hectare) to prevent disease
and maintain water quality. Ponds are designed with distinct zones: deep water
with oxygenating plants, shallow margins with reeds and iris, marsh zones with
meadowsweet and marsh marigold, and bank zones with willows and alder.

## Urban Applications and the Global Vision

Holzer has demonstrated that permaculture principles scale down to balconies,
terraces, and small urban spaces. His balcony garden design centres on
concrete troughs with a hardwood trunk inserted through a drainage hole,
serving as climbing aid for grapes, kiwi, and beans, and as mushroom
substrate. The trunk wicks moisture from a tray beneath, creating a
self-watering system. Earthworms process kitchen waste into fertiliser, and
plants at staggered heights maximise productive capacity. For contaminated
urban soil, the "bypass technique" builds raised beds above polluted surfaces
with imported clean soil. Holzer's ten-step plan for world hunger argues the
planet can feed three times the current population through cooperation with
nature. The first step, representing 70% of the work, is restoring
hydrological balance. Subsequent steps include abolishing industrial livestock
farming, enlarging cultivated areas through terracing and hugelkultur,
regionalising food production, implementing agrarian reform, fostering
community cooperation, conserving traditional food preservation knowledge,
and changing education so children learn to read nature. The practical
demonstration is immediate: given one hectare of unproductive land, two or
three pigs in a four-paddock rotation can transform it into a productive
garden within months. The method has been validated across Europe, Africa,
Asia, and the Americas.
