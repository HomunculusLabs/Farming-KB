## Sepp Holzer Permaculture and Rebel Farming

Sepp Holzer (born 1942) is an Austrian farmer who has spent over five decades
developing a comprehensive system of ecological agriculture on his family's
steep mountain property, the Krameterhof, in Lungau, Salzburg.

Situated at elevations between 1,100 and 1,500 metres in a region nicknamed
the "Austrian Siberia," the Krameterhof demonstrates that productive,
profitable farming is possible in marginal climates without chemical inputs,
irrigation, or specialised equipment.

Holzer's approach predates the formal permaculture design system codified by
Bill Mollison and David Holmgren in the 1970s. He arrived at his methods
independently through childhood observation and decades of experimentation.

## The Krameterhof — A Mountain Farm at 1,100 to 1,500 Metres

The Krameterhof spans roughly 45 hectares of steep alpine terrain. From the
upper boundary at 1,500 metres above sea level to the valley at 1,100 metres,
water is captured, stored, and used for multiple purposes across a network of
approximately 60 ponds, water gardens, wetlands, and ditches.

This water infrastructure supports fish farming, crayfish and mussel
cultivation, aquatic plant production, and duck and goose keeping, while
creating beneficial microclimates across the entire farm.

The farm produces fruit, vegetables, cereals, fish, pork, beef, poultry,
honey, mushrooms, and timber — all without synthetic fertilisers, pesticides,
or imported animal feed. This diversity of output is the foundation of its
economic viability.

## Philosophy — Reading Nature and Acting Intuitively

Holzer's farming philosophy rests on close observation of natural systems and
a willingness to act on intuition rather than following prescribed methods.
He describes this as "reading nature" — learning to recognise the signals that
soil, water, plants, and animals provide about the health of the land.

He argues that modern agriculture has become dangerously detached from this
kind of direct observation. Farmers follow spray schedules and fertiliser
programmes instead of responding to what the land actually needs.

Holzer advocates for decentralisation: as many farming families as possible,
each producing diverse food for their local community. Monoculture, he argues,
supports neither economy nor ecology. The more diverse the food production,
the larger and more diverse the yields and demand.

## Water Landscapes and Pond Construction

Water is the most important resource in Holzer's system. His approach to water
is fundamentally different from conventional drainage-based systems. Rather
than moving water off the land as quickly as possible, he seeks to keep it on
the land for as long as possible and make as much use of it as he can.

### The Vibration Sealing Method

Holzer is convinced that wildlife ponds should never use liners. Liners
prevent natural pond life from developing, are easily damaged, and make future
working impossible. Instead, he uses a vibration sealing method: water is
filled to 30-40cm depth and a small excavator bucket vibrates the subsoil
0.5-1 metres deep, similar to vibrating concrete. This works on almost every
subsoil type and is usually cheaper than liners.

### Design Elements

Stones rising from the water warm quickly in the sun, raising water
temperature and reducing ice formation. Gnarled tree trunks, stone steps, and
crossings add aesthetic and habitat value. Both deep and shallow areas provide
different habitats for plants and animals.

A sharp drop from 2 metres to 3 metres depth prevents aquatic plants from
overgrowing. A standpipe at the deepest point regulates water level, and an
emergency overflow pipe handles heavy rainfall.

### Stream Management

Holzer is strongly opposed to straightening streams. Natural streams meander,
creating diverse habitats and slowing water flow. Flooding at headwaters
prevents worse flooding downstream. Channelling water faster only moves the
problem downstream.

## Microclimate Creation — Extending the Growing Season

Creating microclimates is one of the most important techniques on the
Krameterhof. By breaking up the landscape with varied forms and features,
Holzer creates diverse growing conditions where plants that would otherwise
not survive can thrive.

Terraces and raised beds with rounded, winding lines create wind-sheltered
niches and suntraps. Stone walls and islands balance temperature and collect
wind-deposited fine material for humus build-up. Water bodies reflect sunlight
and release stored heat. Hedges and windbreaks are especially important in
exposed locations.

### Suntraps on North-Facing Slopes

On north-facing slopes and areas with few hours of sunshine, Holzer creates
niches in the hillside. He places large stones that store heat like a masonry
stove and release it slowly. A pond in front of the niche reflects the sun's
rays into the sheltered area, further increasing the warming effect.

Through these techniques, Holzer has grown kiwi fruit, lemons, grapes, shiitake
mushrooms, and even bitter oranges (Poncirus trifoliata) at altitudes where
conventional wisdom says they cannot survive.

### Altitude Shifting

The same variety behaves differently at different altitudes. The White
Transparent apple ripens mid-August at 1,100 metres but not until September at
1,500 metres, where it stores over a month and is excellent for pressing.

## Terrace Systems — Converting Steep Slopes to Productive Land

Terrace systems are central to Holzer's mountain farming. On steep slopes, he
creates level planting areas that prevent erosion while improving water
retention and creating microclimates.

### Humus Storage Ditches

These are long, wide ditches with low banks placed throughout the terraces.
They capture and store organic matter, water, and nutrients, feeding the
adjacent raised beds and plantings.

### Gradual Construction

Large-scale earthworks are done gradually over years. Year one establishes
terraces at the top, middle, and bottom, planted immediately. Year two fills
in between once the original terraces are stable. On steep slopes, work starts
at the bottom and proceeds upward.

### Deep Rooting for Stability

On slopes, Holzer ensures that soil and plants work together at depth — metres
rather than centimetres of root penetration provide real stability. Lupins,
sweet clover, and other deep-rooted species stabilise the slope and improve
water retention capacity.

## Raised Beds and Hugelkultur

Raised beds offer substantial advantages over ground-level beds, especially at
high altitude. Built loosely, they soak up rainwater like a sponge. The raised
portion warms more quickly in spring — critical in cold climates. Position
relative to sun and wind provides different microclimates for different plants
on the same bed.

### Zonation Within a Raised Bed

The top of the bed suits sun-loving, drought-tolerant plants. The sides
accommodate species preferring partial shade depending on aspect. The hollows
between beds suit moisture-loving plants, with aquatic species in deeper
hollows where water collects.

### Management

Mulch throughout the year to protect soil and supply nutrients. Use
polycultures to maintain soil health and prevent pest outbreaks. Green manure
crops left standing over winter are pushed down by the first heavy snow and
decompose slowly, improving soil structure without compaction.

## Crater Gardens — Bowl-Shaped Gardens for Humid Microclimates

The crater garden is a creative landscape design concept that transforms flat
or gently sloping land into a sunken, bowl-shaped planting area. In summer, a
beneficial humid microclimate develops inside the crater, enabling cultivation
of plants that would not normally thrive in the local conditions.

The raised edges around the crater function as hugelkultur beds, providing
fertility and additional microclimates. A small pond or water garden in the
centre combines the benefits of water-based microclimate with the
heat-trapping geometry of the sunken form.

The beds around the crater vary in height, length, width, and shape according
to function, location, soil conditions, and the preferences of the people
managing them. This creative freedom is a hallmark of Holzer's approach.

## Fruit Forest Establishment — Sowing from Seed

Holzer's method for establishing fruit forests is radically different from
conventional orchard planting. Instead of buying nursery-grown trees, he sows
seeds directly from fruit pomace and allows natural selection to identify the
strongest individuals.

### The Process

Pigs prepare the ground by rooting and digging. Holzer then distributes
fermented fruit pomace (seeds and pulp) over the prepared area. The
fermentation breaks down the layers that naturally delay germination. Seeds
germinate and grow naturally, with natural selection eliminating the weakest.

After one to two years, the best trees are selected for grafting with desired
varieties. Ungrafted trees continue growing as genetic resources and wildlife
forage. The method costs no more than planting any other tree.

### Advantages Over Nursery Trees

Trees grown from seed in situ adapt to local soil and climate from seedling
stage, avoiding transplant shock. Genetic diversity provides insurance against
pests and diseases. Even if a grafted variety fails, the rootstock continues
and can be re-grafted.

## Livestock Integration — Animals as Co-Workers

Livestock play a central role in Holzer's system. They provide high-quality
produce and serve as industrious workers. Holzer breeds robust, hardy animals
suited to the terrain — old domestic breeds as well as wild animals.

### Pigs as Living Ploughs

Pigs are indispensable on rough, stony terrain that cannot be ploughed
mechanically. When they search for food they plough and dig through the soil,
improving it. Holzer keeps pigs using a paddock system — moving paddocks
ensures the land is never overgrazed, and the varied diet prevents livestock
diseases almost entirely.

He uses old hardy breeds including Mangalitza, Swabian-Hall Swine, Duroc, and
Turopolje. Pigs also regulate problem plants when grazed in targeted areas.

### Wild Cattle and Old Breeds

Holzer has bred mouflon, chamois, ibex, red deer, roe deer, fallow deer, and
wild cattle including yaks, water buffalo, and bison. Scottish Highland
cattle and Dahomey miniature cattle are favoured for their hardiness and
self-sufficiency. All live outside year-round.

### Cattle and Poultry Housing

Roundwood shelters built into slopes provide housing for cattle and pigs.
Temperature stays stable at 8-10C year-round using the soil's thermal mass.
Construction costs 700-800 euros for a 3x6x2 metre shelter, built in one day
with an excavator and one additional person.

## Aquaculture and Fish Farming

The aquaculture system is not isolated from the rest of the farm. Pond edges
are planted with fruit trees and bushes that benefit from the moderated
microclimate. Livestock access some ponds for drinking. Ducks and geese add
manure and control aquatic plants.

### Ecological Functions

Ponds serve far more functions than fish production alone. They cool the
surrounding area through evaporation. They provide habitat for amphibians,
waterfowl, insects, and aquatic invertebrates. Aquatic plants absorb nutrients
from water, and when harvested, return these nutrients to the terrestrial
ecosystem. Ponds also provide fire protection and recreation.

### Species and Management

Trout thrive in the cooler, oxygen-rich water that exits the Pelton wheel
generators. Warmer ponds support different fish species. Water gardens
designed for aquatic plant cultivation are shallower and may have areas where
the surface freezes in winter. Varied depth creates the right range of
conditions for diverse aquatic life.

## Alternative Energy Systems

The Krameterhof operates entirely without fossil fuel inputs. The 100-metre
elevation difference provides the energy foundation.

### Pelton Wheel Generators

Two small generators built from old Pelton wheels are fed from ponds through
a penstock pipe. The 100-metre head generates enough electricity for the
entire farm. Water exits the generators cool and oxygen-rich, flowing into
lower fish ponds and allowing trout in ponds that would otherwise be too warm.

### Hydraulic Ram Pump

A hydraulic ram pump operates without any external energy source, powered by
water flowing from the ponds. It pumps drinking water to the mountain pasture
at up to 15 bars of pressure, with a flow rate ratio of 10 litres of drive
water to 1 litre of drinking water.

### Water Wheels

Two traditional water wheels drive mills for grain processing, built from
locally available timber with minimal maintenance requirements.

## Seed Autonomy and Ancient Cereals

Holzer is a fierce advocate for seed sovereignty. He points out that 97 per
cent of fruit and vegetable varieties have been lost in the last century. The
Philippines went from several thousand rice varieties to just two. China went
from 10,000 wheat varieties to 1,000.

### Brandroggen — Fire Rye

Brandroggen literally means "fire rye," sown after plant matter has been burnt
on meadow land. This traditional Austrian mountain grain grows anywhere
without artificial fertilisers or pesticides. Through wind pollination, new
genetic material is added in each region, increasing resilience. Holzer has
sown it worldwide and it thrives everywhere, continuously evolving through
natural selection.

### Ancient Cereals

Holzer grows einkorn, emmer, and traditional rye varieties at altitude.
Einkorn dates back over 10,000 years and is naturally disease-resistant with
lower gluten content. Emmer is a precursor to durum wheat. These traditional
varieties require minimal inputs and are well-suited to marginal conditions.

## Polycultures and Catch Crop Design

Holzer's polycultures integrate cereals, legumes, root crops, and sunflowers
in complex mixtures. The principle is that every plant serves multiple
functions and every combination produces beneficial interactions.

### Stubble Drilling and Succession

After cereal harvest, stubble drilling establishes catch crops. Peas and maize
grown together provide a balanced feed combination — maize is energy-rich but
low in protein, while peas supply the missing protein. They can be harvested
and threshed together.

### Slope Stabilisation Mixtures

On steep slopes, seed mixtures include lupins, sweet clover, lucerne, vetch,
peas, sunflowers, Jerusalem artichokes, and turnips. Deep roots stabilise the
slope while above-ground biomass provides diverse yields.

### Leaving Green Manure Standing

In autumn, Holzer leaves green manure crops standing. The first heavy snow
pushes them down and decomposition begins naturally. Plants ripen, bloom, and
produce seeds. Birds eat seeds, stratify them in their stomachs, and
distribute them — natural seed dispersal eliminating the need for resowing.

## Browsing Protection Without Fences

Holzer's fundamental approach to browsing animals is to sow and plant in large
enough quantities that deer, birds, hares, and mice all have something to
eat. Nature is fertile enough to provide for everyone.

### Natural Distraction

Jerusalem artichokes serve as distraction plantings. Extra willow trees,
especially in front of slopes, draw deer because bark is softer and more
elastic. Side shoots and branches between graft and ground provide food at
different heights for hares, roe deer, and red deer.

### Homemade Browsing Salve

For areas with high browsing danger, Holzer makes a salve from bone fat,
linseed oil, slaked lime, fine quartz sand, and fresh cow dung. The bone fat
odour penetrates bark and lasts for years. When odour fades, the quartz sand
remains as a physical deterrent. Wild roses, barberries, and blackthorn
planted around trees provide living barriers.

## Balcony and Urban Applications

Holzer has planted balconies and terraces in many towns, proving that
permaculture principles work at any scale. A 2m x 3m terrace can produce
substantial food using his methods.

### Container Setup

Two concrete troughs with a combined capacity of 1.5 cubic metres of soil are
mounted on bricks with waterproof trays beneath. A hardwood trunk inserted
through drainage holes serves as both a climbing aid for grapes, kiwi, beans,
and peas, and a substrate for culinary mushroom cultivation.

### Self-Watering System

The hardwood trunk wicks water up from the tray below, balancing soil
moisture automatically. Earthworms introduced into the troughs aerate and
fertilise the soil. The "bypass technique" extends the system between
balconies on different floors, with climbing plants rooted in troughs on each
level.

## Animal Ethics and Humane Relationship with Nature

Holzer's philosophy extends beyond technique to encompass an ethic of
relationship with animals. A formative childhood experience raising a lamb
shaped his lifelong commitment to humane treatment.

### Humane Slaughter

He argues that dying is not painful, only the fear of death is. He advocates
on-farm slaughter by a familiar person, eliminating transportation stress and
the terror of the slaughterhouse. Stress before slaughter produces cortisol
that affects meat quality, so humane slaughter produces healthier food as well
as being ethically superior.

### Political Critique

Holzer is fiercely critical of agricultural policy: set-aside payments,
individual animal premiums, monoculture support, seed control, GMO promotion,
and water rights misappropriation. He describes agricultural policy as "one
big cycle of wrongdoing, one bad decision informing the next."

## Beekeeping and Natural Honey Production

Bees are integrated into every zone of Holzer's farm. They pollinate fruit
trees, vegetables, herbs, and wild plants. Comb honey, where honey remains in
the wax comb, is considered the most medicinal form.

The health of bee colonies serves as a barometer for the health of the entire
farm ecosystem. When bees thrive, the landscape is diverse, chemical-free, and
ecologically balanced. Holzer practises natural beekeeping, avoiding chemical
treatments and allowing bees to express their natural behaviours.

## Earth Cellars and Roundwood Construction

Earth provides the cheapest and best insulation. Earth cellars maintain 80-90
per cent humidity and 8-10C temperature using only the soil's thermal mass.
A 10-metre underground air inlet pipe pre-conditions incoming air to soil
temperature. The air outlet at the highest point prevents condensation.

These cellars store fruit and vegetables without refrigeration. During winter,
when animals are moved between paddocks, some shelters double as storage rooms
where crops are grown — exactly where the produce is needed.

## Global Projects and Influence

Holzer has consulted on projects across Europe, Africa, Asia, and the
Americas. In hot, dry climates like South Africa, Brazil, and Colombia, he
begins with fast-growing pioneer trees to create protective forest, then
builds water retention landscapes beneath the canopy.

In Scotland, where rainfall is abundant, the focus shifts to drainage and
making use of excess water. In each location, the same principles apply:
observe the land, work with existing conditions, create diverse microclimates,
and let nature do the work.

His books, including "Sepp Holzer's Permaculture" and "Desert or Paradise,"
have inspired a generation of farmers and homesteaders to adopt ecological
methods on marginal land.
