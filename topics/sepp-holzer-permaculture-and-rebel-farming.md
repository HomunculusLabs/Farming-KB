## Sepp Holzer Permaculture and Rebel Farming

[[sepp-holzer]] (born 1942) is an Austrian farmer who has spent over five decades
developing a comprehensive system of ecological agriculture on his family"s
steep mountain property, the Krameterhof, in Lungau, Salzburg.

Situated at elevations between 1,100 and 1,500 metres in a region nicknamed
the "Austrian Siberia," the Krameterhof demonstrates that productive,
profitable farming is possible in marginal climates without chemical inputs,
irrigation, or specialised equipment.

Holzer"s approach predates the formal [[permaculture-design]] system codified by
[[bill-mollison]] and [[david-holmgren]] in the 1970s. He arrived at his methods
independently through childhood observation and decades of experimentation.

## The Krameterhof — A Mountain Farm at 1,100 to 1,500 Metres

The Krameterhof spans roughly 45 hectares of steep alpine terrain. From the
upper boundary at 1,500 metres above sea level to the valley at 1,100 metres,
water is captured, stored, and used for multiple purposes across a network of
approximately 60 ponds, water gardens, wetlands, and ditches.

This water infrastructure supports fish farming, crayfish and mussel
cultivation, aquatic plant production, and duck and [[goose-keeping]], while
creating beneficial microclimates across the entire farm.

The farm produces fruit, vegetables, cereals, fish, pork, beef, poultry,
honey, mushrooms, and timber — all without synthetic fertilisers, pesticides,
or imported animal feed. This diversity of output is the foundation of its
economic viability.

## Philosophy — Reading Nature and Acting Intuitively

Holzer's farming philosophy rests on close observation of natural systems and
a willingness to act on intuition rather than following prescribed methods.
He describes this as "reading nature" — learning to recognise the signals that
soil, water, plants, and animals provide about the health of the land.

He argues that modern agriculture has become dangerously detached from this
kind of direct observation. Farmers follow spray schedules and fertiliser
programmes instead of responding to what the land actually needs.

Holzer advocates for decentralisation: as many farming families as possible,
each producing diverse food for their local community. Monoculture, he argues,
supports neither economy nor ecology. The more diverse the food production,
the larger and more diverse the yields and demand.

## Water Landscapes and Pond Construction

Water is the most important resource in Holzer's system. His approach to water
is fundamentally different from conventional drainage-based systems. Rather
than moving water off the land as quickly as possible, he seeks to keep it on
the land for as long as possible and make as much use of it as he can.

### The Vibration Sealing Method

Holzer is convinced that wildlife ponds should never use liners. Liners
prevent natural pond life from developing, are easily damaged, and make future
working impossible. Instead, he uses a vibration sealing method: water is
filled to 30-40cm depth and a small excavator bucket vibrates the subsoil
0.5-1 metres deep, similar to vibrating concrete. This works on almost every
subsoil type and is usually cheaper than liners.

### Design Elements

Stones rising from the water warm quickly in the sun, raising water
temperature and reducing ice formation. Gnarled tree trunks, stone steps, and
crossings add aesthetic and habitat value. Both deep and shallow areas provide
different habitats for plants and animals.

A sharp drop from 2 metres to 3 metres depth prevents aquatic plants from
overgrowing. A standpipe at the deepest point regulates water level, and an
emergency overflow pipe handles heavy rainfall.

### Stream Management

Holzer is strongly opposed to straightening streams. Natural streams meander,
creating diverse habitats and slowing water flow. Flooding at headwaters
prevents worse flooding downstream. Channelling water faster only moves the
problem downstream.

## Microclimate Creation — Extending the Growing Season

Creating microclimates is one of the most important techniques on the
Krameterhof. By breaking up the landscape with varied forms and features,
Holzer creates diverse growing conditions where plants that would otherwise
not survive can thrive.

Terraces and raised beds with rounded, winding lines create wind-sheltered
niches and suntraps. Stone walls and islands balance temperature and collect
wind-deposited fine material for humus build-up. Water bodies reflect sunlight
and release stored heat. Hedges and windbreaks are especially important in
exposed locations.

### Suntraps on North-Facing Slopes

On north-facing slopes and areas with few hours of sunshine, Holzer creates
niches in the hillside. He places large stones that store heat like a masonry
stove and release it slowly. A pond in front of the niche reflects the sun's
rays into the sheltered area, further increasing the warming effect.

Through these techniques, Holzer has grown kiwi fruit, lemons, grapes, shiitake
mushrooms, and even bitter oranges (Poncirus trifoliata) at altitudes where
conventional wisdom says they cannot survive.

### Altitude Shifting

The same variety behaves differently at different altitudes. The White
Transparent apple ripens mid-August at 1,100 metres but not until September at
1,500 metres, where it stores over a month and is excellent for pressing.

## Terrace Systems — Converting Steep Slopes to Productive Land

Terrace systems are central to Holzer's mountain farming. On steep slopes, he
creates level planting areas that prevent erosion while improving water
retention and creating microclimates.

### Humus Storage Ditches

These are long, wide ditches with low banks placed throughout the terraces.
They capture and store organic matter, water, and nutrients, feeding the
adjacent raised beds and plantings.

### Gradual Construction

Large-scale earthworks are done gradually over years. Year one establishes
terraces at the top, middle, and bottom, planted immediately. Year two fills
in between once the original terraces are stable. On steep slopes, work starts
at the bottom and proceeds upward.

### Deep Rooting for Stability

On slopes, Holzer ensures that soil and plants work together at depth — metres
rather than centimetres of root penetration provide real stability. Lupins,
sweet clover, and other deep-rooted species stabilise the slope and improve
water retention capacity.

## Raised Beds and Hugelkultur

Raised beds offer substantial advantages over ground-level beds, especially at
high altitude. Built loosely, they soak up rainwater like a sponge. The raised
portion warms more quickly in spring — critical in cold climates. Position
relative to sun and wind provides different microclimates for different plants
on the same bed.
