# Living Fences and Hedgerows

Living fences and hedgerows are linear plantings of trees, shrubs, and sometimes herbaceous perennials that serve as functional boundaries, barriers, windbreaks, and wildlife corridors. They represent one of the oldest forms of land management, with hedgerow traditions spanning thousands of years across Europe, Asia, Africa, and the Americas. In permaculture and regenerative agriculture, living fences are valued as multi-functional elements that produce food, fodder, fuel, and medicine while providing ecological services.

## Definitions and Distinctions

**Living fences** are functional barriers primarily composed of closely planted, often interwoven or pleached, living woody plants. Their primary purpose is containment — keeping livestock in and/or wildlife out. They are actively managed through pruning, weaving, and shaping to maintain density and height.

**Hedgerows** are broader linear plantings that may or may not function as fences. They often include multiple layers of vegetation — trees, shrubs, and ground flora — and prioritize ecological functions such as wildlife habitat, wind protection, [[comparison-biological-nitrogen-fixation-vs-synthetic-fertilizer]].

### Asian and African Traditions

In Southeast Asia, living fences of **Leucaena leucocephala**, **Gliricidia**, and bamboo have been used for centuries. African traditions include **Euphorbia** hedges in East Africa, sisal and cactus barriers in arid regions, and mixed-species hedges across West African farming systems that combine food production with boundary marking.

## Functions and Benefits

### Livestock Containment and Exclusion

The most fundamental function of a living fence is to serve as a physical barrier. A well-established living fence can replace conventional wire fencing entirely for cattle, horses, sheep, and goats (though goats may require additional protection or unpalatable species). For small livestock like poultry, living fences provide an effective predator barrier when dense and thorny.

### Windbreak and Microclimate Modification

Living fences and hedgerows reduce wind speed on their leeward side for a distance of 10–15 times their height. This creates favorable microclimates for crops and livestock by:

- Reducing evapotranspiration and soil moisture loss
- Protecting against wind damage to crops and fruit trees
- Reducing heating costs for nearby structures
- Creating sheltered conditions for [[query-how-do-i-design-habitat-for-beneficial-insects]] insects and pollinators
- Denning sites for hedgehogs, foxes, and other wildlife
- Foraging habitat with flowers, fruits, seeds, and insect prey

Studies in the UK have found that hedgerows support over 2,000 species of vascular plants, fungi, invertebrates, birds, and mammals. Hedgerow width and connectivity are the strongest predictors of biodiversity value.

### Erosion Control and Water Management

On slopes and contours, living fences and hedgerows reduce erosion by:

- Slowing surface water runoff
- Intercepting rainfall and reducing splash erosion
- Binding soil with extensive root networks
- Trapping sediment behind the plant line
- Promoting water infiltration into the soil profile

Contour-planted hedgerows are a cornerstone of [[ethnomycology-cultural-significance-sacred-fungi]] in many regions — British hedgerows are protected heritage features, and French bocage landscapes are UNESCO-recognized cultural landscapes.

## Design Considerations

### Site Assessment

Before planting a living fence or hedgerow, assess:

- **Purpose**: Primary function (barrier, windbreak, privacy, production, wildlife)
- **Climate**: Hardiness zone, rainfall, frost dates, prevailing wind direction
- **Soil**: Soil type, drainage, pH, depth, and fertility
- **Space**: Available width (living fences need 0.5–2 m; hedgerows 3–10 m)
- **Existing vegetation**: What can be incorporated into the design
- **Livestock**: Species to be contained or excluded
- **Regulations**: Local ordinances, property lines, setback requirements

### Species Selection

Species selection depends on function, climate, and management capacity. Key considerations:

**Barrier species** (dense, thorny, or thicket-forming):
- Hawthorn (*Crataegus monogyna*), blackthorn (*Prunus spinosa*), honey locust (*Gleditsia triacanthos*), osage orange (*Maclura pomifera*), multiflora rose (*Rosa multiflora* — invasive in some areas), bougainvillea (tropical), agave and cactus (arid)

**Canopy/standard trees** (in wider hedgerows):
- Oak (*Quercus* spp.), ash (*Fraxinus excelsior*), field maple (*Acer campestre*), elm (*Ulmus* spp.), hawthorn, crabapple, pear, plum

**Shrub layer** (mid-height, fruiting, and flowering):
- Hazel (*Corylus avellana*), dogwood (*Cornus sanguinea*), elder (*Sambucus nigra*), blackberry (*Rubus fruticosus*), gooseberry (*Ribes uva-crispa*), currants (*Ribes* spp.), viburnum, serviceberry (*Amelanchier*)
