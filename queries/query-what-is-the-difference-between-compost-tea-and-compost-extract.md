# What Is the Difference Between Compost Tea and Compost Extract?

## Short Answer
Compost tea is a brewed, aerated liquid that extracts and multiplies
microorganisms from compost, resulting in a biologically active inoculant
teeming with living bacteria, fungi, protozoa, and nematodes. Compost extract
is simply compost steeped in water without aeration or microbial food sources,
producing a liquid that contains soluble nutrients and some microorganisms but
without the explosive population growth that brewing provides.

## Detailed Explanation
The distinction between compost tea and compost extract is one of the most
misunderstood topics in organic growing. Both start with finished compost and
water, but the processes and results are fundamentally different. Understanding
this difference is essential for anyone working with the [[soil-food-web]]
because each preparation serves a different purpose in soil and plant
management.

Compost extract is made by steeping compost in water for a short period,
typically 15 minutes to a few hours. The water extracts soluble nutrients,
humic acids, and a portion of the microorganisms present in the original
compost. The extract contains whatever biology was in the compost but does not
significantly increase microbial populations. Think of it as a dilute liquid
compost. The [[compost-building-guide]] directly
determines the quality of the extract, since you are simply transferring
existing organisms and nutrients into liquid form.

Actively aerated compost tea (AACT), commonly just called compost tea, goes
much further. During the brewing process, which typically runs 24 to 36 hours,
the compost is suspended in aerated water along with microbial food sources like
molasses, kelp meal, and fish hydrolysate. The aeration provides oxygen that
supports explosive microbial reproduction, while the food sources feed bacteria
and fungi. By the end of the brew, microbial populations have multiplied by
orders of magnitude compared to the starting compost. The
[[cervantes-compost-tea-organic-brewing]] transforms a handful of compost into billions
of active microorganisms.

The brewing equipment matters significantly. A proper compost tea brewer
includes an air pump with diffusers that maintain dissolved oxygen levels above
6 parts per million throughout the brew. If oxygen levels drop, anaerobic
bacteria can proliferate and produce compounds that are harmful to plants. The
[[compost-tea-brewing-equipment-ingham]] design directly
affects whether your tea is beneficial or potentially pathogenic, making this a
critical investment for serious organic growers.

Bacterial versus fungal dominance is something you can control in compost tea
but not in extract. By adjusting the food sources in your brew, you can produce
a [[query-what-is-the-difference-between-fungal-dominant-and-bacterial-dominant-compost-tea]] using simple
sugars like molasses, or a fungal-dominant tea using more complex foods like
oatmeal, fish hydrolysate, and humic acids. Bacterial teas are ideal for annual
vegetables and early-stage cannabis growth, while fungal teas better support
perennials, trees, and mature cannabis in living soil systems.

Application methods also differ between the two preparations. Compost extract
can be applied as a soil drench or foliar spray without concern for clogging
equipment, since the particles are small and suspended. Compost tea, with its
dense microbial populations and added food sources, is best applied immediately
after brewing as a soil drench. If applying as a foliar spray, the tea should
be filtered and applied with adequate leaf coverage. The
[[compost-tea-application-rates]] vary depending on whether
you are inoculating soil, treating a deficiency, or providing preventive
disease suppression.

Compost tea provides disease suppression that extract generally cannot match.
The high concentrations of beneficial microorganisms in properly brewed tea
compete with pathogens for space and resources on leaf surfaces and in the root
zone. Certain bacteria and fungi in compost tea produce natural antibiotics and
enzymes that directly inhibit pathogenic organisms. This is why compost tea is
widely used in [[query-what-is-integrated-pest-management-for-organic-mushroom-cultivation]] as a preventive
measure against foliar diseases like powdery mildew and botrytis.

One common misconception is that compost tea is a fertilizer. While tea does
contain some soluble nutrients extracted from the compost and food sources, its
primary value is biological. Compost tea inoculates the soil with beneficial
microorganisms that improve nutrient cycling, disease resistance, and soil
structure over time. For immediate nutrient needs, you would use a separate
fertilizer. The [[soil-food-web-nutrient-cycling]] that tea
supports is a long-term investment in soil health rather than a quick fix.

Quality control is essential for compost tea. The finished product should smell
earthy and sweet, similar to fresh forest soil. A sour, rotten, or
sewage-like odor indicates anaerobic conditions and a brew that should be
discarded. Regular microscope examination of your tea reveals the diversity and
density of microorganisms, allowing you to verify that your brew contains the
bacteria, fungi, protozoa, and nematodes that make it effective as a soil
inoculant.

## Key Points
- Compost extract simply transfers existing compost biology into liquid;
  compost tea multiplies it exponentially
- Aeration and microbial food sources during brewing are what distinguish
  [[cervantes-compost-tea-organic-brewing]] from extract
- You can control bacterial vs. fungal dominance in compost tea through food
  source selection
- Compost tea provides significant [[query-what-is-integrated-pest-management-for-organic-mushroom-cultivation]] that extract cannot match
- Proper brewing equipment maintaining adequate oxygen levels is essential for
  safe, effective compost tea
