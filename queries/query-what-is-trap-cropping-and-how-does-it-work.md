# What Is Trap Cropping and How Does It Work?
Trap cropping is a targeted pest management strategy in which specific
plants are deliberately grown to lure insect pests away from the main
cash or food crops. These sacrificial decoy plants concentrate pest
populations in a defined area where they can be monitored, managed, or
destroyed before reaching the crops you actually want to harvest.
Rather than fighting pests across the entire growing area, trap cropping
lets you control them in one concentrated zone. This makes pest
management more efficient, more affordable, and more environmentally
responsible. The technique pairs naturally with broader
[[hamilton-geoffhamilton-nick-organic-gardening-companion-planting]] principles.
## How Trap Cropping Works
At its core, trap cropping exploits the feeding and reproductive
preferences of insect pests. Many pest species show strong host-plant
specificity, meaning they actively seek out particular plant species for
egg-laying and feeding. By planting these preferred species near or
among your main crops, you intercept pests before they colonize the
target plants.
The most sophisticated form of this approach is the **push-pull
strategy**, developed originally for cereal stem borer management in
sub-Saharan Africa. In this system two mechanisms operate simultaneously:
- **Push plants** are intercropped directly with the main crop. These
  plants release volatile chemicals or possess physical traits that
  repel pests, driving them away from the protected crop.
- **Pull plants** (the trap crops) are planted on the perimeter or in
  designated zones around the field. These emit attractant cues that
  draw the repelled pests inward, capturing them in a manageable area.
The dual directional force moves pests away from the valuable crop and
concentrates them on sacrificial plants. Research shows the push-pull
approach can reduce pest damage by 80 percent or more.
## Common Trap Crop Examples
Choosing the right trap crop depends on the specific pests present in
your garden or field. Well-documented pairings include:
- **Nasturtiums for aphids**: Nasturtiums (Tropaeolum spp.) are highly
  attractive to aphids, including black bean aphids, green peach aphids,
  and cabbage aphids. They draw these pests away from tomatoes, peppers,
  brassicas, and beans while providing edible flowers and leaves.
- **Marigolds for nematodes**: French marigolds (Tagetes patula) release
  alpha-terthienyl and other bioactive compounds from their roots that
  repel or trap root-knot nematodes (Meloidogyne spp.) in the soil,
  protecting susceptible crops like tomatoes and peppers.
- **Blue Hubbard squash for squash bugs**: This specific squash variety
  is a proven trap crop for squash bugs (Anasa tristis) and squash vine
  borers. University extension research has demonstrated that blue
  Hubbard is significantly more attractive to these pests than zucchini
  or yellow squash.
- **Radishes for flea beetles**: Radishes attract flea beetles
  preferentially, protecting eggplants, cabbage, and other brassicas.
- **Chervil for slugs**: Chervil draws slugs away from tender lettuce
  seedlings and other vulnerable plants.
- **Buckwheat for tarnished plant bugs**: Buckwheat attracts these
  pests away from strawberries while supporting beneficial insects.
For a deeper look at plants that attract and sustain beneficial species,
see [[insectary-plants-guide]] and [[insectary-plants-for-pest-control]].
## Placement Strategies
Where you place trap crops matters as much as which species you select.
The two primary strategies each suit different pest behaviors:
### Perimeter Planting
Trap crops are sown as a solid border around the entire field or garden
bed. This is the simplest and most common approach. Pests encounter the
trap crop first as they migrate in from field edges or adjacent
vegetation, reducing pressure on the interior main crop. Perimeter
planting works particularly well for pests that disperse by walking
short distances from overwintering sites, including squash bugs, flea
beetles, and Colorado potato beetles.
### Intercropping
Trap crops are distributed within the main crop planting, either in
alternating rows, in clusters, or scattered at regular intervals. This
approach is more effective for pests already present in the soil or
those that move short distances between plants, such as aphids and
spider mites. Intercropping provides uniform protection but requires
careful planning to avoid competition with the main crop.
### Combined Approaches
Many experienced growers use both methods: a perimeter border backed by intercropped trap plants within the main planting, providing both edge and interior protection.
## Combining with Biological Controls
Trap crops become significantly more effective when paired with
biological control agents. Because the trap crop concentrates pests in
a small, defined area, it creates an ideal target for:
- **Predatory insects**: Ladybugs, lacewings, minute pirate bugs, and
  parasitic wasps can be released onto trap crops where prey density
  is highest. See [[beneficial-insects-guide]] for detailed profiles
  and release protocols.
- **Microbial controls**: Entomopathogenic fungi such as
  [[beauveria-bassiana]] can be applied as a foliar spray directly on
  infested trap crops, maximizing contact with concentrated pest
  populations and reducing overall application volume.
- **Entomopathogenic nematodes**: Soil-dwelling species like Steinernema
  and Heterorhabditis can be applied around trap crops targeted at
  root-feeding pests.
The trap crop functions as a pest collection zone, making biological
treatments far more efficient and reducing the need for broad-spectrum
chemical insecticides.
## When to Destroy Trap Crops
Timing is the most critical factor in successful trap cropping. If trap
crops are left unchecked, pest populations can build to levels that
spill over onto the main crop. Follow these guidelines:
1. **Monitor regularly**: Inspect trap crops at least twice weekly once
   pests begin appearing.
2. **Act before overflow**: Destroy or treat the trap crop when pest
   density reaches a threshold that risks migration to adjacent plants,
   indicated by significant defoliation or large insect aggregations.
3. **Use appropriate removal methods**: Cut severely infested plants,
   bag them, and remove from the site for burning or deep composting.
   Avoid knocking pests off, as many will relocate to the main crop.
4. **Replant as needed**: In long-season gardens, successive plantings
   of trap crops maintain continuous protection through the season.
## Integration with IPM Programs
Trap cropping fits naturally into [[integrated-pest-management]]
frameworks as both a cultural and mechanical control method. Within an
IPM program, trap crops serve several important functions:
- **Early detection**: Trap crops act as biological sentinels, revealing
  pest species and population levels before the main crop is affected.
- **Reduced pesticide reliance**: By concentrating pests, trap crops
  allow targeted spot treatments rather than broadcast applications.
- **Economic threshold data**: Monitoring trap crops provides data for
  informed spray-or-wait decisions based on established thresholds.
- **Ecosystem preservation**: Trap crops maintain habitat complexity and
  floral diversity, supporting pollinators and beneficial predatory
  insects throughout the season.

## Key Takeaways
- Trap cropping uses sacrificial plants to divert pests away from main
  crops, concentrating them in manageable zones.
- The push-pull strategy combines repellent intercrops with attractive
  perimeter trap plants for maximum directional pest control.
- Proven pairings include nasturtiums for aphids, marigolds for
  nematodes, blue Hubbard squash for squash bugs, and radishes for flea
  beetles.
- **Perimeter planting suits edge-migrating pests, while intercropping
  provides uniform interior protection.
- Combining trap crops with biological controls like predatory insects
  and entomopathogenic fungi dramatically amplifies effectiveness.
- Timely destruction of infested trap crops prevents pest spillover onto
  the main crop.
- Integration with IPM programs reduces pesticide dependence while
  supporting overall ecosystem health and biodiversity.
