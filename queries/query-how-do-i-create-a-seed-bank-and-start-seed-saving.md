### 2. Allow Seeds to Fully Mature

Seeds must be fully ripe before harvest. For dry-seeded crops like beans and grains, this means the pod is brown and dry. For wet-seeded crops like tomatoes and cucumbers, the fruit should be fully ripe or even overripe.

### 3. Extract and Clean Seeds

**Dry processing** works for beans, peas, lettuce, grains, and most herbs. Shell or thresh seeds from pods, then winnow by pouring seed between two containers in a light breeze to separate chaff from seed.

**Wet processing** is needed for tomatoes, cucumbers, and squash. Scoop seeds and gel into a jar with water. Let ferment for 2 to 4 days, stirring daily. Good seeds sink; bad seeds and gel float. Pour off the floaters, rinse the sunk seeds in a fine mesh strainer, and spread them on a nonstick surface to dry.

### 4. Dry Seeds Thoroughly

Seeds must be completely dry before storage or they will mold or rot. Spread seeds in a single layer on a screen, paper towel, or glass plate. Dry at room temperature out of direct sunlight for 1 to 2 weeks. Seeds are dry enough when they snap rather than bend when folded.

### 5. Store Properly

The three enemies of seed viability are **heat, moisture, and light**. Store seeds in airtight glass jars or mylar bags with a desiccant packet. Keep them in a cool, dark, dry place. For long-term storage of 5 or more years, keep seeds in the refrigerator or freezer in sealed containers with moisture absorbers.

## How Many Seeds to Save

A common rule is to save seed from a minimum population of 20 to 50 plants for outcrossing species and 5 to 10 plants for self-pollinating species to maintain genetic diversity. For a personal seed bank, save enough seed for two full planting seasons plus a buffer for crop failure. This usually means 50 to 200 seeds per variety depending on the crop.

## Testing Germination

Before planting saved seeds, especially those stored for multiple years, test germination rates. Place 10 seeds on a damp paper towel inside a plastic bag and keep at room temperature. Check after 7 to 14 days depending on the species. If 7 out of 10 germinate, you have a 70 percent germination rate and should plant accordingly, sowing extra seed to compensate.

## Organizing Your Seed Bank

Create a simple tracking system for your seed bank. Record the variety name, source, year saved, germination rate, number of seeds, and any notes about plant performance. A spreadsheet or notebook works well. Organize seeds by planting season and crop family so you can quickly find what you need when it is time to sow.

## Intermediate and Advanced Techniques

Once you are comfortable with the basics, explore these next-level skills:

- **Seed isolation cages**: Build mesh or screen cages over cross-pollinating crops to prevent unwanted crossing without requiring large distance buffers.
- **Hand pollination**: Emasculate flowers and transfer pollen by hand for controlled crosses. This is essential for breeding new varieties.
- **Biennial seed saving**: Crops like carrots, beets, onions, and kale are biennials that flower and set seed in their second year. You must overwinter the roots and replant them the following spring.
- **Seed viability extending**: Store seeds in moisture-proof containers with oxygen absorbers at below-freezing temperatures for maximum longevity. Some seeds, like onions and parsnips, rarely last more than 1 to 2 years even under ideal conditions.
