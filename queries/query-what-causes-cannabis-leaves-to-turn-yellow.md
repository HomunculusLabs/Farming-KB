# What Causes Cannabis Leaves to Turn Yellow?

## Short Answer
Yellowing cannabis leaves (chlorosis) is most commonly caused by nutrient deficiencies,
overwatering, or pH imbalances that prevent nutrient uptake. Nitrogen deficiency is the
single most frequent cause, especially during the flowering stage when the plant mobilizes
nitrogen from older leaves to developing buds. The pattern and location of yellowing provides
critical diagnostic clues for identifying the underlying cause.

## Detailed Explanation

Yellowing leaves on cannabis plants are one of the most common problems growers encounter,
and the causes range from simple environmental factors to complex nutrient interactions.
Understanding the pattern of yellowing, where it starts on the plant, and what stage of
growth the plant is in are all critical clues for accurate diagnosis. A systematic approach
to troubleshooting will save time and prevent misdiagnosis that could worsen the problem.

The most prevalent cause of yellowing is nitrogen deficiency, which typically begins on
the oldest, lowest leaves and progresses upward as the plant redistributes mobile nitrogen
to new growth. During the vegetative stage, nitrogen deficiency usually indicates the grower
needs to increase nitrogen availability through either soil amendments or adjusted feeding
schedules. However, during late flowering some yellowing of lower fan leaves is perfectly
normal and expected as the plant naturally cannibalizes its own foliage to fuel bud
development. The [[cannabis-nutrient-deficiency-guide]] provides a comprehensive visual
reference for identifying nitrogen deficiency versus other nutrient problems, with color
photographs and detailed descriptions of progressive symptoms.

pH imbalance is another leading cause of yellowing that often masquerades as a nutrient
deficiency. Cannabis plants absorb nutrients optimally in a narrow pH range: 6.0 to 7.0
for soil grows and 5.5 to 6.5 for hydroponic and soilless systems like coco coir. When
pH drifts outside this range, essential nutrients become chemically unavailable to the
roots even if they are abundantly present in the growing medium. This phenomenon, known
as nutrient lockout, is one of the most frustrating problems for growers because adding
more fertilizer actually makes the problem worse. The [[cannabis-nutrient-lockout-and-ph]]
page explains this mechanism in detail, and the [[cannabis-ph-management-and-soil-flushing]]
page covers practical correction strategies including how to properly flush and re-balance
your growing medium.

Overwatering is a surprisingly common cause of yellowing that has nothing to do with
nutrients at all. When soil remains persistently saturated, roots are deprived of oxygen
and cannot function properly, leading to drooping, yellowing leaves that feel soft and
limp to the touch. This is often accompanied by root rot in severe cases, where
pathogenic organisms attack the weakened root system. The symptoms can look remarkably
similar to nitrogen deficiency, which leads many growers to add more nutrients when the
real solution is to water less frequently. Checking soil moisture with a finger or
moisture meter before each watering is the simplest preventive measure.

Mobile versus immobile nutrients follow predictable patterns when deficient, and learning
these patterns is the key to accurate diagnosis. Mobile nutrients like nitrogen, phosphorus,
potassium, and magnesium can be relocated within the plant, so deficiency symptoms appear
on older, lower leaves first as the plant moves these elements to support new growth.
Immobile nutrients like calcium, iron, manganese, zinc, and copper cannot be moved once
deposited in plant tissue, so deficiency symptoms appear on new growth and younger leaves
first. The [[cervantes-nutrient-mobility-deficiency-diagnosis]] guide is an excellent
resource for understanding these patterns and using them to narrow down the specific
nutrient that is lacking. For example, interveinal chlorosis (yellowing between the veins
while veins remain green) on new leaves points to iron deficiency, while the same pattern
on older leaves suggests magnesium deficiency.

Light stress can also cause yellowing, particularly when leaves are positioned too close
to intense grow lights. This type of yellowing usually appears as bleaching, whitening, or
a papery translucence on the uppermost leaves closest to the light source rather than a
true yellow. The affected leaves may also feel brittle and crispy. Heat stress combined
with light stress accelerates the damage and can cause widespread canopy yellowing if not
corrected. Reviewing the [[heat-stress-cannabis]] page can help distinguish light burn
from nutrient deficiency, and the solution is simply to increase the distance between
the canopy and the light fixture.

Temperature extremes on either end of the spectrum can interfere with nutrient uptake
and cause yellowing. Cold temperatures below 15 degrees Celsius slow metabolic processes
and root function dramatically, while temperatures above 30 degrees Celsius can cause heat
stress that increases transpiration faster than roots can absorb water and nutrients.
Root zone temperature is especially important, as cold roots cannot take up phosphorus
efficiently, leading to a distinctive purpling and yellowing of leaves. The
[[cannabis-plant-stress-types-and-recovery]] page covers the full spectrum of environmental
stressors and their solutions.

Nutrient toxicity, particularly nitrogen toxicity from overfeeding during early
vegetative growth, can also cause yellowing leaves, though the pattern differs from
deficiency. Nitrogen toxicity typically causes extremely dark green leaves with a shiny
waxy appearance and downward leaf curling (clawing) before progressing to yellowing and
necrosis on the leaf tips. Excessive phosphorus can cause zinc and iron lockout, which
then presents as interveinal chlorosis. The [[cannabis-nutrient-burn-and-toxicity]] page
explains how to identify and correct toxicity issues.

Root problems including root bound containers, root rot from overwatering, and pest
damage to roots from fungus gnat larvae will all manifest above ground as yellowing
leaves because the plant simply cannot take up water and nutrients effectively regardless
of how much is available. Checking root health by gently inspecting the root zone is an
important diagnostic step that many growers overlook until the problem becomes severe.

## Key Points

- Nitrogen deficiency is the most common cause and starts on the oldest, lowest leaves
  progressing upward [[cannabis-nutrient-deficiency-guide]]
- pH imbalance causes nutrient lockout even when nutrients are abundant in the medium
  [[cannabis-nutrient-lockout-and-ph]]
- Overwatering deprives roots of oxygen, causing yellowing unrelated to nutrient levels
  [[cannabis-lighting-guide]]
- Mobile nutrients show deficiency on old leaves first; immobile nutrients affect new
  growth [[cervantes-nutrient-mobility-deficiency-diagnosis]]
- Light burn from proximity to grow lights causes bleaching rather than true chlorotic
  yellowing [[heat-stress-cannabis]]
- Nutrient toxicity can cause yellowing through leaf tip burn and downward leaf clawing
  [[cannabis-nutrient-burn-and-toxicity]]
- Some lower leaf yellowing during late flowering is normal bud-directed nutrient
  mobilization [[cannabis-nutrient-management]]
