# How to Attract Native Bees and Beneficial Insects to Your Garden

## Short Answer
Attracting native bees and beneficial insects requires providing continuous
food sources through diverse flowering plants, creating nesting habitat like
bare soil and hollow stems, and eliminating pesticide use. Unlike honeybees,
most native bees are solitary and need undisturbed ground or dead plant
material to reproduce, making thoughtful garden design essential for their
survival.

## Detailed Explanation
The decline of pollinator populations worldwide has made it critical for
gardeners to create habitats that support not just honeybees, but the
thousands of native bee species and predatory insects that keep ecosystems
balanced. While managed beekeeping-basics|beekeeping focuses primarily
on the European honeybee, the vast majority of pollination services in home
gardens are performed by native solitary bees, bumblebees, hoverflies, and
wasps. Understanding their needs is the first step to building a thriving
insect community.

Native bees come in many forms, from tiny sweat bees to large carpenter
bees, and each species has different foraging preferences and nesting
requirements. Unlike honeybees that live in large colonies, most native bees
are solitary, nesting in bare ground, hollow plant stems, or rotting wood.
Providing these nesting sites alongside the right plants dramatically
increases the number of pollinators your garden can support. A
comprehensive [[beneficial-insects-guide]] can help
you identify the species already present in your area and understand what
they need to thrive.

The foundation of any pollinator habitat is a diverse selection of flowering
plants that bloom in succession from early spring through late fall.
Early-season bloomers like crocus and willow provide critical resources when
queens emerge, while late-season flowers like asters and goldenrod help
pollinators build reserves for winter. A well-designed
[[mollison-designers-bee-forage-tree-planting]] strategy ensures no gaps in the
nectar and pollen calendar, which is especially important for sustaining
populations through lean times.

Beyond just planting flowers, creating dedicated
[[mollison-designers-insectary-plants-and-beneficial-insect-habitat]] involves providing
shelter, water, and overwintering sites. This might include leaving some
areas of bare, undisturbed soil for ground-nesting bees, maintaining dead
flower stalks through winter for stem-nesting species, and providing shallow
water sources with stones for landing. Insect hotels can supplement natural
nesting sites, though they should be properly maintained to avoid becoming
breeding grounds for pests and disease organisms.

Predatory insects like ladybugs, lacewings, and parasitic wasps are equally
important to attract, as they provide natural pest control services. The
[[gaias-garden-bees-and-pollinators]] emphasizes integrating these insects into the broader garden ecosystem through guild planting, where each plant supports multiple functions including pest management, nutrient accumulation, and pollination.

Plant selection matters enormously. Native plants generally support more
insect species than ornamentals, and many native bees have evolved to
specialize on particular plant families. Clustering plants in groups of
three to five rather than scattering singles makes them more visible to
foraging insects. The [[hemenway-insectary-plants-and-beneficial-insects]] recommends dedicating specific areas of the garden to plants known to attract predatory insects, creating a biological pest control buffer around vulnerable crops.

Seasonal management also plays a role. Avoiding fall garden cleanup allows
overwintering larvae and eggs to survive. Leaving leaf litter in garden beds
provides insulation for ground-nesting bees and habitat for ground beetles
and other predatory insects. In spring, wait until temperatures consistently
reach 50 degrees Fahrenheit before disturbing garden beds, as many native
bees are still emerging from their winter retreats.

Water is an often-overlooked resource for pollinators and beneficial insects.
A shallow dish filled with pebbles or marbles provides a safe drinking
source where insects can land without drowning. Birdbaths can serve double
duty if they include stones or branches for insect access. In dry periods,
consistent water availability can be the difference between a thriving
pollinator population and one that abandons the area entirely.

## Key Points
- Provide continuous bloom from early spring through late fall with
  [[permaculture-plants-insectary-bee-forage]] that include both native and
  adapted species
- Leave areas of bare, undisturbed soil and standing dead stems for solitary
  bee nesting habitat
- Eliminate or minimize pesticide use, as even organic sprays can harm
  beneficial insects and disrupt food webs
- Create dedicated [[mollison-designers-insectary-plants-and-beneficial-insect-habitat]] with
  water sources, shelter, and overwintering sites
- Practice [[holzer-bee-collapse-natural-beekeeping]] methods if
  keeping honeybees, prioritizing colony health over honey production

Creating corridors and connected habitat is increasingly recognized as
important for pollinator health. Rather than isolated garden plots, a
network of pollinator-friendly spaces across a neighborhood supports larger,
more resilient insect populations. Even small contributions like a window
box with native flowers or a single fruit tree in bloom can strengthen these
corridors and provide stepping stones for pollinators moving through the
landscape.
