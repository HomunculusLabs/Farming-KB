# What Is Gypsum and How Do I Use It in My Garden?

## Short Answer

Gypsum (calcium sulfate dihydrate) is a naturally occurring mineral used as a [[soil-ph|soil amendment]] to improve structure without changing pH.
Unlike lime, gypsum adds calcium and sulfur without raising soil acidity or alkalinity, making it ideal for [[clay-soil|clay soils]] and sodic conditions.
It also plays a role in [[growing-gourmet-gypsum-lime-ph-management|mushroom cultivation]] where it improves substrate texture and pH stability.

## What Is Gypsum?

Gypsum is a soft sulfate mineral composed of calcium sulfate dihydrate (CaSO₄·2H₂O).
It has been used in agriculture for centuries to treat problem soils.
The mineral dissolves slowly in water, releasing calcium and sulfate ions into the soil solution.
Agricultural gypsum is typically sold as a fine powder or pelletized form for easier spreading.
It is distinct from [[dolomite]] and [[calcitic-limestone|agricultural lime]], which are carbonate-based amendments that raise pH.

## How Gypsum Improves Soil Structure

Gypsum works primarily by displacing sodium ions on clay particles with calcium ions.
This displacement causes clay particles to flocculate, or clump together, improving [[soil-structure-and-aggregation|soil structure]].
Better structure means improved [[soil-porosity|porosity]], water infiltration, and root penetration.
In [[clay-soil-management|clay soil management]], gypsum can reduce crusting on the surface and make the soil more workable.
It does this without altering [[soil-ph-and-nutrient-availability|soil pH]], which is a critical distinction from lime-based amendments.
The sulfate component also contributes [[sulfur-plant-nutrition|sulfur as a plant nutrient]], supporting protein synthesis and chlorophyll formation.

## Gypsum vs Lime vs Dolomite

Many gardeners confuse gypsum with lime, but they serve very different purposes.
Lime ([[calcitic-limestone|calcitic]] or [[dolomite|dolomitic]]) neutralizes soil acidity and raises pH, while gypsum does not.
[[comparison-gypsum-vs-dolomite|Gypsum compared to dolomite]] shows that dolomite adds both calcium and magnesium but always raises pH.
[[comparison-gypsum-vs-sulfur|Gypsum compared to sulfur]] reveals that elemental sulfur lowers pH while gypsum does not.
[[comparison-sulfur-vs-gypsum|The reverse comparison]] confirms that sulfur is for acidifying soil, gypsum is for structural improvement.
As [[solomon-lime-types-agricultural-dolomitic-gypsum-ph-myth-organic-garden|Solomon explains]], the "gypsum as a pH amendment" myth persists but is incorrect.
Choose gypsum when you need calcium without pH change; choose lime when you need to raise pH.

## When to Apply Gypsum

Gypsum is most beneficial in three specific soil situations.
First, sodic or saline soils where excess sodium has degraded structure respond dramatically to gypsum applications.
Second, heavy [[clay-soil-gardening|clay soil gardens]] benefit from improved tilth and drainage after gypsum treatment.
Third, soils already at the correct pH that need additional calcium without raising it further.
Use a [[soil-testing-and-interpretation|soil test]] to confirm whether gypsum is needed before applying it.
If your soil test shows adequate calcium and low sodium, gypsum will provide little benefit.
Avoid applying gypsum to sandy soils unless addressing a specific sodium problem, as it can leach calcium away.

## Application Rates and Methods

For general [[gypsum-soil-amendment|soil amendment]], spread 20-40 pounds per 1,000 square feet on garden beds.
Work the gypsum into the top 6 inches of soil for best contact with clay particles.
For established lawns or perennial beds, surface application followed by irrigation works well.
Results are not immediate; gypsum can take several months to fully react with clay soils.
Fall application allows winter freeze-thaw cycles to help incorporate the mineral.
In raised beds with known clay issues, mixing gypsum into the fill soil at planting time is effective.
Always water thoroughly after application to begin the dissolution process.

## Gypsum in Mushroom Cultivation

Gypsum serves an important role in [[growing-gourmet-gypsum-lime-ph-management|mushroom substrate preparation]].
It prevents substrate grains from clumping together during sterilization and incubation.
The calcium content supports mycelial growth while the sulfate contributes to metabolic processes.
Typical addition rates are 2-5% of dry substrate weight for grain-based grows.
It also helps buffer pH shifts that occur during mycelial colonization.
Mushroom growers use agricultural gypsum rather than construction-grade (drywall) gypsum to avoid contaminants.

## What About Gypsum in Container Gardens?

Container soils benefit less from gypsum than in-ground clay soils.
Most potting mixes use [[loam-soil|loam]], peat, or coir-based blends that already drain well.
However, if your container mix has heavy clay components, gypsum can improve drainage.
Container gardeners should focus more on [[soil-fertility-amendment-rates|fertility amendments]] and proper watering.
Gypsum can supplement calcium in container-grown tomatoes and peppers that are prone to blossom end rot.
Apply at half the recommended garden rate for containers, mixed into the top few inches.
If you are making your own potting mix with native soil, adding gypsum during mixing helps prevent hardening.

## What About Using Gypsum Alongside Cover Crops?

Combining gypsum applications with [[coleman-cover-crop-rotation-and-soil-health|cover cropping]] creates a synergistic soil improvement strategy.
The gypsum opens up the soil structure while cover crop roots physically penetrate and break up compacted layers.
Deep-rooted cover crops like daikon radish and annual rye create channels that gypsum-treated soil maintains.
This combination is especially effective for reclaiming degraded or [[clay-soil-remediation|clay soil remediation]] sites.
Apply gypsum before seeding the cover crop so it begins reacting during the growing season.
When the cover crop is terminated and incorporated, the improved soil structure persists for subsequent vegetable crops.

## Limitations and Common Misconceptions

Gypsum is not a universal soil cure despite some marketing claims.
It does not break up compacted soil by itself; [[soil-compaction-solutions|compaction solutions]] require physical intervention and organic matter.
Core aeration, cover cropping, and compost additions work alongside gypsum to restore damaged soils.
It does not replace the need for [[soil-health-and-management|comprehensive soil health management]].
Gypsum will not correct severe nutrient imbalances beyond calcium and sulfur.
The [[calcium-magnesium-secondary-nutrients|calcium-to-magnesium ratio]] in soil is not significantly improved by gypsum alone.
Some gardeners mistakenly apply gypsum annually as a routine amendment when it is only needed for specific conditions.
A proper [[soil-testing-methods-interpretation|soil test interpretation]] will tell you whether gypsum addresses an actual deficiency or imbalance.

## Key Points

- Gypsum (calcium sulfate) improves soil structure without changing pH.
- It is most useful for clay soils, sodic soils, and situations needing calcium without pH adjustment.
- Gypsum is different from lime — [[comparison-gypsum-vs-dolomite|compare gypsum vs dolomite]] to understand when each is appropriate.
- Always conduct a [[soil-testing|soil test]] before applying gypsum to confirm it is needed.
- Application rates typically range from 20-40 lbs per 1,000 sq ft for garden beds.
- Gypsum also benefits [[growing-gourmet-gypsum-lime-ph-management|mushroom substrate preparation]] by preventing clumping.
- It does not replace organic matter, [[soil-compaction-diagnosis-remediation|compaction remedies]], or comprehensive fertility management.
- Results take months to appear as gypsum slowly reacts with soil particles.
- Container gardens rarely need gypsum unless using heavy clay-based mixes.
