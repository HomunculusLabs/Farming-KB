# How to Make a Balanced Compost Recipe

## Short Answer
A balanced compost recipe combines carbon-rich brown materials (leaves, straw,
[[fukuoka-textdoc-sawdust-wood-chips-orchard-soil-acidification-experiment]]) with nitrogen-rich green materials (food scraps, grass clippings,
manure) in an approximate ratio of 30 parts carbon to 1 part nitrogen by weight.
Achieving this C:N ratio, maintaining proper moisture at 50 to 60 percent, and
ensuring adequate aeration produces [[query-what-is-the-difference-between-hot-compost-and-cold-compost]] that decomposes quickly into
nutrient-rich humus.

## Detailed Explanation
Composting is the controlled decomposition of organic materials into a stable,
humus-rich soil amendment. The fundamental principle behind successful
composting is balancing carbon and nitrogen in the right proportions. Carbon
provides energy for the decomposer organisms, while nitrogen provides the
protein they need to grow and reproduce. Too much carbon and the pile breaks
down slowly and stays cool. Too much nitrogen and the pile becomes anaerobic,
producing foul odors and losing nitrogen as ammonia gas. The ideal
[[biointensive-composting]] approach emphasizes collecting a wide diversity of materials to
maximize microbial diversity in the finished compost.

Building the pile correctly determines whether you get hot compost that finishes
in weeks or cold compost that takes months. A hot [[mushroom-cultivator-compost-pile-management]] needs to be at
least three feet on each side to generate and retain heat. Start with a layer of
[[fungal-diversity-leaf-litter-coarse-woody-debris]] material at the bottom for drainage, then alternate layers of
browns and greens, aiming for roughly three parts browns to one part greens by
volume. Each layer should be watered as it is added so that the entire pile is
uniformly moist, like a wrung-out sponge. Some growers add a handful of finished
compost or garden soil between layers to inoculate the pile with decomposer
organisms.

Moisture management is critical throughout the composting process. The pile
should feel damp but not dripping wet, roughly 50 to 60 percent moisture by
weight. If the pile is too dry, decomposition slows dramatically and the pile
cools. If it is too wet, anaerobic conditions develop, producing hydrogen
sulfide (rotten egg smell) and butyric acid (vomit-like smell). Cover the pile
with a tarp during heavy rain to prevent waterlogging, and add water during dry
spells. Turning the pile every one to two weeks with a pitchfork reintroduces
oxygen, redistributes moisture, and brings material from the cooler outer layers
into the hot center.

Temperature monitoring tells you whether the pile is working correctly. A
healthy hot compost pile will reach 130 to 160 degrees Fahrenheit within two to
three days of building. This heat kills weed seeds, pathogens, and most disease
organisms. If the pile does not heat up, it usually means the C:N ratio is off,
the pile is too dry, or the pieces are too large. If the pile gets too hot
(above 170 degrees), it can kill beneficial organisms and slow decomposition.

## Key Points
- Aim for a 30:1 carbon-to-nitrogen ratio by weight using roughly three parts
  browns to one part greens by volume
- Build piles at least three feet on each side and maintain 50 to 60 percent
  moisture content
- Turn the pile every one to two weeks to maintain oxygen and redistribute heat
- Target internal temperatures of 130 to 160 degrees Fahrenheit for pathogen
  kill and fast decomposition
- Finished compost is dark, crumbly, and earthy-smelling with no recognizable
  ingredients
