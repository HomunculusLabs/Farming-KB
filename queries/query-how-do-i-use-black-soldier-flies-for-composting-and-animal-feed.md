# How Do I Use Black Soldier Flies for Composting and Animal Feed?

## Short Answer
Black soldier fly larvae (Hermetia illucens) are an incredibly efficient means of converting food waste and organic matter into nutrient-dense animal feed and compost.
The larvae can consume enormous quantities of organic waste, reducing volume by up to 95 percent while converting it into protein-rich biomass suitable for feeding poultry, fish, and other livestock.
Setting up a [[black-soldier-fly-composting]] system requires a well-designed bin, a food waste source, and patience while establishing a breeding colony.

## Detailed Explanation

The black soldier fly is a native insect found throughout much of the world that has gained enormous popularity among permaculture practitioners and sustainable farmers.
Unlike houseflies, black soldier flies do not bite, do not carry diseases, and are not attracted to human dwellings.
The adult flies live only five to eight days and do not eat at all during their adult stage, focusing solely on mating and egg-laying.
It is the larval stage that performs the valuable work of waste conversion, and a single colony can process tens of pounds of food waste per day.

The biology of the black soldier fly makes it uniquely suited to composting applications in a variety of settings.
The larvae are voracious eaters that can consume virtually any organic material, including fruit and vegetable scraps, meat, dairy, manure, and processed foods that would be problematic in traditional composting.
They thrive in dense populations and naturally self-harvest when they reach maturity by seeking a dry place to pupate.
This self-harvesting behavior means that with properly designed bins, the mature larvae will crawl out of the composting chamber and into a collection container with no human intervention required.
The [[black-soldier-fly-larvae-as-feed]] resource details the nutritional profile of harvested larvae and feeding guidelines for various livestock species.

Setting up a black soldier fly composting system begins with constructing or purchasing a suitable bin for your scale of operation.
Commercial units are available, but many homesteaders build their own using plastic containers, PVC pipes, and collection ramps.
The basic design includes a composting chamber where food waste is added, drainage for excess liquid, and a ramp that allows mature larvae to crawl upward and drop into a harvest container.
The larvae need warmth, ideally between 77 and 95 degrees Fahrenheit, to grow rapidly and process waste efficiently.
Various [[composting-systems]] designs can be adapted for black soldier fly use with relatively minor modifications to the basic structure.

Attracting a wild colony to your bin is the simplest approach for getting started with black soldier fly composting.
Black soldier flies are common in most areas during warm months, and a bin filled with food waste will naturally attract egg-laying females from the surrounding area.
Placing the bin in a partially shaded location near a food source helps attract wild populations more quickly.
Some practitioners purchase larvae from suppliers to jump-start their colonies, which is more reliable but adds initial cost to the project.
Once established, a colony can persist year-round in warm climates or be maintained through the winter with heated bins.
The [[composting-methods-comparison]] resource shows how black soldier fly composting compares to other methods in terms of speed, volume capacity, and nutrient retention.

The liquid runoff from a black soldier fly bin, sometimes called soldier fly tea, is a potent liquid fertilizer for garden use.
Unlike vermicompost leachate, which can vary in quality, soldier fly tea is consistently nutrient-rich because the larvae process waste so thoroughly.
This liquid can be diluted and applied directly to garden plants as a nitrogen-rich foliar feed or soil drench.
The residual material left after the larvae have finished processing, sometimes called frass, is a dark, odorless compost that makes an excellent soil amendment.
Together, the larvae, liquid, and frass represent three valuable outputs from what would otherwise be waste material.

When comparing black soldier fly composting to traditional [[vermicomposting]], each system has distinct advantages and trade-offs.
Black soldier flies process waste much faster, can handle a wider range of materials including meat and dairy, and produce harvestable protein as a byproduct.
Vermicomposting operates at lower temperatures, can be done indoors year-round, and produces worm castings that many gardeners consider the gold standard of compost.
The [[comparison-black-soldier-fly-vs-vermicomposting]] provides a detailed breakdown of processing speed, nutritional output, space requirements, and management intensity for each system.

Harvested black soldier fly larvae are approximately 40 percent protein and 30 percent fat by dry weight, making them comparable to commercial fishmeal in nutritional value.
They can be fed live to poultry, fish, and reptiles, or dried and ground into meal for inclusion in formulated feeds.
The larvae also contain beneficial calcium and other minerals that contribute to animal health and bone development.
For small-scale operations, feeding live larvae to backyard chickens is the simplest and most rewarding approach, as chickens will enthusiastically consume them.

Black soldier fly larvae go through five instar stages before reaching maturity, with the greatest food consumption occurring during the final instar stages.
Under optimal conditions of warmth and adequate food supply, the entire larval development from egg to prepupae takes approximately two to three weeks.
Temperature is the most critical environmental factor; below 60 degrees Fahrenheit, larval development slows dramatically and may stop entirely.
The larvae generate heat as they process waste, which can help maintain optimal temperatures in the bin during cooler weather if the colony is large enough.
Adult black soldier flies are attracted to decaying organic matter for egg-laying and are most active during warm, humid conditions in summer months.
Females can lay up to 500 eggs in their short lifespan, though most lay between 200 and 300 eggs in protected crevices near the food source.
The eggs hatch within approximately four days under warm conditions and the tiny larvae immediately begin feeding on available organic waste.
The nutritional quality of harvested larvae depends on the quality of the waste they are fed throughout their development cycle.
Black soldier fly composting produces significantly less odor than traditional composting, making it suitable for urban and suburban settings.
Some practitioners supplement the larvae diet with spent grain from brewing or coffee grounds, both of which are readily available in many communities.

## Key Points
1. Black soldier fly larvae can reduce organic waste volume by up to 95 percent
2. They process meat, dairy, and manure in addition to standard food scraps
3. Mature larvae self-harvest by crawling into collection containers with proper bin design
4. Harvested larvae provide protein-rich feed for poultry, fish, and other livestock
5. Both the liquid runoff and residual frass make excellent garden fertilizers
