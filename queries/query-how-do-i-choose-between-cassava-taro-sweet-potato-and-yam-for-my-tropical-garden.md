# How Do I Choose Between Cassava, Taro, Sweet Potato, and Yam for My Tropical Garden?

## Short Answer

**Start with sweet potato** — it's the easiest to grow, fastest to harvest (3–5 months), has the fewest hazards, and provides both edible roots and leaves. Then add cassava for calorie production on poor soils, taro if you have wet areas or ponds, and yam if you have the labor and patience for a high-value, long-storing staple. Most tropical gardeners benefit from growing a combination rather than choosing just one.

---

## Decision Flowchart

```
Is your soil waterlogged or do you have a pond/wetland area?
├── YES → Plant TARO in the wet zone
└── NO ↓
Is your soil very poor, acidic, and drought-prone?
├── YES → Plant CASSAVA
└── NO ↓
Do you need food in under 4 months?
├── YES → Plant SWEET POTATO
└── NO ↓
Do you have good soil, full sun, and can provide tall stakes?
├── YES → Plant YAM (8–12 month commitment)
└── NO → Plant SWEET POTATO (it works almost everywhere)
```

---

## Choose Cassava When...

- Your soil is poor, acidic (pH as low as 4.5), or sandy with low organic matter
- Rainfall is unreliable — cassava survives months-long dry spells once established
- You want maximum calories per unit of labor over a single growing season
- You have access to sweet varieties (safer, less processing needed)
- You're establishing a food forest and need a hardy, low-maintenance mid-layer crop
- You can process roots within 48 hours of harvest (or leave them in the ground)

**Red flags**: You lack processing knowledge, have young children who might eat raw roots, or need food quickly (cassava takes 9–18 months).

---

## Choose Taro When...

- You have wetland areas, pond edges, or can create flooded paddies
- You want to integrate crop production with aquaculture (fish + taro paddies)
- Both the corm and the leaves are important to your cooking
- You're in a high-rainfall area (1,500+ mm/year) where other crops might waterlog
- Cultural or culinary tradition favors taro-based dishes (poi, fufu, callaloo)

**Red flags**: Your climate is seasonally dry, you have limited water, or taro leaf blight is prevalent in your region.

---

## Choose Sweet Potato When...

- You want the fastest return — harvest in 90–150 days
- You have limited labor and need a low-maintenance crop
- Your soil is well-drained (sweet potato rots in waterlogged conditions)
- You want edible leaves for daily greens in addition to root harvest
- You're growing for nutrition — orange-fleshed varieties are vitamin A powerhouses
- You want a living ground cover that suppresses weeds under fruit trees or in food forests
- You're a beginner gardener — sweet potato is the most forgiving of the four

**Red flags**: Your soil is heavy clay that stays wet, or you live in a cool climate (below 15°C average).

---

## Choose Yam When...

- You need long-term food storage (3–6 months at ambient temperature)
- You're in West Africa or the Pacific where yam has cultural importance
- You have deep, well-drained, fertile soil and can build large mounds
- You can provide 1.5–2 meter stakes or live trellis trees for climbing vines
- You want the highest-value root crop for local markets
- You're planning a mixed crop rotation (yam + maize + melon + cassava)

**Red flags**: You have shallow or compacted soil, can't afford stakes, or need food in under 8 months.

---

## Space and Planting Density Comparison

| Garden Size | Best Options | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| **Small backyard (50–100 m²)** | Sweet potato + taro | Fast harvests, low staking needs, can grow in containers |
| **Medium garden (200–500 m²)** | Sweet potato + cassava + taro | Diversify with cassava for calories; taro in wet spots |
| **Large homestead (1,000+ m²)** | All four | Full polyculture with yam on deep soil, cassava on marginal areas, taro near water, sweet potato as ground cover |

---

## Can I Grow All Four Together?

Yes, and this is actually the traditional approach across the tropics. A classic West African polyculture arranges them vertically and temporally:

1. **Yam mounds** provide the structural foundation (deepest, tallest)
2. **Maize** is planted on mound sides, harvested first (3 months)
3. **Cassava** is planted between mounds, grows through the canopy (9–18 months)
4. **Sweet potato** vines spread as ground cover (3–5 months, or interplanted after maize harvest)
5. **Taro** goes in any wet or low-lying areas of the garden

This stacking strategy maximizes yield per unit area while distributing labor across the calendar.

---

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

1. **Planting cassava and not processing it properly** — learn the cyanide risks before growing bitter varieties
2. **Planting sweet potato in waterlogged soil** — roots will rot, not swell
3. **Not staking yam vines** — yields drop 30–50% without support
4. **Harvesting taro without cooking it** — raw taro causes severe throat irritation from calcium oxalate
5. **Storing sweet potatoes below 10°C** — causes chilling injury (hard core, off-flavors)
6. **Planting yam in shallow soil** — tubers will deform, fork, and be hard to harvest
7. **Not curing sweet potatoes after harvest** — uncured roots spoil within weeks instead of months
