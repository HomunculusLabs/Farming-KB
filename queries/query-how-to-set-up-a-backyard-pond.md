# How to Set Up a Backyard Pond
A backyard pond adds beauty, wildlife habitat, and water management capacity to
your landscape. This guide covers the full process from planning through
winterization, with connections to broader permaculture water harvesting
strategies. For foundational design principles, see [[pond-design-and-construction]].
## Step 1: Site Selection
Choosing the right location is the most critical decision. Evaluate these factors:
- **Sunlight**: Most aquatic plants need 4 to 6 hours of direct sun daily. Over
  8 hours accelerates algae; too little limits flowering plants like lilies.
  Observe your yard across the day and seasons before committing.
- **Trees and roots**: Avoid placing the pond under large deciduous trees.
  Falling leaves decompose into ammonia that harms fish. Roots can puncture
  liners and shift preformed shells, leading to costly repairs.
- **Drainage**: Natural runoff should flow away from the pond, not toward it.
  Low-lying areas flood during heavy rain and collect fertilizer or sediment.
  Test soil percolation and avoid areas with seasonally high water tables.
- **Utility lines**: Always call before you dig. Mark all underground electrical,
  gas, water, and septic lines.
- **Views and access**: Position the pond where you can enjoy it from patios or
  windows. Ensure a firm path for maintenance access with equipment.
For broader context on siting water features in permaculture design, see
[[water-garden-and-ponds]].
## Step 2: Sizing and Depth Considerations
Pond size depends on your goals: ornamental display, wildlife habitat, fish
keeping, or water storage.
- **Surface area**: A minimum of 50 square feet is recommended for ecological
  stability. Smaller ponds swing rapidly in temperature and water chemistry.
- **Depth**: Aim for 18 to 24 inches for goldfish and general planting. Koi
  need at least 3 feet to overwinter safely and evade predators. Include a
  shallow shelf at 6 to 12 inches deep around the perimeter for marginals.
- **Volume**: Calculate length times width times average depth, then multiply
  cubic feet by 7.48 for gallons. Larger volumes are more stable.
Detailed building guidance with dimension tables is in [[backyard-pond-building]].
## Step 3: Excavation Methods
Choose your approach based on pond size and soil conditions:
- **Manual digging**: Suitable for ponds under 100 square feet in soft or loamy
  soil. Use a flat shovel for shelves and a spade for the deep zone. Check
  depth frequently with a string line and level.
- **Mini excavator**: Rent for ponds over 100 square feet or in hard-packed
  clay and rocky soils. Much faster but requires adequate yard access.
- **Layered approach**: Dig the deep center first, then carve graduated shelves.
  Tamp the bottom smooth to remove sharp rocks and roots that could damage the
  liner.
## Step 4: Liner vs. Preformed Options
- **Flexible liners (EPDM rubber or PVC)**: Preferred for custom shapes and
  larger ponds. EPDM is more durable (20-plus year lifespan) and UV resistant.
  Calculate liner size as pond length plus twice the depth plus 2 feet of
  overhang on each side, in both dimensions.
- **Preformed shells**: Easier for small beginner ponds. Limited to fixed shapes
  and sizes, typically under 200 gallons. Risk of warping if not fully
  supported by a sand bed.
- **Underlayment**: Always install geotextile fabric beneath the liner to
  protect against punctures from rocks and roots.
## Step 5: Filtration Systems
Clean, balanced water requires three complementary filtration types:
- **Mechanical filtration**: Captures physical debris (leaves, fish waste,
  particles) using foam pads, brushes, or settlement chambers. Clean media
  regularly to maintain flow.
- **Biological filtration**: Beneficial bacteria (Nitrosomonas and Nitrobacter)
  convert toxic ammonia into nitrite and then into nitrate. Biofilters contain
  porous media like lava rock, bio-balls, or ceramic rings for maximum surface
  area for bacterial colonization.
- **Plant-based filtration**: Aquatic plants absorb nitrates directly, completing
  the nitrogen cycle. A constructed bog filter where water percolates through
  gravel planted with marginals is highly effective.
Combining all three produces the healthiest water. For ongoing filter care, see
[[pond-maintenance-and-troubleshooting]].
## Step 6: Plant Selection
Plants provide oxygen, shade, nutrient uptake, and habitat. Layer by depth:
- **Marginal plants** (shelf zone, 0 to 6 inches): Blue flag iris, pickerelweed,
  soft rush, cattail, cardinal flower. They stabilize edges and filter runoff.
- **Submerged plants** (deep zone): Hornwort, anacharis, eelgrass. These
  oxygenate water and compete with algae for dissolved nutrients.
- **Floating plants** (surface): Hardy water lilies provide essential shade.
  Water hyacinth and duckweed absorb nutrients aggressively but must be
  controlled to prevent surface coverage.
## Step 7: Fish Options
Fish add life and color but increase the biological load on the pond:
- **Goldfish**: Hardy, inexpensive, tolerant of varied conditions. Suitable for
  ponds 18 inches or deeper. Popular varieties include comet, shubunkin, and
  fantail.
- **Koi**: Larger, more colorful, and longer-lived (25-plus years). Require 3
  feet minimum depth, heavier filtration, and premium feed. More visible to
  predators and susceptible to disease in crowded conditions.
Introduce fish only after the pond has cycled for 2 to 4 weeks. Start with a
few fish and add gradually to avoid overwhelming the biofilter. For aquaculture
integration, see [[pond-and-aquaculture]].
## Step 8: Mosquito Control
Stagnant water attracts mosquitoes. Use a multi-layered approach:
- **Circulation**: A pump, waterfall, or fountain keeps the surface moving and
  discourages egg-laying.
- **Biological controls**: Mosquito fish (Gambusia) or native fathead minnows
  consume larvae. Bacillus thuringiensis israelensis (Bti) dunks are a safe
  biological larvicide harmless to fish, plants, and wildlife.
- **Design**: Eliminate still, shallow pockets where water collects without flow.
  Ensure adequate surface agitation throughout the pond.
## Step 9: Winterizing
In cold climates, prepare the pond before the first hard freeze:
- Trim and remove dead or dying plant material to prevent decomposition and
  nutrient release in winter.
- Move tropical plants indoors or into a greenhouse before frost.
- Stop feeding fish when water temperature drops below 50 degrees Fahrenheit.
  Their metabolism slows and undigested food causes illness.
- Install a floating de-icer or air bubbler to maintain a small opening in the
  ice for gas exchange. Never smash ice with a hammer as shock waves harm fish.
- Clean mechanical filters and store UV clarifiers indoors for the season.
## Step 10: Integration with Permaculture Water Harvesting
A backyard pond can serve as a multifunctional element in permaculture design:
- Connect gutter downspouts to the pond via a settling basin to capture roof
  runoff while filtering sediment.
- Use the pond as part of a larger swale and rain garden system to slow, spread,
  and sink water across your property, reducing erosion and recharging
  groundwater.
- Position the pond to passively irrigate downhill garden beds through
  controlled overflow outlets, creating a gravity-fed distribution network.
- Design pond edges with constructed wetland zones that treat greywater before
  it enters the main basin.
For a detailed comparison of harvesting methods, see
[[water-harvesting-techniques-comparison]] and [[rainwater-harvesting-systems]].
## Summary Checklist
1. Select a site with 4 to 6 hours of sun, good drainage, no overhead trees.
2. Size the pond for its purpose with proper depth zones and adequate volume.
3. Excavate with graduated shelves and a deep center, tamping smooth.
4. Install geotextile underlayment and a flexible EPDM liner or preformed shell.
5. Set up mechanical, biological, and plant-based filtration working together.
6. Add marginal, submerged, and floating plants in appropriate depth zones.
7. Introduce fish after cycling, choosing goldfish or koi based on pond depth.
8. Control mosquitoes with circulation, fish, and Bti biological larvicide.
9. Winterize by trimming plants, halting feeding, and ensuring gas exchange.
10. Integrate with rainwater harvesting for a resilient, productive landscape.
