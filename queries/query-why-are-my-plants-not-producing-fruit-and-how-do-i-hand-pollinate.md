# Why Are My Plants Not Producing Fruit and How Do I Hand Pollinate?

## Short Answer

When garden plants flower but fail to set fruit, the most common cause is inadequate pollination.
Many vegetables like squash, cucumbers, tomatoes, and peppers rely on insects or wind to transfer pollen between flowers.
If pollinator populations are low or weather prevents insect activity, you can step in and hand pollinate to ensure a harvest.
Understanding why fruit set fails helps you choose the right intervention for each crop in your garden.
Even experienced gardeners face pollination challenges in greenhouses, on balconies, or during extreme weather seasons.

## Detailed Explanation

### Understanding Pollination Basics

Pollination is the transfer of pollen from the male part of a flower (anther) to the female part (stigma).
Some plants are self-pollinating, meaning pollen transfers within the same flower without outside help.
Others are cross-pollinating and require pollen from a different plant of the same species.
Understanding which type your crop needs is the first step in diagnosing fruit set failures.

Strawberries and raspberries are generally self-fertile but produce better berries with insect visits.

### How to Hand Pollinate Squash and Cucurbits

Identify male flowers (straight stems) and female flowers (miniature fruit at the base).
Pick a freshly opened male flower and strip away the petals to expose the pollen-covered anther.
Gently brush the anther against the center of each open female flower, transferring visible yellow pollen.
Repeat every morning during the flowering period since flowers often last only a single day.
Each female flower needs multiple visits for full seed and fruit development.

### How to Hand Pollinate Tomatoes and Peppers

Tomatoes are self-fertile, so pollen just needs to move from anthers to stigma within the same flower.
The easiest method is to tap or flick the flower truss with your finger to release a small cloud of pollen.
An electric toothbrush held against the back of the flower cluster works even better by vibrating pollen loose.
Do this during the warmest part of the day when humidity is moderate for the best pollen release.

Row covers and low tunnels protect flowers from wind and rain but must be opened during bloom for pollinator access.
Greenhouses and high tunnels often exclude pollinators entirely, making hand pollination essential for enclosed crops.
