# What Is an Earthship and How Does It Work?











## Frequently Asked Questions

### What exactly is an earthship?

An earthship is a type of passive solar house built from recycled materials — primarily discarded automobile tires filled with rammed earth — designed to be entirely self-sufficient. It produces its own electricity, harvests its own water, treats its own sewage, and heats and cools itself without fossil fuels. Earthships were invented by architect Michael Reynolds in the 1970s and are now built worldwide through his company, Earthship Biotecture.

### How do earthship walls work?

The walls are constructed from car tires packed with damp earth using a sledgehammer. Each tire holds about 300 pounds of rammed earth, creating a massive thermal-mass brick. Tires are stacked in staggered courses like masonry and plastered over. These walls absorb heat during the day and release it slowly at night, keeping indoor temperatures stable around 18–24°C (65–75°F) year-round without mechanical heating or cooling.

### Can you really build a house out of old tires?

Yes. A fully packed tire is denser and stronger than a standard concrete block. Tire walls have been load-tested and proven to withstand seismic forces and extreme weather. A typical modest earthship uses 600–1,000 tires. The tires are sealed within the wall structure and not exposed to sunlight, so they do not degrade or off-gas. Using tires as building material permanently sequesters waste that would otherwise sit in landfills for thousands of years.

### How does an earthship get water?

Rainwater is harvested from the metal roof and stored in large cisterns (typically 5,000–20,000 gallons) buried or partially buried beside the home. The water passes through sediment filters, carbon filters, and UV sterilization to become potable. In arid regions with insufficient rainfall, supplementary water sources like wells can be added, but the system always prioritizes harvested rainwater first.

### What happens to wastewater in an earthship?

Wastewater is separated into two streams. **Greywater** from sinks, showers, and washing machines flows into indoor planter boxes — botanical cells filled with gravel and soil where plants filter and clean the water naturally. This treated water is then used for toilet flushing or outdoor irrigation. **Blackwater** from toilets goes to an outdoor septic tank and then into a lined constructed wetland where plants and soil biology complete the treatment. No mechanical aeration or chemicals are needed.

### How does an earthship generate electricity?

Photovoltaic solar panels and sometimes a small wind turbine generate electricity, stored in battery banks. Earthships use 12V or 24V DC systems with inverters for standard AC appliances. The key is conservation — earthships are designed from the start to minimize electrical demand through passive solar heating, natural ventilation, and efficient appliances. A typical system needs 600–1,200 watts of solar capacity.

### Can you grow food inside an earthship?

Yes. The south-facing greenhouse zone contains planter beds irrigated by greywater, creating a year-round growing environment for vegetables, herbs, and small fruit trees. The constant temperature and abundant light make the indoor growing space productive in virtually any climate. Many earthship residents grow a significant portion of their annual vegetable consumption indoors.

### Do earthships work in cold climates?

Earthships perform best in climates with clear winter skies and large diurnal temperature swings, but they have been built successfully in cold climates including Montana, Colorado, Canada, and Scotland. Cold-climate adaptations include additional insulation on exterior tire walls, double or triple glazing, and supplementary heating for extreme cold snaps. The thermal mass helps retain heat, but prolonged overcast periods in very cold regions may require backup heating.

### How much does an earthship cost to build?

A professionally built earthship with all systems typically costs $150–$300 per square foot, comparable to conventional construction. Owner-built earthships using scavenged materials can be built for significantly less. The long-term savings are substantial — no utility bills for electricity, water, or sewage. The biggest "cost" is labor: tire packing is physically demanding and time-intensive, often requiring a crew and several months of work.

### Do I need a permit to build an earthship?

Building codes and zoning regulations vary widely by location. Some areas — particularly parts of New Mexico and Colorado — have provisions for alternative building methods. In many jurisdictions, obtaining a permit requires working with engineers who can certify the structural design. Earthship Biotecture has experience navigating permitting processes and offers construction crews who can handle code compliance. Some earthship owners build in areas with minimal building codes.

### How long does it take to build an earthship?

A small earthship (600–800 sq ft) built by a crew of 4–6 experienced workers typically takes 3–4 months. Larger or more complex designs take 6–12 months. Owner-builders with volunteer labor should plan on 6–12 months for a modest home. The tire-packing phase is typically the longest single step.

### Can I build an earthship myself?

Yes, many earthships are owner-built. The tire-packing work requires physical strength and endurance but no special skills. Earthship Biotecture offers Academy programs that teach construction techniques over 1–2 month sessions. The plumbing, electrical, and glazing systems require competent tradespeople or willing learners. A community workshop model — where friends and volunteers help pack tires in exchange for food and experience — is common and effective.

### What are the main problems with earthships?

Common issues include condensation on interior glass surfaces in cold weather, moisture management in humid climates, and the need for ongoing maintenance of greywater planters and mechanical systems. Some owners report difficulty with building permits and financing, as lenders are sometimes hesitant to finance unconventional structures. The labor-intensive construction is a barrier for many. And while thermal mass provides excellent temperature stability, it cannot overcome extreme or prolonged conditions without supplementary systems.

### How does an earthship compare to other natural building methods?

Earthships differ from cob, straw bale, and other natural building methods in their systems integration. While cob and straw bale focus primarily on the building envelope, earthships incorporate water harvesting, wastewater treatment, and energy generation as integral parts of the design. Earthships use recycled industrial materials (tires, cans, bottles) alongside earth, while methods like cob use only natural materials. Each approach has strengths — earthships offer a complete off-grid package; cob offers greater design freedom and lower material costs.
