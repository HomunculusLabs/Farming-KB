# How Do I Start Farming Spirulina at Home for Nutrition?

## What Is Spirulina?

Spirulina is a filamentous cyanobacterium (blue-green microalgae) belonging to the genus *Arthrospira*, most commonly *Arthrospira platensis* and *Arthrospira maxima*. Despite being called "blue-green algae," spirulina is technically a bacterium that photosynthesizes like a plant. It forms microscopic spiral-shaped filaments and naturally thrives in warm, alkaline lakes across Africa, Mexico, and Asia. It has been harvested as food for centuries — the Aztecs collected it from Lake Texcoco, and the Kanembu people of Chad still harvest *dihe* from Lake Chad today.

## Nutritional Profile

Spirulina is one of the most nutrient-dense foods on earth per gram:

- **Protein**: 55–70% dry weight, containing all nine essential amino acids
- **Vitamins**: B1 (thiamine), B2 (riboflavin), B3 (niacin), B6, B9 (folate), and provitamin A (beta-carotene)
- **Minerals**: Iron (highly bioavailable), calcium, magnesium, zinc, potassium
- **Phytonutrients**: Phycocyanin (blue pigment with strong anti-inflammatory properties), chlorophyll, gamma-linolenic acid (GLA)
- **Other**: Significant antioxidant capacity, typically 2–3 g of spirulina per day is considered a functional dose

## Home Cultivation Setup

### Container

Start with a food-grade transparent or translucent container holding 10–40 liters. Glass carboys, acrylic aquariums, or food-safe plastic tubs work well. The goal is to maximize surface area for light exposure. For larger setups, shallow raceway ponds (6–12 inches deep) with gentle paddle-wheel stirring are ideal but not necessary for beginners.

### Growing Medium and Nutrients

Spirulina requires an alkaline, mineral-rich medium. The standard recipe uses:

- **Sodium bicarbonate** (baking soda): 16 g/L — provides carbonate alkalinity
- **Potassium nitrate**: 2 g/L — nitrogen source
- **Sea salt or mixed salts**: 1 g/L — trace minerals; many growers use refined aquarium salt or a premixed spirulina nutrient formula
- **Iron chelate** (Fe-EDTA): 0.1 g/L — critical for chlorophyll synthesis
- **Potassium phosphate**: 0.5 g/L — phosphorus source
- **Magnesium sulfate** (Epsom salt): 0.3 g/L

Distilled or filtered water is recommended. Fill the container, dissolve nutrients completely, and adjust pH before inoculation.

### Light

Spirulina needs 12–16 hours of light per day. Direct sunlight works but risks overheating; a bright windowsill or a simple LED grow light (5000–6500K, full spectrum) positioned 6–12 inches above the culture provides consistent results. Avoid intense midday sun without shade — aim for roughly 2,000–5,000 lux.

### Temperature

Optimal growth occurs at **30–35°C (86–95°F)**. Below 20°C growth slows dramatically; below 15°C the culture may crash. In cooler climates, a seedling heat mat or aquarium heater on a thermostat is essential. Above 38°C, thermal stress can cause culture collapse.

### pH Management

Spirulina thrives in highly alkaline conditions: **pH 9.0–10.5**. This high pH is actually protective — most competing organisms cannot survive in this range. Monitor pH daily with a digital meter. When pH drops below 9.0, add sodium bicarbonate. If pH exceeds 10.5, dilute with fresh water. The alkaline environment is your primary defense against contamination.

## Harvesting and Drying

### Harvesting

Harvest when the culture is deep green and dense (Secchi disk reading below 3 cm, or roughly every 7–14 days depending on growth rate):

1. Pour the culture through a fine mesh screen (20–50 microns) or a dedicated spirulina harvesting cloth
2. Collect the dark green paste retained on the screen
3. Rinse briefly with cold, fresh water to remove residual salts (optional but improves taste)
4. Harvest no more than 50% of the culture volume at once to maintain population density

### Drying

- **Dehydrator**: Spread paste thin on parchment paper; dry at 40–45°C for 4–8 hours until brittle
- **Solar drying**: Spread on food-safe screens in direct sun — effective but may degrade some vitamins
- **Freezing**: Freeze the fresh paste in ice cube trays for longest nutrient retention
- **Fresh consumption**: The paste can be eaten immediately blended into smoothies, spreads, or soups

Store dried spirulina in an airtight glass jar, away from light, at room temperature. Properly dried spirulina lasts 1–2 years.

## Daily Maintenance Routine

| Task | Frequency | Notes |
|------|-----------|-------|
| Stir/agitate the culture | 2–4× daily | Prevents settling and ensures even light exposure |
| Check temperature | Daily | Adjust heater if needed |
| Check pH | Daily | Add bicarbonate if below 9.0 |
| Top off evaporated water | As needed | Use fresh water with ~half-strength nutrients |
| Visual inspection | Daily | Look for color changes or clumping |
| Harvest | Every 7–14 days | When culture is deep green and dense |

## Common Problems

- **Contamination (yellow/brown culture, foul smell)**: Caused by invasive algae, bacteria, or protozoa. Usually results from pH dropping too low or poor hygiene. Sterilize everything, reset pH to 10+, and consider restarting with a clean culture.
- **pH crash**: Spirulina consumes bicarbonate as it grows. If pH drops below 8.5, the culture is vulnerable. Keep sodium bicarbonate on hand and dose preemptively.
- **Algae color changes**: Healthy spirulina is vibrant blue-green. Yellowing suggests nutrient depletion (often nitrogen or iron). Brown or reddish color may indicate dead or stressed culture. White foam on the surface can signal bacterial contamination.
- **Slow or no growth**: Usually a light or temperature issue. Verify the culture is receiving 12+ hours of adequate light and staying above 25°C.
- **Clumping and settling**: Insufficient agitation. Stir more frequently or add a small air pump with a diffuser stone (use filtered air only).

## Yield Expectations

A healthy 20-liter culture can produce **5–15 g of dried spirulina per week**, roughly equivalent to a daily dose of 1–2 g. Scaling to 50–100 liters with proper conditions can yield 20–50 g/week. A dedicated homestead setup (200+ liters in raceway tanks) could theoretically produce 100–200 g/week, approaching meaningful food self-sufficiency for one person.

## Integration with Homesteading Systems

- **Aquaponics**: Spirulina can be grown in the nutrient-rich water from aquaculture systems, provided pH is adjusted upward. Fish wastewater provides nitrogen and phosphorus.
- **Solar integration**: Outdoor raceway ponds powered by solar pumps reduce energy inputs to near zero in warm climates.
- **CO₂ supplementation**: Connecting the culture to a compost off-gas capture system (or simply placing it near a worm bin) can boost growth rates by 20–30%.
- **Waste heat**: Placing the culture vessel near a wood stove, greenhouse, or solar water heater reduces heating costs in cold months.
- **Permaculture zones**: Place spirulina cultivation in Zone 1 (near kitchen) for daily access and monitoring.

## Quick-Start Checklist

- [ ] Obtain a live spirulina starter culture from a reputable supplier
- [ ] Prepare a 10–20 L food-grade transparent container with lid
- [ ] Mix growing medium: baking soda, potassium nitrate, sea salt, iron chelate, potassium phosphate, Epsom salt in filtered water
- [ ] Verify pH is 9.5–10.0; adjust with baking soda if needed
- [ ] Inoculate with starter culture
- [ ] Place in location with 12–16 hours of bright, indirect light
- [ ] Maintain temperature at 30–35°C (use heater if necessary)
- [ ] Stir 2–4 times daily
- [ ] Monitor pH and temperature daily
- [ ] Harvest with 50 μm cloth when culture is dense green (7–14 days)
- [ ] Dry at ≤45°C or consume fresh
- [ ] Replenish nutrients and top off water after each harvest
