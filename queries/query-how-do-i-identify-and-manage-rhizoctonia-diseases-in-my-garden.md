## How Do I Identify and Manage Rhizoctonia Diseases in My Garden?

## Short Answer

Rhizoctonia diseases are caused by the soil-borne fungus [[rhizoctonia-solani]], one of the most destructive and widespread plant pathogens in agriculture and home gardens.
This pathogen attacks plants at multiple growth stages, causing [[cervantes-damping-off-seedling-disease-prevention]] in seedlings, stem cankers in established plants, and root rot in mature crops.
Unlike many fungal pathogens, Rhizoctonia does not produce spores for infection; instead it spreads through soil-borne mycelium and durable resting structures called sclerotia.
The pathogen has an extremely broad host range, infecting hundreds of plant species including vegetables, cereals, turf grasses, and ornamental plants.
Management relies on cultural practices, biological controls, and [[plant-disease-management-in-organic-gardens]] strategies since there are no effective chemical cures for home gardeners.
Understanding the disease cycle and implementing integrated prevention measures described in [[plant-disease-ecology-and-organic-management]] gives the best chance of protecting your crops.

## What Is Rhizoctonia Solani?

[[rhizoctonia-solani]] is a basidiomycete fungus that lives primarily in soil and survives on organic matter as a saprophyte when host plants are absent.
It is classified into multiple anastomosis groups (AGs), each with different host preferences and disease characteristics, which is why the same pathogen causes such diverse symptoms.
The fungus produces thread-like mycelium that grows through soil and makes direct contact with plant surfaces to initiate infection.
Mathematical modeling of its mycelial growth patterns, as documented in [[gadd-mathematical-modelling-rhizoctonia-solani-mycelial-growth]], reveals how the pathogen efficiently colonizes substrates and locates host roots.
When conditions are unfavorable, Rhizoctonia forms hard, compact sclerotia that can persist in soil for years without a host.
The pathogen thrives in warm, moist soils with temperatures between 20 and 30 degrees Celsius, making it especially problematic during spring planting and summer months.
Research described in [[ingham-aact-fungal-disease-suppression-fusarium-rhizoctonia-anthracnose]] shows that compost tea applications can suppress Rhizoctonia through competitive exclusion and antibiosis.

## What Diseases Does Rhizoctonia Cause?

The diseases caused by [[rhizoctonia-solani]] vary depending on the plant species and growth stage affected.
Damping-off is the most familiar symptom, killing seedlings before or shortly after emergence by rotting the stem at the soil line.
This is closely related to the damping-off described in [[cervantes-root-rot-pythium-damping-off]], though Pythium is an oomycete while Rhizoctonia is a true fungus.
Wirestem affects brassica seedlings, causing the stem to become thin, dark, and wiry, often leading to plant death or stunting.
Brown patch disease devastates turf grasses during hot, humid weather, creating circular patches of dead grass that can expand rapidly.
Root rot and below-ground stem cankers develop on mature plants including potatoes, beans, and many vegetables, reducing yield without obvious above-ground symptoms until significant damage has occurred.
Potato scab, while primarily caused by Streptomyces species, can be worsened by Rhizoctonia co-infection leading to rough, corky lesions on tubers.
The diversity of symptoms makes accurate diagnosis essential, and [[plant-disease-identification]] techniques help distinguish Rhizoctonia from other [[fungal-plant-diseases]].

## Which Plants Are Most Susceptible?

Rhizoctonia solani has one of the broadest host ranges of any plant pathogen, affecting hundreds of species across many plant families.
Seedling vegetables of all types are vulnerable to damping-off, including tomatoes, peppers, lettuce, cabbage, broccoli, and cucumbers.
Brassica crops are particularly susceptible to wirestem and bottom rot, especially when planted in cool, wet conditions.
Potatoes suffer from stem canker and tuber black scurf, where dark sclerotia form on the potato skin.
Rice, wheat, barley, and other cereals can be affected by root rot and seedling blight caused by different AG groups.
Turf grasses including bentgrass, ryegrass, and fescue are devastated by brown patch during warm, humid periods.
Beans and legumes develop root rot and hypocotyl cankers that reduce stands and yields significantly.
Ornamental plants including marigolds, zinnias, and impatiens can also fall victim to Rhizoctonia stem rot in landscape beds.
Understanding crop-specific susceptibility helps with planning [[crop-rotation-vegetable-garden]] schedules to break disease cycles.
Keeping records of which crops have shown symptoms in specific beds allows you to tailor [[crop-rotation-benefits]] to your particular disease pressure.

## How Do I Identify Rhizoctonia Symptoms?

Damping-off symptoms include seedlings that fail to emerge (pre-emergence damping-off) or topple over at the soil line shortly after emerging.
A close examination of fallen seedlings reveals a shriveled, darkened, water-soaked stem near the soil surface.
Wirestem in brassicas causes the lower stem to become thin, tough, and darkly discolored while the plant may remain alive but stunted.
Brown patch in turf appears as circular or irregular patches of brown, dead grass ranging from a few inches to several feet in diameter.
A distinctive grayish-white ring of mycelium (called a smoke ring) may be visible at the edge of active brown patch lesions in early morning dew.
Root rot symptoms include poor growth, yellowing of lower leaves, wilting on sunny days, and reduced yields even when above-ground symptoms appear mild.
Digging up affected plants reveals discolored, rotting roots with reddish-brown lesions and reduced feeder root mass.
The practical diagnostic guidelines in [[plant-diseases-and-remedies]] can help confirm whether Rhizoctonia or another pathogen is responsible for symptoms you observe.
Comparing symptoms with those of other vascular wilts described in [[fungal-plant-diseases]] can prevent misdiagnosis and inappropriate treatment.
When in doubt, sending a sample to a plant diagnostic lab is the most reliable way to confirm Rhizoctonia infection before committing to a management plan.

## How Do Cultural Practices Help Manage Rhizoctonia?

Cultural controls form the foundation of Rhizoctonia management because the pathogen is nearly impossible to eradicate once established in soil.
[[crop-rotation-principles]] are essential: rotating away from susceptible crops for at least three to four years reduces pathogen inoculum in the soil.
Avoid consecutive plantings of highly susceptible crops like potatoes, brassicas, and beans in the same location, as documented in [[crop-rotation-and-soil-health-planning]].
Plant seeds and transplants at the proper depth and spacing to promote rapid emergence and reduce the vulnerable seedling stage.
Avoid overwatering and improve soil drainage, since waterlogged conditions create ideal environments for Rhizoctonia infection.
Use certified disease-free seed and avoid saving seed from infected plants or fields.
Remove and destroy infected plant debris at the end of each season rather than composting it near susceptible crops.
Maintain optimal soil fertility balanced with adequate calcium and phosphorus, as stressed plants are more susceptible to infection.
[[coleman-cover-crop-rotation-and-soil-health]] describes how cover crops and rotation together build the soil biology needed to suppress soil-borne pathogens.

## How Do Biological Controls Suppress Rhizoctonia?

Beneficial fungi in the genus [[trichoderma]] are the most effective biological agents against Rhizoctonia solani.
[[trichoderma-harzianum]] produces cell-wall-degrading enzymes and antibiotics that directly attack and kill Rhizoctonia mycelium in the root zone.
[[trichoderma-virens]] produces gliotoxin and other antifungal compounds that inhibit sclerotia germination and mycelial growth.
[[trichoderma-viride]] and [[trichoderma-koningii]] also demonstrate strong mycoparasitic activity against Rhizoctonia species.
Research in [[bloomfield-mycoparasites-fungi-infecting-fungi-trichoderma-biocontrol]] documents the mechanisms by which Trichoderma hyphae coil around and penetrate Rhizoctonia cells.
The comparison in [[comparison-trichoderma-vs-bacillus-vs-pseudomonas-biocontrol]] shows that Trichoderma is generally more effective against Rhizoctonia than bacterial biocontrol agents.
Mycorrhizal fungi provide indirect protection through [[arbuscular-mycorrhizae-pathogen-protection]] by forming physical barriers and stimulating plant immune responses.
Actively aerated compost tea introduces diverse beneficial microorganisms that compete with Rhizoctonia for resources and root colonization sites.
The protocols described in [[ingham-trichoderma-biocontrol-fungi-aact]] and [[ingham-trichoderma-biocontrol-fungi-biology-preactivation]] optimize Trichoderma applications for maximum disease suppression.

## Can Cover Crops and Biofumigation Help?

Certain cover crops can suppress Rhizoctonia through biofumigation, allelopathy, and by fostering beneficial soil microorganisms.
[[biofumigation-and-brassica-cover-crops-for-pest-management]] describes how brassica cover crops release isothiocyanate gases toxic to soil-borne pathogens when their tissues are incorporated into soil.
Mustard and radish cover crops are particularly effective biofumigants that can reduce Rhizoctonia inoculum when turned under warm, moist soil.
Buckwheat, as described in [[solomon-buckwheat-summer-cover-crop-guide]] and [[permaculture-buckwheat]], can suppress weed hosts that harbor Rhizoctonia between crop cycles.
Cover crops with high C:N ratios promote fungal-dominated soil food webs where [[trichoderma]] and other mycoparasites thrive.
The general benefits of [[crop-rotation-benefits]] are amplified when cover crops are included in the rotation to continuously feed beneficial soil biology.
A healthy [[soil-food-web]] provides natural disease suppression through competition, antibiosis, and predation of pathogenic organisms.
Building this living soil ecosystem is a long-term investment that pays dividends in reduced disease pressure across all crops.

## Key Points

- [[rhizoctonia-solani]] causes damping-off, wirestem, brown patch, root rot, and stem canker across hundreds of plant species.
- The pathogen spreads through soil-borne mycelium and persistent sclerotia rather than airborne spores.
- Damping-off kills seedlings at the soil line, while brown patch creates circular dead patches in turf during hot humid weather.
- [[crop-rotation-principles]] of three to four years away from susceptible crops reduce soil inoculum levels over time.
- Biological controls with [[trichoderma-harzianum]], [[trichoderma-virens]], and related species are highly effective against Rhizoctonia.
- [[biofumigation-and-brassica-cover-crops-for-pest-management]] using mustard and radish cover crops can reduce pathogen levels in soil.
- Avoid overwatering, improve drainage, and remove infected debris to make the environment less favorable for infection.
- Maintaining a healthy [[soil-food-web]] through [[crop-rotation-and-soil-health-planning]] provides natural disease suppression.
