# How to Read a Soil Test Report

## Short Answer
A soil test report measures pH, organic matter content, nutrient levels (NPK and
micronutrients), cation exchange capacity, and base saturation. Read it by first
checking pH and organic matter, then comparing each nutrient level to the optimal
range for your crop, and finally looking at the base saturation percentages to
understand nutrient balance. The [[interpreting-soil-test-results]] page provides a
complete guide to interpreting every section of a standard soil test report.

## Detailed Explanation

Soil testing is one of the most important and underused tools available to
gardeners and growers. Rather than guessing at what amendments your soil needs, a
soil test provides objective data on pH, nutrient levels, organic matter content,
and the soil's capacity to hold and exchange nutrients. This information allows you
to make precise, targeted amendments rather than blanket-applying fertilizers that
may create imbalances or waste money on nutrients your soil already has in excess.
Most university extension offices offer affordable soil testing, and private labs
provide more comprehensive analyses that include micronutrient levels and biological
activity measurements.

The first section to check on any soil test report is the pH value. Soil pH
directly controls the availability of virtually every nutrient to plant roots. In
cannabis cultivation, the optimal soil pH range is 6.0 to 6.8 for soil-based grows
and 5.5 to 6.5 for soilless mixes. If your pH is outside this range, it should be
corrected before addressing any individual nutrient levels, because pH correction
alone will unlock nutrients that are already present but unavailable due to lockout.
The [[cannabis-ph-management-and-soil-flushing]] page covers pH correction strategies
using lime, sulfur, and organic amendments for different growing situations.

Organic matter percentage is the second critical metric to evaluate. Organic matter
is the foundation of soil health because it feeds the microbial community, improves
soil structure, increases water-holding capacity, and provides cation exchange sites
that hold nutrients in a plant-available form. Most agricultural soils have 1 to 5%
organic matter, while healthy garden soils should target 5 to 10%. If your organic
matter is below 3%, adding compost, cover crops, or mulch should be your top
priority before spending money on mineral fertilizers, because low organic matter
limits the soil's ability to hold and cycle any nutrients you add. The
[[nutrient-lockout]] page explains how low organic matter contributes to nutrient
availability problems.

The macronutrient section reports levels of nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), and
potassium (K), along with secondary nutrients calcium (Ca), magnesium (Mg), and
sulfur (S). These are typically reported in parts per million (ppm). It is important
to compare your results against the optimal ranges for your specific crop rather
than a generic ideal, because cannabis has different nutrient demands at different
growth stages. For example, phosphorus demand is relatively low during vegetative
growth but increases significantly during early flowering. The
[[cervantes-organic-amendments-chart]] and [[cervantes-organic-mineral-amendments]]
pages provide detailed organic amendment recommendations based on soil test results
for cannabis cultivation at various growth stages.

Cation exchange capacity (CEC) measures the soil's ability to hold positively
charged nutrient ions (cations) and release them to plant roots over time. Soils
with high CEC (clay soils, soils high in organic matter) can hold more nutrients
but may require more aggressive amendment to shift pH. Soils with low CEC (sandy
soils) leach nutrients quickly and require more frequent but smaller applications.
Base saturation, expressed as a percentage of CEC, shows the balance between
calcium, magnesium, potassium, and hydrogen on the exchange sites. The ideal base
saturation ratio is roughly 65 to 75% calcium, 10 to 20% magnesium, and 2 to 5%
potassium. Deviations from these ratios can cause [[nutrient-lockout]] even when
total nutrient levels appear adequate on the test report.

Micronutrient levels, including iron, manganese, zinc, copper, boron, and others,
are reported in ppm and are critical for cannabis health despite being needed in
tiny quantities. Micronutrient deficiencies are often pH-related rather than actual
shortages, so always check pH before supplementing micronutrients. If your soil test
shows adequate micronutrient levels but your plants show deficiency symptoms, the
issue is almost certainly pH lockout or an antagonistic relationship with another
nutrient at excessive levels. The [[cannabis-zinc-nutrient]], [[cannabis-boron-nutrient]],
and [[cannabis-manganese-nutrient]] pages provide specific guidance for diagnosing
and correcting individual micronutrient deficiencies in cannabis.

For cannabis growers transitioning to [[living-soil-cannabis]]
methods, soil testing provides a valuable baseline for building your soil recipe. By
knowing your starting pH, organic matter content, and nutrient levels, you can
calculate exactly what amendments are needed to create an optimal growing
environment. Periodic retesting every 6 to 12 months allows you to track how your
soil is changing over time and adjust your amendment strategy accordingly. This
data-driven approach to soil management is far more effective than following generic
recipes or guesswork.

## Key Points
- Always check pH first on a soil test; pH controls the availability of all other
  nutrients and is the most common cause of apparent deficiency
- Organic matter percentage is the foundation of soil health and should be
  prioritized above mineral amendments if below 3%
- CEC and base saturation reveal nutrient balance issues that total nutrient levels
  alone cannot detect
- [[interpreting-soil-test-results]] provides a complete walkthrough of every metric
  on a standard soil test report with cannabis-specific recommendations
- Compare your results against crop-specific optimal ranges rather than generic
  ideals; cannabis demands change between vegetative and flowering stages
