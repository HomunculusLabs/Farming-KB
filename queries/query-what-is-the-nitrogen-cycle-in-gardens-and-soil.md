# What Is the Nitrogen Cycle in Gardens and Soil

## Short Answer
The nitrogen cycle is the continuous process by which nitrogen moves from the
atmosphere into soil, through plants and animals, and back again. In gardens,
this cycle is driven by soil microorganisms that convert atmospheric nitrogen
into plant-available forms, decompose organic matter to release stored nitrogen,
and transform nitrogen compounds between forms that plants can and cannot
absorb. Understanding this cycle is essential for managing soil fertility
without relying on synthetic fertilizers.

## Detailed Explanation

### Why Nitrogen Matters Most
Nitrogen is the nutrient that plants need in the largest quantities, and it is
also the nutrient most likely to become limiting in organic gardens. Although
78 percent of Earth's atmosphere is nitrogen gas, plants cannot use atmospheric
nitrogen directly. They depend entirely on soil organisms to convert nitrogen
into ammonium and nitrate, the two forms that plant roots can absorb. The
[[teaming-with-nutrients-nitrogen]] overview explains this dependency in
detail, showing that the entire nitrogen economy of a garden is mediated by
bacteria, archaea, and fungi living in the soil.

### Biological Nitrogen Fixation
The entry point of new nitrogen into garden soil is biological nitrogen
fixation, performed primarily by bacteria in the genus Rhizobium that form
symbiotic relationships with legume roots. These bacteria colonize legume
roots, forming visible nodules where they convert atmospheric nitrogen gas into
ammonium. The [[symbiotic-fungi-nitrogen-fixing-symbioses-agriculture]] research
extends this understanding to include free-living nitrogen-fixing bacteria like
Azotobacter and Clostridium that fix nitrogen independently in the rhizosphere,
as well as associative relationships between nitrogen-fixing bacteria and grass
roots. Even non-legume garden beds receive some nitrogen input from biological
fixation, though legume cover crops remain the most productive strategy.

### Nitrification: Making Nitrogen Available
Once nitrogen enters the soil system as ammonium, it undergoes a two-step
microbial transformation called nitrification. First, ammonia-oxidizing bacteria
convert ammonium to nitrite, then nitrite-oxidizing bacteria convert nitrite to
nitrate. Nitrate is the preferred nitrogen form for most garden vegetables
because it is highly mobile in soil solution and easily taken up by roots.
However, this same mobility means nitrate is also the form most susceptible to
leaching below the root zone during heavy rains or over-irrigation.

### The Carbon-to-Nitrogen Ratio Connection
Decomposition is the other major pathway releasing nitrogen for plant uptake.
When organic matter breaks down, the carbon-to-nitrogen ratio of the material
determines whether nitrogen is released or temporarily immobilized. Materials
with a C:N ratio below about 20:1, such as young legume green manure or
finished compost, release nitrogen as they decompose. Materials with a high
C:N ratio, such as wood chips or straw, temporarily tie up available nitrogen.
The [[solomon-carbon-nitrogen-ratios]] reference provides specific C:N values
for common garden materials and explains how to manage them to avoid nitrogen
tie-up during the growing season.

### Mycorrhizal Nitrogen Transfer
Plants take up nitrogen primarily as nitrate and ammonium, with most vegetables
preferring nitrate. Root architecture and mycorrhizal associations affect how
efficiently plants access soil nitrogen. The
[[symbiotic-fungi-mycorrhizal-nitrogen-transfer-networks]] research reveals
that mycorrhizal fungal networks can actually transfer nitrogen between plants,
moving it from nitrogen-rich areas or from decomposing organic matter to
nitrogen-demanding plants. This underground nitrogen sharing means that in
biodiverse gardens with active mycorrhizal networks, nitrogen use efficiency
is significantly higher than in monoculture systems.

### Nitrogen Loss Pathways
Nitrogen losses from garden soil occur through several pathways. Leaching of
nitrate below the root zone is the most common loss, especially in sandy soils
or during heavy rainfall. Denitrification occurs when waterlogged, anaerobic
soil conditions cause bacteria to convert nitrate back into nitrogen gas, which
escapes to the atmosphere. Volatilization of ammonia occurs when ammonium-based
fertilizers or manures are left on the soil surface without incorporation.
Erosion carries nitrogen-rich topsoil off-site. Understanding these loss
pathways allows gardeners to choose management practices that minimize waste.

### Living Mulch for Nitrogen Scavenging
The [[white-clover-ground-cover]] approach uses living mulch to scavenge
residual nitrogen before it leaches below the root zone. Clover's shallow,
dense root system captures nitrate that would otherwise be lost, storing it
biologically. When the clover is eventually mowed or incorporated, this stored
nitrogen is released back to the soil for the next crop. This biological
nitrogen capture is far more efficient than trying to time synthetic fertilizer
applications to match plant uptake patterns.

### Managing the Cycle Organically
Managing the nitrogen cycle organically means working with these natural
processes rather than substituting them with synthetic inputs. Planting legume
cover crops adds new nitrogen biologically. Composting organic waste creates a
slow-release nitrogen source with balanced mineralization rates. Mulching
prevents erosion and leaching losses. Maintaining active soil biology through
no-till practices ensures efficient nitrogen processing and transfer. The
result is a garden nitrogen economy that becomes more self-sufficient over
time, requiring fewer external inputs while producing more consistent, healthy
plant growth.
