# What Are the 12 Permaculture Principles?
## Short Answer
The 12 permaculture principles are a set of design directives formulated by David
Holmgren that guide the creation of sustainable, resilient systems modeled on
natural ecosystems. They serve as a practical framework for
[[permaculture-principles]] applied across agriculture, architecture, community
planning, and daily life. Each principle encourages designers to observe natural
patterns and work with them rather than against them.
## Detailed Explanation
Permaculture, originally developed by Bill Mollison and David Holmgren in the
1970s, combines permanent agriculture and permanent culture into a holistic
design science. While Mollison laid out foundational design thinking in works
such as [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-permaculture-design-principles]],
it was Holmgren who formalized the widely cited list of 12 principles in his
2002 book *Permaculture: Principles and Pathways Beyond Sustainability*. These
principles are grounded in the ethical framework of earth care, people care, and
fair share, as explored in
[[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-ethical-foundations-in-permaculture]].
### Principle 1: Observe and Interact
Before making changes to a landscape or system, a designer spends time watching
how natural forces — sun, wind, water, wildlife — move through the site. Good
observation leads to designs that are better adapted to local conditions rather
than imposed from outside. Tools such as
[[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-zone-and-sector-analysis]]
provide structured methods for this observation, mapping energies and uses
across zones and sectors so that placement decisions are informed by reality
rather than assumption.
### Principle 2: Catch and Store Energy
This principle asks designers to capture resources when they are abundant and
hold them for times of scarcity. On a homestead this might mean building soil
organic matter, installing rainwater tanks, or preserving harvests through
fermentation and drying. At a broader scale, it informs strategies for storing
carbon in forests and grasslands and building community resilience through
shared grain banks or seed libraries.
### Principle 3: Obtain a Yield
Every element of a permaculture system should perform useful functions that
justify the energy and resources invested in it. As discussed in
[[obtain-a-yield-permaculture-principle]], a yield is not only a crop but any
beneficial output — food, fiber, fertility, knowledge, or social connection.
Systems that fail to produce a tangible return cannot sustain themselves or the
people who manage them, so this principle keeps designs grounded in practical
reality.
### Principle 4: Apply Self-Regulation and Accept Feedback
Healthy systems limit their own growth and correct imbalances through feedback
loops. [[apply-self-regulation-permaculture-principle]] emphasizes that
designers must be willing to curb excessive consumption, admit when a strategy
is not working, and respond to the signals their systems send back. This mirrors
natural ecosystems, where predator-prey dynamics and nutrient cycles maintain
stability without external management. In practice it means monitoring soil
health, plant vigor, and resource use and adjusting interventions accordingly.
### Principle 5: Use and Value Renewable Resources and Services
This principle encourages reliance on biological and cyclical resources over
finite, imported inputs. A living fence of leguminous shrubs, for example,
provides wind protection, nitrogen fixation, and forage simultaneously. Valuing
nature's services — pollination by insects, water purification by wetlands —
reduces the need for costly technological substitutes.
### Principle 6: Produce No Waste
Waste is simply a resource in the wrong place. This principle asks designers to
close loops so that the output of every process becomes the input for another.
[[produce-no-waste-permaculture-principle]] highlights composting, closed-loop
nutrient cycling, and creative repurposing as core strategies. In a well-designed
system, kitchen scraps feed chickens, chicken manure fertilizes gardens, and
garden surplus feeds the household in an unbroken cycle.
### Principle 7: Design from Patterns to Details
Before filling in specific elements, a designer steps back to understand the
large-scale patterns that shape a site. [[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-pattern-understanding-in-permaculture]]
covers branching, spiral, wave, and network patterns found throughout nature.
By recognizing these patterns — the way water flows across a slope, the
successional trajectory from field to forest — designers can place details like
swales, hedgerows, and buildings in ways that harmonize with the broader system.
### Principle 8: Integrate Rather Than Segregate
This principle promotes assembling components so that they support one another
through beneficial relationships. [[integrate-rather-than-segregate]] explains
how guilds, polycultures, and mixed land-use strategies create mutually
beneficial connections that outperform isolated monocultures. The three sisters
planting of corn, beans, and squash is a classic example: each plant provides
structural support, nitrogen fixation, or weed suppression for the others.
### Principle 9: Use Small and Slow Solutions
Large-scale interventions often carry high risk, high cost, and hidden
vulnerabilities. [[use-small-and-slow-solutions]] advocates for manageable,
decentralized approaches that build resilience over time. Starting with a small
garden bed, a single rain barrel, or a neighborhood skill-share allows a
designer to learn from mistakes at low cost before scaling up. Small solutions
are also more accessible to people with limited resources, making permaculture
more broadly applicable.
### Principle 10: Use and Value Diversity
Diverse systems are more resilient, productive, and adaptable to change.
[[use-and-value-diversity-permaculture]] explores how genetic, species, and
functional diversity create redundancy and stability in both ecological and
social systems. A polyculture that includes many crop species is less likely to
suffer total failure from a single pest or weather event than a monoculture.
Diversity also applies to livelihoods, skills, and community networks.
### Principle 11: Use Edges and Value the Marginal
The interfaces between ecosystems — forest edges, riparian zones, shorelines —
are often the most productive areas because they combine the resources of two
adjacent systems. [[use-edges-and-value-the-marginal]] encourages designers to
notice and cultivate these boundary zones. A pond edge, for instance, supports
more species per square meter than either the open water or the dry field beside
it. Socially, valuing the marginal means including overlooked voices.
### Principle 12: Creatively Use and Respond to Change
Disturbance is inevitable and this principle asks designers to treat change as
an opportunity rather than a threat. A tree blown down in a storm becomes
firewood, hugelkultur material, and a new light gap for succession. Flexibility,
adaptability, and a willingness to experiment are hallmarks of a mature
permaculture practitioner who understands that no design is ever truly finished.
Together, these 12 principles form an interconnected framework rather than a
rigid checklist. They can be applied in any order and at any scale, from a
balcony garden to a regional watershed plan. By internalizing these directives,
practitioners learn to think in systems and create designs that regenerate
rather than degrade the living world.
## Key Points
- The 12 principles were formalized by David Holmgren as practical design tools
  for sustainable living and land stewardship
- They build on the ethical foundations of earth care, people care, and fair
  share described in the permaculture literature
- Principles like [[obtain-a-yield-permaculture-principle]] and
  [[produce-no-waste-permaculture-principle]] ensure systems are productive and
  cyclical
- [[integrate-rather-than-segregate]] and [[use-and-value-diversity-permaculture]]
  emphasize relationships, redundancy, and systemic resilience
- The principles are universal guidelines, not prescriptive rules, adaptable to
  any climate, culture, or scale of design
