# Why Do Periodical Cicadas Emerge in 13- and 17-Year Cycles?

## Short Answer

Periodical cicadas (*Magicicada*) of [[polypore-fungi-eastern-north-america]] le-mushrooms-north-america]] emerge in synchronized broods on cycles of exactly **13 or 17 years** — both prime numbers. The leading evolutionary explanation is that **prime-number cycles minimize overlap with predator population cycles** and **reduce hybridization between adjacent broods**. By emerging on prime-number intervals, cicadas avoid synchronizing with predators that cycle at regular (non-prime) intervals, and they reduce the chance that two different broods will emerge in the same year. This strategy, combined with **predator satiation** (emerging in such overwhelming numbers that predators cannot eat them all), has made periodical cicadas one of the most successful and abundant insect species in eastern North America.

## How the Cycles Work

There are **15 extant broods** of periodical cicadas: 12 on 17-year cycles and 3 on 13-year cycles. Each brood is geographically distinct and emerges in a specific year. The broods are numbered with Roman numerals:

- **17-year broods**: I, II, III, V, VI, VII, VIII, IX, X, XIII, XIV, XVII
- **13-year broods**: XIX, XXII, XXIII

(Several historical brood numbers — IV, XI, XII, XVI, XVIII, XXI, XXIV through XXX — are now extinct or have been absorbed into other broods.)

In any given year, there are always multiple broods developing underground at different stages. Only the brood whose 13- or 17-year timer has elapsed emerges. Because 13 and 17 are coprime (they share no common factors), two broods can only co-emerge every **13 × 17 = 221 years**. [[permaculture-zones-in-practice]], most adjacent broods co-emerge far less frequently than this theoretical maximum.

## The Prime-Number Hypothesis

The most widely discussed explanation for the 13- and 17-year cycles is the **prime-number hypothesis**, first articulated mathematically by Stephen Jay Gould and others. The logic runs as follows:

### Avoiding Predator Synchronization

If cicadas emerged on a non-prime cycle (say, 12 or 15 years), their emergences would more frequently coincide with population peaks of predators that have 2-, 3-, 4-, or 6-year cycles. A 12-year cycle, for example, would overlap with any predator cycling at 2, 3, 4, or 6 years. But a 13-year cycle only overlaps perfectly with predators cycling at exactly 13 years — an unlikely cycle length. And a 17-year cycle only overlaps with 17-year predators.

The mathematical argument:
- A 12-year cycle shares common factors with 2, 3, 4, 6 → frequent predator overlap
- A 14-year cycle shares factors with 2, 7 → moderate overlap
- A 15-year cycle shares factors with 3, 5 → moderate overlap
- A 13-year cycle shares factors with only 1 and 13 → minimal overlap
- A 17-year cycle shares factors with only 1 and 17 → minimal overlap

### Avoiding Brood Hybridization

When two different broods emerge in the same area in the same year, they can interbreed, potentially disrupting the synchrony of their offspring. Prime-number cycles minimize the frequency of co-emergence between adjacent broods. Two 17-year broods can only co-emerge every 17 years; a 13-year and 17-year brood every 221 years. This genetic isolation helps maintain the integrity of each brood's developmental timing.

### Mathematical Modeling

[[fukuoka-textdoc-microclimate-mite-scale-population-dynamics]] models (e.g., Hoppensteadt & Keller, 1976; Hayes, 2005) have shown that:
- Periodical [[rust-fungi-biodiversity-life-cycles]] longer than ~10 years are selected for when predator populations cycle at intervals less than the cicada's period.
- Prime-number periods are evolutionarily stable once established because any mutant emerging on a non-prime cycle would suffer higher predation.
- Small advantages in survival compound over millions of years to fix prime-number cycles in the population.

## Alternative and Complementary Explanations

The prime-number hypothesis is the most famous explanation, but it is not the only one. Several complementary and alternative hypotheses have been proposed:

### Climate Synchronization During the Pleistocene

Some researchers propose that the long life cycles originated as an adaptation to glacial-interglacial climate oscillations during the Pleistocene epoch. During glacial periods, cooler summers may have been unfavorable for adult cicada activity. By extending the nymphal period to 13 or 17 years, cicadas increased the probability that at least one emergence year would coincide with a warm summer favorable for reproduction. The prime-number cycles could then have been reinforced by the predator avoidance advantages described above.

### Nymphal Development Rate

The underground development of cicada nymphs is governed by the availability of xylem sap from tree roots. Xylem sap is extremely dilute in nutrients compared to phloem sap, making cicada growth exceptionally slow. A periodical cicada nymph grows at a rate of roughly 1–2 mm per year in body length. The long life cycle may simply be a consequence of feeding on the poorest-quality food source available to any herbivorous insect. The synchronization mechanism (how billions of nymphs coordinate emergence to the same year) likely involves counting seasonal root growth cycles or soil temperature cues.

### Predator Satiation as the Primary Driver

Some researchers argue that the prime-number aspect is secondary to the more fundamental strategy of **predator satiation**. Regardless of the exact cycle length, emerging in overwhelming synchrony — billions of individuals in a few weeks — is the key adaptation. The prime numbers may be a byproduct of how the synchronization mechanism evolved rather than a directly selected trait. Under this view, 13 and 17 are simply the cycle lengths that happened to become established and are now evolutionarily stable because any shorter cycle would not provide enough time for adequate predator population decline between emergences.

## Why Not Longer or Shorter Cycles?

The constraint on cycle length reflects a balance between competing pressures:

- **Shorter cycles** (< 10 years) don't allow predator populations to decline sufficiently between emergences, reducing the effectiveness of satiation.
- **Longer cycles** (> 20 years) increase the risk of local extinction from stochastic events (drought, deforestation, disease) during the long underground period, and slow population recovery after disturbances.
- **13 and 17 years** appear to be an evolutionary "sweet spot" — long enough for predator populations to crash, short enough to maintain viable populations, and prime to minimize cycle overlap.

## The Synchronization Mystery: How Do They Count Years?

One of the deepest unsolved questions in cicada biology is the **synchronization mechanism** — how do billions of individuals, spread across thousands of square kilometers, all count 13 or 17 years and emerge in the same summer? Proposed mechanisms include:

- **Root-chemistry cue**: Nymphs may monitor seasonal changes in xylem sap composition (amino acid profiles that shift with leaf flush in spring and senescence in autumn), counting a specific number of seasonal cycles.
- **Soil temperature accumulation**: Nymphs may accumulate degree-days below a threshold, with the long cycle reflecting the enormous total degree-day requirement.
- **Endogenous molecular clock**: A gene-based timer may regulate developmental progression. Recent genomic studies have identified candidate genes involved in molting hormone (ecdysone) regulation that may underlie the long cycle.
- **Social synchronization**: Nymphs may communicate subterraneously through vibrational signals, maintaining loose synchronization. This would explain how neighboring populations remain coordinated even if individual "counting" drifts over years.

The actual mechanism likely involves a combination of these factors. Importantly, individual cicadas do not emerge at exactly 13 or 17 years — there is a spread of roughly ±1 year. But natural selection strongly penalizes early or late emergers (they are easily picked off by predators), keeping the population tightly synchronized.

## Are the Cycles Changing?

Climate change may be disrupting the stability of periodical cicada cycles:

- Some 17-year broods are producing **"straggler" emergences** 4 years early (i.e., emerging on a 13-year schedule). This has been documented in Brood X and other large broods.
- The 13-year and 17-year lineages likely originated from a common ancestor and periodically exchange genes when they co-emerge, potentially allowing cycle-switching.
- Warming soil temperatures may accelerate nymphal development, shortening the effective cycle length.
- If climate change causes widespread cycle-switching from 17 to 13 years, the genetic distinctness of 17-year broods could erode over centuries.
