---# How Do Plants Use Chemical Signals to Defend Against Insects and Communicate With Other Organisms?

## Quick Answer

Plants produce **volatile organic compounds (VOCs)** — airborne chemicals released from leaves, flowers, and roots — that serve as a sophisticated communication system. When attacked by herbivorous insects, plants release a specific blend of VOCs called **herbivore-induced plant volatiles (HIPVs)** that serve three key functions: (1) directly repelling or poisoning the attacker, (2) attracting predators and parasitoids that eat the attacker, and (3) warning neighboring plants to prepare their own defenses. This chemical language operates across the entire ecosystem, involving plants, insects, microbes, and even other plants in a network of volatile-mediated interactions.

## What Are Plant Volatile Organic Compounds?

Plant VOCs are small, airborne molecules that evaporate easily at ambient temperatures. Plants emit over 1,700 known [[mycelium-running-mushroom-antimicrobial-properties-species-chart]].

These are constitutive (always present at baseline levels) but are dramatically upregulated upon attack.

### Strategy 2: Indirect Defense (Calling for Help)

This is the most remarkable strategy. Plants release specific volatile blends that **attract the natural enemies of their attackers** — a phenomenon known as a **tritrophic interaction** (plant → herbivore → predator/parasitoid).

**How it works:**
1. A caterpillar feeds on a maize leaf, introducing its salivary FACs.
2. The maize plant responds by releasing a blend of terpenoids, indole, and GLVs.
3. A parasitic wasp (e.g., *Cotesia marginiventris*) detects this volatile blend from up to several meters away.
4. The wasp follows the chemical trail to the exact leaf where the caterpillar is feeding.
5. The wasp lays eggs inside the caterpillar; the wasp larvae consume the caterpillar from within.

This system works because the volatile blend is **specific to the herbivore species** — different caterpillar species trigger different volatile profiles, and different parasitoid species are tuned to detect different blends. It's essentially an aerial SOS system with caller ID.

**Other examples:**
- Spider mite-infested bean plants release volatiles that attract predatory mites (*Phytoseiulus persimilis*).
- Maize roots attacked by corn rootworm larvae emit **β-caryophyllene** underground, attracting entomopathogenic nematodes that kill the rootworm larvae.
- Aphid-infested plants release methyl salicylate and other compounds that attract lady beetles, lacewings, and parasitic wasps.

### Strategy 3: Warning Neighbors (Plant-to-Plant Communication)

Plants under attack emit VOCs that neighboring unattacked plants can detect and respond to:

- When **sagebrush** (*Artemisia tridentata*) is clipped, nearby wild tobacco plants upregulate their defense genes and experience less herbivore damage.
- **Lima beans** infested with spider mites emit volatiles that cause neighboring plants to pre-emptively attract predatory mites — even before the neighbors are attacked themselves.
- **Methyl salicylate** released by pathogen-infected plants can travel through the air and trigger systemic acquired resistance (SAR) in neighbors.

**Important nuance — priming vs. full activation:** Neighboring plants don't typically fully activate their defenses in response to airborne signals. Instead, they enter a state of **priming** — their defense genes are on standby, ready to mount a rapid, strong response if they are actually attacked. This is metabolically efficient because full defense deployment is costly.

The effective communication distance ranges from a few centimeters to several meters, depending on wind, volatile stability, and plant density.

## How Can Gardeners and Farmers Use This Knowledge?

### Companion Planting with VOC Principles

Many traditional companion planting combinations work through volatile-mediated effects:

- **Basil + tomato:** Basil emits linalool and eugenol, which may repel tomato hornworms and attract parasitic wasps.
- **Marigolds + vegetables:** Marigolds release thiophenes and terpenoids that repel nematodes and some above-ground pests.
- **Garlic/onions + other crops:** Sulfur volatiles mask the crop's scent, making it harder for pests to locate their host plants.
- **Dill, fennel, yarrow + vegetables:** Umbellifer flowers provide nectar for parasitic wasps, extending their lifespan and egg-laying capacity.

### Minimize Disruption of Natural Communication

- **Avoid broad-spectrum insecticides** — they kill the predatory wasps and beetles that plants are actively recruiting through their volatile signals.
- **Maintain plant diversity** — diverse plant communities support more complex volatile-mediated communication networks and more robust predator communities.
- **Allow some damage** — plants need to detect herbivore feeding to activate their VOC defenses. Completely pest-free plants don't produce the signals that recruit natural enemies.

### Pheromone-Based Pest Control

Synthetic copies of insect sex pheromones can be deployed to disrupt mating:

- **Mating disruption:** Flood an area with synthetic female sex pheromone so males can't locate real females. Effective for codling moth in apples, grape berry moth in vineyards, and many other orchard pests.
- **Pheromone traps:** Bait traps with synthetic pheromone for monitoring pest populations or mass trapping in enclosed spaces (greenhouses, grain storage).
- **Attract-and-kill:** Combine pheromone lure with a small insecticide dose or entomopathogenic fungus so attracted insects are killed.

### VOC Priming for Crop Protection

Emerging technology allows growers to use volatile compounds to prime crop defenses:

- **Methyl jasmonate sprays** can prime tomatoes, grapes, and other crops for enhanced resistance against [[coleman-beneficial-insects-and-ecological-pest-control]].
