# What Are the Best Grain Legumes for a Small Homestead?

[[grain-legumes-and-pulse-crops]] (pulses) are essential crops for any small homestead. They provide homegrown protein, fix atmospheric nitrogen for your garden, improve [[soil-structure-and-aggregate-ecology]], and fit into diverse rotation and intercropping schemes. The "best" pulse crops depend on your climate, growing season, culinary preferences, and how much space you can dedicate. This guide covers the top recommendations organized by climate zone and purpose, with practical growing advice for each.

## Quick-Start Recommendations by Climate

| Climate Zone | Top Picks | Why |
|-------------|-----------|-----|
| **Temperate (USDA 3–6)** | Fava bean, Field pea, Chickpea, Lupin | Cold-tolerant, good for spring/fall planting |
| **Mediterranean (Mild wet winters, dry summers)** | Chickpea, Fava bean, Lentil | Winter-growing, drought-escaping |
| **Subtropical (USDA 7–9)** | Cowpea, Common bean, Pigeon pea | Heat-tolerant, long warm seasons |
| **Tropical (No frost)** | Pigeon pea, Cowpea, Mung bean, Bambara groundnut | Year-round potential, heat-loving |
| **Short-season / Cold climates** | Field pea, Lentil | Fast maturity (55–90 days) |

## The Top 6 Homestead Pulse Crops

### 1. Fava Bean (Broad Bean) — *Vicia faba*

**Best for**: Temperate and Mediterranean climates, heavy soils, maximum nitrogen fixation.

Fava beans are arguably the most useful pulse for a small homestead. They tolerate heavier clay soils than any other grain legume, survive frosts to -10°C, and fix 150–300 kg N/ha — the highest of any cool-season pulse. Their large seeds are easy to harvest and shell by hand.

**Growing Tips**:
- Sow in early spring (4–6 weeks before last frost) or fall in mild climates for winter production
- Space 15–20 cm apart in rows 45–60 cm apart
- Provide support for tall varieties (stake or netting)
- Pinch growing tips when first flowers appear to deter blackfly aphids
- Harvest when pods swell but before seeds harden (fresh eating) or when pods dry and blacken (dry storage)
- Chop stems at ground level after harvest and leave roots in place — they're loaded with fixed nitrogen
**Yield Expectation**: 1.5–3 kg per 10 m row (dry seed)
**Storage**: Dry seeds store 2–3 years in airtight containers. Fresh favas freeze well after blanching.

### 2. Chickpea (Garbanzo Bean) — *Cicer arietinum*

**Best for**: Mediterranean and semi-arid climates, drought conditions, culinary versatility.

Chickpeas are the homesteader's gateway to Middle Eastern and Indian cooking — hummus, falafel, chana masala, and roasted chickpea snacks. The desi types (small, dark seeds) are more [[drought-tolerant-planting-design]]; kabuli types (large, cream seeds) have higher yields and are easier to process.

**Growing Tips**:
- Sow 2–3 weeks before last frost (they need cool weather to set pods; heat during flowering causes flower drop)
- Direct sow only — chickpeas dislike transplanting
- Space 5–8 cm apart in rows 30–45 cm apart
- Very drought-tolerant once established; avoid irrigation after flowering in humid climates (promotes *Ascochyta* blight)
- Harvest when plants dry down and pods turn golden-brown (typically 90–120 days)
- Desi types are easier to grow in marginal conditions; kabuli types need better soil and more consistent moisture

**Yield Expectation**: 0.5–1.5 kg per 10 m row (dry seed)
**Storage**: Excellent — dry chickpeas store 3+ years. Cook and freeze in batches for convenience.

### 3. Dry Pea — *Pisum sativum*

**Best for**: Cold climates, short seasons, easy growing, versatile use.

Dry peas are among the easiest grain legumes to grow. Their short season (80–100 days), reliable performance, and broad adaptation make them a low-risk choice for beginning homesteaders. Yellow peas are the workhorse for soups and split pea production; green peas command premium prices.

**Growing Tips**:
- Sow as early as soil can be worked (4–6 weeks before last frost)
- Inoculate with pea-specific rhizobia for best nitrogen fixation
- Provide a trellis or support for semi-leafless varieties
- Powdery mildew is the main disease concern — ensure good air circulation and choose resistant varieties
- Harvest when pods are tan and dry; vines should be brown and brittle
- Thresh by flailing dried plants in a tarp and winnow with a fan

**Yield Expectation**: 1–2.5 kg per 10 m row (dry seed)
**Storage**: Split peas store 2–3 years dry. Cooked peas freeze well for quick meals.

### 4. Cowpea (Black-eyed Pea) — *Vigna unguiculata*

**Best for**: Hot climates, poor soils, intercropping, dual-purpose (grain + greens).

Cowpea is the homestead pulse for tough conditions. Where other crops wilt and fail in summer heat, cowpea thrives. The leaves are also edible as a nutritious cooked green — giving you two harvests from one planting.

**Growing Tips**:
- Sow after soil has warmed to at least 18°C (2–3 weeks after last frost)
- Direct sow 5–8 cm apart; thin to 15–20 cm
- Extremely drought-tolerant — water only during flowering and pod fill if rainfall is inadequate
- No inoculation needed — cowpea nodulates freely with native soil bacteria
- Harvest dry pods as they mature (pick every few days to avoid bruchid beetle damage)
- Young leaves can be harvested throughout the season for cooked greens — don't remove more than 30% of foliage

**Yield Expectation**: 0.8–2 kg per 10 m row (dry seed) + significant leaf harvest
**Storage**: Dry seeds store 1–2 years. Freeze or use hermetic storage to control bruchid beetles.

### 5. Common Dry Bean — *Phaseolus vulgaris*

**Best for**: Culinary variety, temperate to subtropical climates, [[seed-saving-seed-resources-mollison]].

Common beans offer the greatest culinary diversity of any pulse — kidney, navy, pinto, black, cannellini, cranberry, and hundreds of heirloom varieties. For the homesteader interested in food preservation, dry beans are calorie-dense, easy to store, and culturally central to cuisines worldwide.

**Growing Tips**:
- Sow after all frost danger has passed and soil is warm (18°C+)
- Bush types: 5 cm apart in rows 45–60 cm apart; Pole types: 8–10 cm at base of trellis or corn stalks
- Pole beans produce 2–3× the yield of bush types but require support and a longer season
- Dry beans need warm, dry conditions at maturity — avoid overhead watering late in the season
- Pull entire plants when 90% of pods are dry and finish drying under cover
- Shell by hand or thresh in a bag/bucket; winnow to clean

**Yield Expectation**: 0.5–1.5 kg per 10 m row (bush); 1–3 kg per 10 m row (pole)
**Storage**: Excellent — dry beans store 3–5 years. One of the longest-storing staple foods.

### 6. Pigeon Pea — *Cajanus cajan*

**Best for**: Frost-free climates, perennial production, food forest integration, edible hedge.

Pigeon pea is unique among grain legumes as a woody perennial shrub that produces for 2–5 years from a single planting. It functions simultaneously as a food crop, nitrogen fixer, windbreak, animal fodder producer, and chop-and-drop mulch source.

**Growing Tips**:
- Sow after all frost danger has passed; requires 6+ month frost-free growing season
- Transplant seedlings or direct sow (direct sowing is more common)
- Space 60–100 cm apart — mature plants are large (1.5–3 m tall)
- Flowers and pods over an extended period; pick dry pods regularly
- Prune annually after harvest to maintain productivity and generate mulch
- Intercrop with cereals, fruit trees, or vegetables as a productive living support

**Yield Expectation**: 1–3 kg per plant per year (dry seed), plus significant prunings for mulch
**Storage**: Dry pigeon peas (toor dal) store 2–3 years. Split pigeon peas are the basis of sambar and dal across South Asia.
