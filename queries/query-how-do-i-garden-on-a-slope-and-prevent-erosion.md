# How Do I Garden on a Slope and Prevent Erosion?

## Short Answer

Gardening on a slope requires slowing water runoff, building soil in place, and choosing techniques that work with gravity rather than against it.
The main strategies include [[contour-line-water-management]], [[mollison-designers-earthworks-for-erosion-control|Erosion control]] is not optional on slopes — without it, rain strips topsoil, exposes roots, and creates gullies that worsen over time.

## Detailed Explanation

### Why Slopes Are Challenging

Water moves downhill fast on an uncovered slope, carrying soil particles and dissolved nutrients with it.
A 10 percent grade can lose five times more topsoil per rainfall event than flat ground.
Slopes also dry out faster because water has less time to infiltrate before running off.
Wind exposure increases with elevation, compounding desiccation on hillside sites.
The steeper the slope, the more aggressive your interventions need to be.
Despite these challenges, slopes offer unique advantages: excellent air drainage reduces frost risk, and south-facing grades capture more solar energy than flat ground.

### Start With Observation and Contour

Before building anything, observe how water moves across your slope during and after rain.
Mark the contour lines of your land using an A-frame level or a laser level.
[[mollison-designers-swale-design-and-water-harvesting-earthworks|swale]] is a level ditch excavated along the contour that captures runoff and lets it soak into the soil below.
Swales are ideal for moderate slopes (5 to 25 percent grade) where you want to rehydrate the land.
The excavated soil is mounded on the downhill side to form a berm, which is planted with perennial crops or cover crops.
[[holzer-terrace-construction-guide|terraces]] are a safer choice.

### Terracing for Steep Slopes

[[mollison-designers-tropical-terrace-design-and-earth-shaping|Tropical terrace designs]] often incorporate stone walls that double as heat sinks and habitat for beneficial organisms.
Terraces eliminate the runoff problem almost entirely because each level is functionally flat.
The tradeoff is higher upfront labor and cost compared to swales or contour beds.

### Hugelkultur on Slopes

[[comparison-hugelkultur-vs-terracing|Compared to terraces]], hugelkultur is cheaper and faster to build but less permanent.
The decomposing wood feeds soil biology for years, improving both fertility and water-holding capacity over time.
Hugel beds work best on moderate slopes where the mound height naturally conforms to the grade.

### Sheet Mulching and Ground Covers

Bare soil on a slope is an open invitation to erosion, so covering the ground is essential.
[[cover-crop|cover crops]] such as clover, vetch, or rye immediately after shaping earthworks to anchor the soil with living roots.
[[chop-and-drop-mulching]] allows you to recycle biomass from support species directly onto the slope surface.

### What About Small or Urban Slopes?

Not every slope needs full-scale earthworks — many residential hillside gardens succeed with simpler methods.
Retaining walls made from stone, timber, or gabions create flat planting pockets without reshaping the entire hillside.
[[mollison-soil-formation-rates-and-erosion-costs|Soil formation is slow]] — losing it to a landslide takes seconds, but rebuilding it takes centuries.
French drains and carefully placed outlet channels can safely move excess water away from vulnerable areas without concentrating flow.

### What About Maintenance Over Time?

Slope gardens require ongoing attention to ensure earthworks continue functioning as intended.
Inspect swales and terraces after major storm events for signs of breaching, scouring, or sediment accumulation.
Replant bare spots on berms and terrace edges immediately — even a small gap can become an erosion channel quickly.
Refresh mulch layers annually, especially on south-facing slopes where decomposition is faster due to sun exposure.
As perennial plantings mature, their root networks progressively stabilize the slope, reducing maintenance needs over the years.

## Key Points

- Observe water flow and mark contour lines before building anything on a slope.
- [[holzer-terraced-landscape-design|Terraces]] are the most robust solution for steep slopes above 25 percent grade.
- [[sheet-mulching|mulch]] or living ground covers.
- [[backyard-water-harvesting|Rainwater harvesting]] above the slope feeds swales and terraces by gravity.
- [[bioswales|Bioswales]] are a landscaped alternative to standard swales that combine water management with aesthetic plantings.
