## Overview
Compost tea and vermicompost tea are both liquid extracts used as organic soil
amendments, but they originate from different source materials and offer distinct
benefits. Understanding their differences helps growers choose the right product
for specific crops and soil conditions. Both are considered active aerated
compost teas (AACT) when brewed with aeration and food sources. For formal
definitions, see [[aact-definitions-and-types-ingham]].
## Source Material
The fundamental distinction lies in the starting material. [[compost-tea-aact]]
is brewed from traditional thermophilic compost produced through microbial
decomposition of organic matter in hot piles that reach 130 to 160 degrees
Fahrenheit. This process creates a diverse community of bacteria, fungi,
protozoa, and nematodes. [[vermicompost-tea]], on the other hand, is brewed
from vermicompost, which is the product of earthworm digestion of organic
material. See [[vermicomposting]] for details on how worm castings are produced.
The [[composting-methods-comparison]] page outlines the broader differences
between thermophilic composting and vermicomposting approaches.
## What is Compost Tea?
Compost tea is an aerobic extract made by suspending finished compost in water
and actively aerating it with dissolved nutrients to feed microbial growth.
It serves as a concentrated delivery system for the diverse microbiology
present in quality compost. The quality of the finished tea depends heavily
on the quality and maturity of the source compost. Immature compost can
introduce pathogens or phytotoxic compounds into the brew, so sourcing
well-cured compost is essential.
## What is Vermicompost Tea?
Vermicompost tea follows the same brewing principle but uses worm castings
as the base ingredient. Vermicompost tends to be richer in certain nutrients
and humic acids compared to conventional compost, which carries through into
the brewed tea. The worm gut selectively promotes beneficial bacteria such
as Pseudomonas, Bacillus, and Azotobacter species, giving vermicompost tea
a unique microbial signature.
## Microbial Population Differences
The microbial communities in each tea differ significantly due to their source
materials. Understanding these differences is key to selecting the right tea
for a given application.
### Bacterial Dominance
Both teas are generally bacterial-dominant when brewed using standard methods.
However, vermicompost tea often contains higher total bacterial counts because
the worm gut acts as an incubator for beneficial bacteria. Studies have shown
that vermicompost can host bacterial populations orders of magnitude higher
than traditional compost. For targeted bacterial-dominant brewing strategies,
see [[aact-bacterial-dominant-tea]].
### Fungal Populations
Traditional compost, especially when made with woody materials, tends to
support more diverse fungal communities. If fungal dominance is the goal,
compost tea brewed from fungal-rich compost may outperform vermicompost tea.
Fungi are critical for woody perennials and soils that need improved aggregate
structure. See [[aact-fungal-dominant-tea]] for brewing techniques that favor
fungi, including the use of fungal food sources like oatmeal, fish hydrolysate,
and humic acids.
### Key Microbial Differences
- Vermicompost tea typically has higher concentrations of plant
  growth-promoting rhizobacteria (PGPR).
- Compost tea often contains more diverse fungal species, including beneficial
  Trichoderma strains.
- Vermicompost tea may carry higher levels of humic and fulvic acids, which
  support microbial colonization in soil.
- Compost tea from mature, woody compost can provide more saprophytic fungi
  beneficial for woody plant care.
- Protozoan populations tend to be more varied in compost tea, aiding in
  nutrient cycling through the bacterial loop.
## Nutrient Profiles
Vermicompost tea generally provides higher concentrations of plant-available
nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium compared to compost tea. The worm
digestion process partially mineralizes nutrients, making them more
immediately available to plants. Vermicompost tea also contains plant growth
hormones such as cytokinins and auxins produced by the worm gut microbiome.
Compost tea offers a broader but sometimes less immediately available nutrient
spectrum, with more complex organic compounds that release slowly over time.
## Brewing Methods
The brewing process for both teas follows similar principles outlined in
[[compost-tea-brewing]]. Key considerations include:
- Aeration is critical for both teas to maintain dissolved oxygen levels
  above 6 ppm and prevent anaerobic conditions that can produce toxins.
- Food sources such as molasses, fish hydrolysate, and kelp are added to
  feed microbial multiplication during the brew cycle.
- Standard brew times range from 24 to 36 hours depending on the recipe
  and target biology.
- Water quality matters: use dechlorinated water to avoid killing beneficial
  microbes before brewing begins.
- Compost tea recipes targeting fungi require longer brew times and specific
  food sources compared to bacterial-dominant recipes.
## Application Rates
Both teas are typically applied as soil drenches or foliar sprays. The
following guidelines apply to most situations:
- Soil drench: Apply at 10 to 20 gallons per acre, diluted if needed for
  sensitive plants or young seedlings.
- Foliar spray: Apply undiluted or slightly diluted, ensuring thorough
  coverage on leaf surfaces for direct microbial benefits.
- Frequency: Every 2 to 4 weeks during the growing season for optimal
  results, or more frequently for stressed plants.
- Apply in the early morning or late evening to minimize UV damage to
  living organisms on leaf surfaces.
## When to Use Compost Tea
- When fungal diversity is needed for woody perennials, orchards, or trees.
- When the goal is broad-spectrum microbial inoculation from a diverse
  compost source.
- When treating soil that needs a balanced bacterial and fungal community
  for long-term soil health.
- When working with established perennial systems that benefit from fungal
  associations with plant roots.
## When to Use Vermicompost Tea
- When higher plant-available nutrient levels are desired for fast-growing
  annuals and vegetables.
- When targeting bacterial-dominant inoculation for vegetable gardens or
  row crops.
- When humic acid content is a priority for soil structure improvement.
- When working with compacted or degraded soils that benefit from the humic
  substances found abundantly in worm castings.
- When plant growth hormones from vermicompost can give seedlings a
  germination and establishment advantage.
## Summary
While both [[compost-tea-aact]] and [[vermicompost-tea]] are aerobic brewed
extracts, they serve complementary roles in a soil health management program.
Compost tea excels where fungal diversity and broad-spectrum biology are
needed, making it ideal for perennials and orchard systems. Vermicompost tea
delivers stronger bacterial populations, more readily available nutrients, and
valuable plant growth hormones. Choosing between them depends on the crop type,
current soil condition, and the specific biological targets of the grower.
Many integrated programs alternate between the two teas to provide the widest
range of benefits throughout the growing season.
