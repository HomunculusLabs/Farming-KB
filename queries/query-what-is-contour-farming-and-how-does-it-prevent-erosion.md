# What is contour farming and how does it prevent erosion?

## Short Answer

Contour farming is an agricultural practice where crops are planted and tillage operations follow the natural contour lines of sloping land rather than running up and down the slope.
This perpendicular arrangement to the slope creates natural barriers that slow water runoff, allowing it to infiltrate into the soil rather than carrying topsoil downhill.
It is one of the oldest and most cost-effective erosion control strategies, forming the foundation for more advanced systems like [[keyline-design]] and [[contour-swale-irrigation-design]].
Contour farming dates back thousands of years, with evidence of contour terracing in ancient Phoenician, Roman, and Incan agricultural systems.

## How Contour Farming Works

When rain falls on a sloped field plowed up and down, water gains speed as it travels downhill, increasing its erosive power exponentially.
Each centimeter per second of additional water velocity roughly doubles the particle size a runoff stream can transport.
Contour farming interrupts this acceleration by creating a series of miniature dams along the slope.
The crop rows, furrows, and ridges built on contour catch and hold water long enough for it to soak into the ground.
This simple reorientation of planting rows can reduce soil loss by 50 to 80 percent compared to up-and-down slope tillage.

## Surveying and Marking Contour Lines

Accurate contour lines are essential for the system to function properly.
The traditional tool is the [[a-frame-level]], a simple wooden A-shaped device with a plumb bob that costs almost nothing to build.
More precise surveying uses a [[bunyip-level]], which is a water-filled tube that finds exact level over long distances.
Bill Mollison's designers manual covers contour surveying techniques in [[mollison-designers-contour-surveying-a-frame-bunyip-level]].
Modern farmers sometimes use laser levels or GPS-guided equipment for large-scale operations.
The key principle is to mark contour lines at regular vertical intervals, typically 1 to 2 meters of elevation drop between lines depending on slope steepness and soil type.

## Contour Farming vs Keyline Design

While contour farming follows natural contour lines, [[comparison-keyline-vs-contour|keyline design]] is a refinement that connects contour lines along their natural watershed pattern.
Keyline design, developed by P.A. Yeomans, identifies the "keypoint" where a slope transitions from steep to gentle and uses that as the basis for an integrated water management plan.
[[keyline-design]] optimizes water distribution across the entire landscape rather than just preventing erosion on individual slope segments.
Both approaches work together: contour farming is the practical on-ground technique, while keyline provides the strategic landscape-level plan.
The [[comparison-keyline-vs-contour-swale|comparison between keyline and contour swale]] approaches shows how each suits different property sizes and goals.

## Integration with Swales and Earthworks

Contour farming pairs naturally with earthworks that enhance water retention.
A [[swale]] is a level ditch dug on contour that intercepts runoff and spreads it along the slope.
[[comparison-swale-vs-contour-line-water-management|Contour swales and contour line water management]] differ mainly in scale: swales are deeper earthworks while contour farming uses shallow furrows and ridges.
[[earthworks-for-water-conservation]] describes how graded channels, check dams, and contour banks work together as a system.
For steeper terrain, [[banks-benches-and-terracing]] provides more substantial earthworks that create flat planting benches on hillsides.
The [[contour-dam-mollison|Mollison contour dam]] is another structure that combines contour principles with water storage for dry-season irrigation.

## Benefits Beyond Erosion Control

Contour farming does far more than just prevent soil loss.
The increased water infiltration raises soil moisture levels, reducing irrigation needs during dry periods.
Higher moisture retention supports soil microbial communities and mycorrhizal networks that improve nutrient cycling.
[[natural-farming-guide-contour-farming-strip-cropping|Natural farming approaches to contour farming and strip cropping]] emphasize how contour rows support beneficial insect habitat and reduce pest pressure.
The practice also reduces fertilizer runoff into waterways, protecting downstream ecosystems.
On pastoral land, contour furrows can increase forage production by 30 to 50 percent by retaining rainfall that would otherwise be lost as surface runoff.

## What About Strip Cropping on Contour?

Strip cropping is a companion practice where different crops or crop and fallow strips alternate along contour lines.
[[intercropping-systems|Intercropping systems]] on contour combine erosion control with the biodiversity benefits of polyculture.
[[mollison-designers-alley-cropping-and-contour-hedgerow-systems|Alley cropping and contour hedgerow systems]] use permanent woody rows on contour with crop alleys between them.
Strip widths depend on slope gradient: steeper slopes need narrower strips with more erosion-resistant crops in the critical rows.
A common pattern places grass or legume strips every 20 to 40 meters on moderate slopes, with row crops in between.
This combination of contour alignment and vegetative barriers provides double protection against both water and wind erosion.

## What About Contour Farming in Arid Climates?

In arid and semi-arid regions, contour farming shifts from erosion prevention to water harvesting.
[[desert-basins-pans-scalds-and-gully-erosion|Desert basin and gully erosion patterns]] show how even rare rainfall events can devastate unprotected land.
[[halting-and-absorbing-water-runoff|Halting and absorbing water runoff]] becomes critical when rain falls in intense, infrequent bursts.
[[infiltration-slope-stabilisation-and-floodwater-harvesting|Infiltration, slope stabilisation, and floodwater harvesting]] techniques use contour structures to turn destructive runoff into productive soil moisture.
[[earth-shaping-and-water-management-in-the-tropics|Earth shaping and water management]] in tropical systems applies similar principles for high-rainfall environments where runoff velocity is extreme.
Contour micro-catchments in arid zones can concentrate enough moisture to support tree establishment in areas receiving as little as 200mm annual rainfall.
Even a simple contour bund 30cm high can capture and infiltrate runoff from a storm delivering 50mm of rain in an hour.
The captured moisture creates a green belt of vegetation upslope of the bund that persists weeks longer than surrounding bare ground.

## What About Contour Farming for Different Soil Types?

Soil texture determines how closely contour structures must be spaced and how deep they need to be.
Sandy soils infiltrate quickly but hold little moisture, requiring deeper swales and closer contour spacing to capture enough water.
Clay soils retain water well but infiltrate slowly, meaning contour ridges must be taller to prevent water from overtopping during intense storms.
Loam soils are the easiest to work with, allowing wider contour spacing and shallower structures.
Highly erodible silty soils benefit from narrow strip cropping on contour combined with permanent grass buffer strips.
[[earthworks-for-water-conservation]] techniques vary the cross-section of contour channels depending on whether the goal is infiltration, conveyance, or storage.
Rocky soils may require hand-built stone contour walls rather than earthen ridges, similar to traditional agricultural terracing found across the Mediterranean and Andean regions.
The [[comparison-terracing-vs-swale|comparison of terracing versus swales]] shows how soil depth influences whether terracing or contour swaling is the better approach for steep land.

## What About Maintenance and Common Failures?

Contour farming systems require regular maintenance to function effectively over time.
Furrows and ridges can degrade after heavy storms, needing regrading to maintain their water-holding capacity.
Contour lines should be re-surveyed every few years because tillage operations and soil settling gradually shift the land surface.
A common failure mode is installing contour structures too far apart for the slope gradient, which allows runoff to build destructive velocity between the lines.
Another frequent mistake is running rows slightly off contour, which channels water into concentrated flow paths rather than spreading it evenly.
[[earthworks-for-water-management]] guidance recommends inspecting contour systems after every significant rainfall event during the first year.
Once vegetation establishes along contour rows and soil structure improves from increased organic matter, the system becomes largely self-maintaining.
The transition period typically takes two to three growing seasons, during which light repair work preserves the initial investment.

## Key Points

- Contour farming aligns crop rows and tillage with natural land contours to slow water and reduce erosion by 50 to 80 percent.
- Surveying tools like the [[a-frame-level]] and [[bunyip-level]] are used to mark accurate contour lines before planting or earthwork begins.
- [[keyline-design]] refines contour farming by planning water movement across entire watersheds rather than individual slopes.
- Combining contour farming with a [[swale]] or [[bioswales]] dramatically increases water infiltration and soil moisture.
- [[earthworks-for-water-conservation]] including contour banks and [[check-dam-mollison|check dams]] provide additional erosion protection on steep terrain.
- Strip cropping and [[intercropping-systems]] on contour add biodiversity benefits to the erosion control function.
- In arid regions, contour structures shift focus from erosion prevention to water harvesting for tree establishment and crop production.
- The practice integrates naturally with permaculture design principles described in [[contour-line-water-management]] and related earthworks systems.
- Soil type determines contour spacing: sandy soils need closer spacing, clay soils need taller ridges, and rocky soils may require stone walls.
- Expect a two to three season transition period before the system becomes largely self-maintaining with minimal repair work needed.
