# What Are the Best Perennial Vegetables for a Food Forest

## Short Answer
The best perennial vegetables for food forests combine reliable productivity,
low maintenance requirements, and compatibility with polyculture systems. Top
choices include asparagus, Jerusalem artichokes, walking onions, sea kale,
perennial kale, sorrel, Good King Henry, and groundcover berries. These
plants return year after year without replanting, build soil carbon, and
produce food with minimal inputs once they are fully established.

## Detailed Explanation
Perennial vegetables are the backbone of any productive food forest or
edible landscape. Unlike annual vegetables that must be planted, tended, and
harvested each year, perennial vegetables establish permanent root systems
that become more productive over time. They protect and build soil, require
less water and fertilizer than annuals, and provide harvests earlier in
spring and later in fall than most annual crops. The
[[comparison-perennial-vegetables-vs-cover-crops]] provides a comprehensive
overview of species suited to various climates and growing conditions.

Asparagus is arguably the most popular and widely grown perennial vegetable,
and for good reason. A well-maintained asparagus bed can produce for
fifteen to twenty years or more with minimal ongoing care.
[[hamilton-asparagus-growing-organic]] requires
patience, as plants should not be harvested for the first two to three
years while the crown develops an extensive root system. Once established,
asparagus provides one of the earliest spring harvests available, with
tender spears emerging as soon as the soil warms. All-male varieties like
Jersey Giant produce heavier yields than traditional mixed-sex plantings
because they do not divert energy into seed production.

Jerusalem artichokes, also called sunchokes, are among the most productive
perennial vegetables, capable of producing enormous yields of tubers from a
single planting. The [[permaculture-jerusalem-artichoke]] guide covers how to use these vigorous plants
effectively in food forest designs. They grow six to ten feet tall, produce
attractive sunflower-like blooms, and the tubers can be harvested from fall
through spring. Their main drawback is that they can become aggressively
invasive, so containment strategies like rhizome barriers or dedicated beds
are important for preventing them from overtaking other plantings.

Perennial kales and collards are relatively unknown but extremely valuable
for food forest systems. Varieties like Daubenton's kale, Taunton Deane
kale, and tree collards produce edible leaves year-round in mild climates
and for nine to ten months in colder zones. They are propagated by cuttings
rather than seed and can live for many years, providing a reliable source of
nutritious greens throughout the seasons. Their tall, branching growth habit
makes them suitable for the understory layer of a food forest beneath fruit
trees and larger canopy specimens.

Alliums offer several excellent perennial options for the food forest.
Walking onions, also called Egyptian walking onions or topsetting onions,
produce bulbils at the top of their stalks that bend over and plant
themselves, gradually walking across the garden over multiple seasons. The
base clump can be harvested as green onions or allowed to produce topsets
for replanting. [[solomon-growing-garlic-onions-leeks]] and perennial leeks also
have valuable spots in the food forest, providing aromatic allium flavor
with minimal replanting effort from year to year.

Leafy perennial greens round out the food forest vegetable layer
beautifully. Sorrel produces lemony-tasting leaves from early spring through
late fall and thrives in partial shade beneath taller plants. Good King
Henry, a traditional European pot herb, produces edible leaves, flower buds,
and seeds for multiple harvests. Sea kale, a coastal native, produces
blanchable shoots similar to asparagus in early spring. The
[[hemenway-perennial-vegetables]]
integrates these species into polyculture designs where they fill specific
niches in time, space, and function within the food forest ecosystem.

Groundcover perennial vegetables fill the lowest layer of the food forest,
suppressing weeds, building soil, and providing additional harvests from
areas that would otherwise be unproductive. Alpine strawberries produce
small but intensely flavored berries from spring through fall with minimal
maintenance. Nasturtiums provide edible leaves, flowers, and seed pods while
acting as a trap crop for aphids and other pests. Creeping thyme provides
culinary herbs while serving as a living mulch between larger plantings. The
[[perennial-food-forests]] guide demonstrates how to
layer these ground covers with taller perennials and canopy trees for
maximum productivity per square foot.
