## How to Make Herbal Salves and Balms at Home
Herbal salves and balms are semi-solid preparations made by infusing herbs into a carrier oil and then combining that oil with beeswax to create a spreadable, shelf-stable topical remedy. They are one of the oldest and most practical forms of herbal medicine, used for everything from soothing burns and scrapes to relieving muscle aches and dry skin.
## What Is the Difference Between a Salve and a Balm?
The terms are often used interchangeably, but there are subtle distinctions:
- **Salve**: A softer preparation, typically 10-20% beeswax by weight. Softer consistency, easier to spread, ideal for general skin care and minor wounds.
- **Balm**: A firmer preparation, typically 20-30% beeswax by weight. Holds its shape better in tins or tubes, good for lip balms, muscle rubs, and products that need to travel.
- **Ointment**: Generally oil-based without water, similar to a salve. The term is more common in pharmaceutical contexts.
The ratio of beeswax to oil is the primary variable. More wax yields a harder product; less wax yields a softer one. A good starting point is 1 part beeswax to 4 parts infused oil (20% wax) for a standard salve.

### Beeswax
Beeswax is the thickener that turns oil into a salve. Use **filtered cosmetic-grade beeswax pellets** for easiest measuring. Yellow beeswax has a mild honey scent; white beeswax is filtered and more neutral. One ounce of beeswax firms up roughly four ounces of oil.
**Plant-based alternatives** include candelilla wax (harder than beeswax, use about half the amount) and carnauba wax (very hard, use sparingly). Soy wax is softer and less suitable for salves.

## Step 1: Make an Herbal-Infused Oil
The quality of your salve depends entirely on the infused oil. There are two main methods:
### Solar or Cold Infusion (Best for Delicate Herbs)
1. Fill a clean glass jar 1/2 to 2/3 full with **dried** herbs.
2. Cover the herbs completely with carrier oil, leaving about 1 inch of headspace.
3. Stir to release air bubbles and ensure all plant material is submerged.
4. Cap tightly and place in a sunny windowsill for **4 to 6 weeks**, shaking daily.
5. Strain through cheesecloth, squeezing the plant material to extract all oil.
This gentle method preserves heat-sensitive compounds. It works well for calendula, chamomile, lavender, and St. John's wort.
### Heat Infusion (Faster Method)
1. Combine herbs and oil in a double boiler over low heat (do not exceed 120°F / 49°C).
2. Simmer gently for **2 to 4 hours**, checking occasionally that the oil still covers the herbs.
3. Remove from heat, let cool slightly, then strain through cheesecloth.
