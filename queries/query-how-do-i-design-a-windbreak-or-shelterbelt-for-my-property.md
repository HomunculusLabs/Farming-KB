# How Do I Design a Windbreak or Shelterbelt for My Property?

## Short Answer
A windbreak is a linear planting of trees and shrubs designed to reduce wind speed
and protect crops, livestock, and buildings. Effective design requires selecting
the right species mix in multiple staggered rows, positioning the windbreak
perpendicular to prevailing winds at the correct distance, and integrating
multiple functions like food production and wildlife habitat.

## Detailed Explanation

Wind causes more crop damage and soil erosion than most growers realize. It
increases evapotranspiration, desiccates young transplants, physically damages
foliage and branches, blows away valuable topsoil and mulch, and increases
heating costs for nearby structures. The [[windbreak-and-shelterbelt-design]]
guide provides the foundational principles for designing plantings that reduce
wind speed by 40 to 60 percent on the leeward side for a distance of 10 to 15
times the height of the windbreak itself.

The first step in design is identifying your prevailing wind direction. The
[[bill-mollison-permaculture-a-designers-manual-zone-and-sector-analysis]]
framework uses sector analysis to map the directions from which wind, sun, fire,
noise, and other forces arrive at your site. For most properties in temperate
North American climates, the strongest winds come from the northwest in winter
and the southwest in summer. Your windbreak should be positioned perpendicular
to these prevailing winds, on the windward side of the areas you want to
protect. Local airport weather data and online wind rose tools can provide
precise wind direction frequency data for your specific location.

The [[comparison-windbreak-vs-hedgerow]] page distinguishes between windbreaks,
which are primarily functional plantings designed for maximum wind reduction, and
hedgerows, which serve additional functions like livestock fencing, wildlife
habitat corridors, and forage production. Many designs combine both functions
seamlessly, but understanding the distinction helps you prioritize species
selection and row spacing. A windbreak designed solely for wind reduction uses
denser planting than a hedgerow that also needs to function as a fence line.

Species selection follows a layered approach for maximum effectiveness. The
[[windbreak-shelterbelt-species-selection]] reference recommends a minimum of
three rows for effective wind reduction, with five to seven rows for large-scale
agricultural applications. The windward row should consist of dense, low-growing,
coniferous or evergreen species that intercept wind at ground level and prevent
it from curling under the canopy. The middle row should contain tall, fast-
growing deciduous trees that create the main wind-deflecting canopy. The leeward
row can include a mix of fruiting and nut-bearing species that provide food
production alongside wind protection.

For permaculture properties, the [[gaias-garden-hedgerows-and-windbreaks]]
approach by Toby Hemenway integrates multi-functional species throughout the
windbreak. Instead of a single-purpose planting, choose species that also provide
nitrogen fixation (black locust, sea buckthorn, autumn olive), wildlife habitat
(native berry shrubs like elderberry and serviceberry), forage for livestock
(mulberry, honey locust, caragana), and timber or coppice (oak, black walnut,
hazel). The [[mollison-designers-casuarina-windbreak-erosion-control]] design
uses Casuarina species in tropical climates for rapid windbreak establishment
combined with erosion control on slopes and riparian margins.

Spacing within the windbreak significantly affects its performance. Trees in
the main canopy row should be spaced 6 to 12 feet apart depending on species
mature width, with rows spaced 10 to 15 feet apart. Stagger the rows so that
plants in one row fill the gaps in the adjacent row, creating a permeable rather
than solid barrier. Counterintuitively, a windbreak that is 40 to 60 percent
permeable reduces wind speed more effectively than a solid wall, because a solid
barrier creates damaging turbulence on the leeward side that can actually
increase crop damage close to the barrier.

The [[faires-zone-and-sector-planning]] guide recommends placing windbreaks at a
distance of 4 to 6 times the mature height of the trees from the area being
protected. For a windbreak of 40-foot-tall trees, this means positioning it 160
to 240 feet upwind of your garden. If space is limited, a shorter, denser
windbreak closer to the garden is far better than no windbreak at all, as even
partial wind reduction provides measurable benefits for crop growth and soil
moisture conservation.

The [[hemenway-hedgerow-and-shelterbelt-design]] emphasizes that windbreaks also
create valuable microclimates. The sheltered zone behind a windbreak is several
degrees warmer in winter due to reduced wind chill and less convective heat loss,
which extends the growing season for tender crops. Windbreaks also reduce soil
moisture loss, which is especially valuable in arid climates where the
[[arid-zone-permaculture-strategies-mollison]] approach uses windbreaks as a
primary water conservation tool alongside swales and keyline design.

## Key Points
- Windbreaks reduce wind speed by 40 to 60 percent for a distance of 10 to 15
  times their height on the leeward side of the planting
- Position windbreaks perpendicular to prevailing winds identified through
  sector analysis and local wind rose data for your property
- Use at least three staggered rows: dense evergreens windward, tall canopy
  trees in the middle, and productive species on the leeward side
- A windbreak that is 40 to 60 percent permeable reduces wind more effectively
  than a solid barrier due to reduced leeward turbulence
- Integrate multi-functional species for nitrogen fixation, wildlife habitat,
  livestock forage, and food production alongside wind protection
