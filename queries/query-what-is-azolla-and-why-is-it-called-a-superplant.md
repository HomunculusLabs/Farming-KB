# What Is Azolla and Why Is It Called a Superplant?

## Short Answer

Azolla is a tiny free-floating aquatic fern that grows on the surface of still or slow-moving freshwater.

It forms a unique symbiotic relationship with a cyanobacterium called Nostoc (formerly Anabaena) azollae that lives inside its leaf cavities.

This partnership allows Azolla to fix atmospheric nitrogen at rates that rival commercial fertilizers, earning it the nickname "superplant."

Farmers across Asia have used Azolla as a green manure in rice paddies for centuries, and modern agriculture is rediscovering its potential as a biofertilizer, livestock feed, and [[mycorrhizal-fungi-soil-carbon-sequestration-by-type]] tool.

## What Exactly Is Azolla?

Azolla belongs to the family Salviniaceae and is one of the fastest-growing plants on Earth.

Under ideal conditions it can double its biomass in just two to three days.

The two most widely cultivated species are [[azolla-caroliniana]], native to the Americas, and [[azolla-filiculoides]], originally from South America but now naturalized worldwide.

Azolla fronds are only one to three centimeters across and form dense green mats on the water surface.

Each frond contains a leaf cavity that houses the cyanobacterium Nostoc azollae.

This is not a casual association but a permanent, co-evolved symbiosis that has existed for at least 80 million years.

The cyanobacterium receives shelter and a steady supply of sugars from the fern.

In return it converts atmospheric nitrogen into ammonium that the fern can absorb directly.

This arrangement makes Azolla one of the few non-leguminous plants capable of significant [[biological-nitrogen-fixation-agriculture]] fixation.

## How Does Azolla Fix Nitrogen?

The nitrogen-fixing process in Azolla centers on the enzyme nitrogenase, which is produced by the cyanobacterial symbiont.

Nitrogenase converts diatomic nitrogen gas (N₂) from the atmosphere into ammonia (NH₃), which the plant assimilates into amino acids and proteins.

Azolla can fix between one and five kilograms of nitrogen per hectare per day depending on growing conditions.

Over a full [[savory-growing-season-and-non-growing-season-management]] this can add 40 to 120 kilograms of nitrogen per hectare to a rice paddy.

This nitrogen input is comparable to what a moderate application of synthetic urea fertilizer would provide.

The key advantage is that Azolla-derived nitrogen is released gradually as the fern decomposes, reducing leaching losses.

This slow-release pattern aligns well with crop demand curves in flooded crop systems.

Unlike synthetic fertilizer, Azolla also adds [[hamilton-composting-and-organic-matter-management]], improves soil structure, and supports microbial communities.

The [[nitrogen-cycle-in-soil]] benefits from this biological input because fixed nitrogen enters the soil food web rather than leaching into groundwater.

## Using Azolla as a Biofertilizer

The most established use of Azolla is as a green manure in flooded rice cultivation.

Farmers inoculate their paddies with Azolla spores or starter mats before or shortly after transplanting rice seedlings.

The fern spreads across the water surface, fixing nitrogen and suppressing weed growth by blocking sunlight.

When the rice seedlings grow tall enough to shade the water, the Azolla dies back and decomposes, releasing its stored nitrogen.

In some systems farmers do a mid-season "flip" where they drain the paddy briefly to kill the Azolla, incorporate it into the soil, then reflood.

This technique can supply 30 to 50 percent of a rice crop's total nitrogen requirement.

Azolla fits naturally into [[cover-crops-green-manures]] rotations and pairs well with [[biofertilizers-and-soil-inoculants]] programs.

Beyond rice, researchers have tested Azolla as a surface mulch for taro, lotus, and other aquatic crops with promising results.

## Azolla as Livestock Feed

Azolla is remarkably nutritious for a aquatic fern, containing 25 to 35 percent crude protein on a dry-weight basis.

It also provides essential amino acids, minerals including iron and potassium, and beta-carotene.

Dairy farmers in India and Vietnam have incorporated dried Azolla into cattle rations at 10 to 20 percent of total dry matter.

Studies report increases in milk yield of 10 to 15 percent when Azolla partially replaces conventional protein supplements.

Poultry producers have also used Azolla meal as a partial replacement for soybean meal in layer and broiler diets.

The protein quality is not quite equal to soy but the cost savings and local availability make Azolla attractive for smallholder operations.

Integration with [[livestock-feed-formulation]] strategies works best when Azolla is grown on-farm, eliminating transport costs entirely.

Compared to [[black-soldier-fly-larvae-as-feed]], Azolla requires less infrastructure but produces lower protein yields per unit area.

## Growing Azolla: Practical Considerations

Azolla thrives in warm, still, nutrient-rich freshwater with full to partial sunlight.

Optimal growth occurs between 20 and 30 degrees Celsius, and growth essentially stops below 10 degrees.

The pH should be slightly acidic to neutral, ideally between 5.5 and 7.0.

Azolla requires phosphorus for growth, so phosphorus-deficient water will need supplementation with rock phosphate or organic sources.

A simple Azolla pond can be made from a lined trench or container filled with fresh water and a small amount of organic compost as a nutrient starter.

Inoculate with a handful of live Azolla from an existing colony or purchase spore packets.

Within two weeks the pond should develop a full surface cover if conditions are right.

Common problems include overgrowth that depletes [[aact-dissolved-oxygen-fine-bubble-aeration-ingham]] (killing fish in integrated systems), predation by aquatic insects, and wind that pushes mats to one side.

In integrated systems combining Azolla with [[rhizobium-legume-symbiosis]] cover crops and [[cervantes-organic-amendments-chart]], farmers can dramatically reduce or eliminate synthetic nitrogen inputs.

## Limitations and Cautions

Despite its benefits, Azolla is not a silver bullet and has several limitations.

Its nitrogen contribution is significant but usually insufficient as a sole nitrogen source for high-yielding modern rice varieties.

Azolla is sensitive to cold temperatures and cannot overwinter outdoors in temperate climates without protection.

In some regions Azolla species have become invasive, clogging irrigation canals and waterways.

Careful management and containment are essential, especially in tropical and subtropical areas outside its native range.

The nutrient profile of Azolla can vary depending on water chemistry, which complicates its use as a standardized animal feed.

Heavy metal accumulation is a concern if Azolla is grown in contaminated water, as the fern bioaccumulates toxins.

Farmers should always test water quality before using Azolla as feed.
