# Which legumes fix the most nitrogen for gardens and farms?

## Short Answer

The legumes that fix the most nitrogen are usually the ones that produce the most healthy biomass while maintaining active nodules.
Perennial and vigorous cover-crop legumes such as alfalfa, clovers, vetches, peas, sunn hemp, lupins, and some tropical tree legumes often outperform short-season dry beans.
The best choice depends on climate, season, soil pH, water, termination method, and whether the crop will be harvested or returned to the soil.
Use [[legume-nitrogen-yields-by-species]] for crop-specific expectations and [[cover-crop-selection-guide]] for practical selection.
For most gardens, the winning legume is not the theoretical highest fixer, but the one that grows reliably before the next heavy-feeding crop.

## Detailed Explanation

### Biomass is the first filter

Nitrogen fixation is tied to living plant growth.
A legume cannot add much nitrogen if it germinates poorly, grows slowly, or gets terminated too early.
A lush stand with deep roots and abundant leaf area can support more rhizobia activity than a sparse stand.
That is why [[legume-nitrogen-dynamics]] should be read alongside any yield table.
The highest-fixing option on paper may underperform if it does not fit the planting window.
The most reliable option often wins in real gardens and farms.

### Perennial forage legumes

Alfalfa and perennial clovers can be strong nitrogen builders because they grow for a long season and regrow after cutting.
They can support repeated root turnover and residue return over months or years.
Their advantage comes from persistence, deep rooting, and repeated biomass production.
They are most useful where the bed can be occupied for a full season or in pasture and orchard systems.
They are less convenient in a quick vegetable rotation that needs the bed back soon.
Perennial forage context connects to [[mollison-pasture-legumes-and-forage-legumes-of-the-tropics]] and [[pasture-management-and-forage]].

### Annual cover-crop legumes

Annual cover-crop legumes are often the best fit for vegetable gardens and small farms.
Hairy vetch, field peas, cowpeas, crimson clover, berseem clover, lupins, and sunn hemp can produce useful nitrogen in planned windows.
Cool-season choices work before summer crops, while warm-season choices fill summer gaps.
A good annual legume cover crop is easy to sow, easy to terminate, and well matched to local weather.
The design process overlaps with [[cover-crops-green-manures]] and [[cover-crop-species-comparison]].
For practical rotations, [[cover-cropping-systems]] is often more important than chasing a single maximum number.

### Grain legumes and garden beans

Garden beans and peas fix nitrogen, but they are not always the biggest soil builders.
They often send much of their nitrogen into pods and seeds, especially when the harvest is removed.
Roots and leaves still contribute nitrogen after the crop ends, but the net return can be modest.
This is why [[growing-beans-peas-legume-guide]] and [[solomon-growing-beans-and-peas]] are crop guides rather than complete fertility plans.
Beans are still valuable because they produce food while participating in biological nitrogen fixation.
They simply should not be treated as a substitute for a dedicated green manure stand.

### Tropical and subtropical legumes

Warm climates can use high-biomass legumes that are unavailable or impractical in cold regions.
Sunn hemp, cowpea, pigeon pea, leucaena, gliricidia, and other tropical support plants can cycle large amounts of biomass.
Some are annual cover crops, while others are coppiced shrubs or trees.
Their value comes from repeated pruning, mulch production, forage, shade, and nitrogen fixation.
The tree-legume side is covered by [[permaculture-plants-legume-trees-acacia-carob-locust]] and [[permaculture-nitrogen-fixing-trees]].
Tropical pasture and forage design is covered by [[mollison-pasture-legumes-and-forage-legumes-of-the-tropics]].

### Inoculation changes the result

A high-potential legume can fail to fix well when the correct rhizobia are missing.
Fresh inoculant matched to the crop group can dramatically improve nodulation on new ground.
Old inoculant, heat-damaged inoculant, or the wrong strain may produce weak results.
Digging a few roots and checking nodules is better than assuming fixation occurred.
Active nodules are explained in [[rhizobia-legume-nodule-formation]].
The broader symbiosis is explained in [[rhizobium-legume-symbiosis]].
For tree systems, [[mollison-designers-legume-tree-inoculation-and-nitrogen-distribution]] adds design context.

### Soil nitrogen can suppress fixation

Legumes do not always fix at their maximum rate.
If the soil already contains abundant soluble nitrate, the plant may use that nitrogen and reduce its carbon investment in nodules.
This is efficient for the plant but disappointing for the grower trying to build new nitrogen.
A heavily fertilized bed can therefore produce lush legume growth with less biological fixation than expected.
The relationship between soil nitrogen and fixation is part of [[nitrogen-availability-in-legumes]].
It also fits the broader [[nitrogen-cycle-agriculture]] and [[nitrogen-cycle-in-soil]].

### Harvest versus return

The nitrogen benefit depends on what stays on the site.
If seed, hay, pods, or forage are exported, nitrogen leaves with the harvest.
If the crop is chopped, dropped, grazed, trampled, or composted on site, more nitrogen returns to the system.
A legume grown for seed can be food first and fertility second.
A legume grown as green manure is fertility first and food second.
The distinction is central to [[green-manure-guide]] and [[green-manure-crops]].

### Practical ranking

For quick gardens, choose a legume that germinates easily and fits the season.
For winter cover, consider vetch, clover, peas, or regionally adapted mixes.
For summer cover, consider cowpea, sunn hemp, soybean, or locally adapted warm-season legumes.
For perennial systems, consider clover, alfalfa, tree legumes, or shrub support species.
For food production, choose beans and peas for harvest while accepting a smaller soil nitrogen return.
For maximum cycling, terminate before seed export and keep the biomass on the bed.

### Mixes can outperform single species

Grass-legume mixes often make better cover crops than pure legume stands.
The legume fixes nitrogen, while the grass captures soluble nutrients, adds carbon, and improves residue cover.
A cereal rye and vetch mix is a classic example in cool-season systems.
An oat and pea mix is easier to terminate in many small gardens.
The grass can reduce nitrate loss and create a better carbon-to-nitrogen ratio for decomposition.
This is why [[cover-crop-multifunction-design]] and [[comparison-green-manure-vs-cover-crops]] matter in practical planning.

## Key Points

- The highest nitrogen-fixing legume is usually the best-growing legume for the site, not the species with the largest theoretical number.

- Grain beans and peas are useful food crops, but they are often weaker fertility builders than dedicated cover crops.
- Tree legumes and perennial forage legumes can build fertility over longer time frames.
- Grass-legume mixes often balance nitrogen with carbon and soil cover better than pure legume stands.
