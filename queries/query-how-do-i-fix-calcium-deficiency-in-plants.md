# How Do I Fix Calcium Deficiency in Plants?

## Short Answer
Calcium deficiency causes distorted new growth, curling leaves,
brown spots, and tip burn in cannabis and other plants. Fix it by
ensuring soil pH is in the correct range (6.0 to 7.0 for soil,
5.5 to 6.2 for hydroponics), applying calcium-rich amendments
like limestone, gypsum, or cal-mag supplements, and improving
soil biology to enhance calcium availability. The root cause is
often pH-related nutrient lockout rather than actual calcium
deficiency.

## Detailed Explanation

Calcium is an essential secondary macronutrient that plays a
critical role in cell wall structure, membrane integrity, and
cell division. Unlike mobile nutrients like nitrogen, calcium is
immobile in plants, meaning it cannot be relocated from older
tissues to new growth. This is why calcium deficiency symptoms
always appear first on the newest, actively growing parts of the
plant: emerging leaves, shoot tips, and root tips.

The [[calcium-magnesium-secondary-nutrients]] page provides a
thorough overview of calcium's role in plant biology and its
relationship with magnesium and sulfur. In cannabis, calcium
deficiency typically manifests as distorted, crinkly, or curled
new leaves that may have yellow or brown spots along the margins.
The growing tips may appear burnt or necrotic, and in severe
cases, new growth can be stunted or deformed.

Root tips may also die back, reducing the plant's ability to take
up water and other nutrients. These symptoms are covered in
detail on the [[cannabis-calcium-nutrient]] page along with
photographic references for identification. Because calcium is
immobile, deficiency is always most visible at the growing points
of the plant rather than on older, lower leaves.

The most common cause of calcium deficiency is not a lack of
calcium in the soil or nutrient solution, but rather
[[cannabis-nutrient-lockout-and-ph]] that prevents the plant from
absorbing it. Calcium is most available to plants at slightly
acidic to neutral pH. In soil, the optimal range is 6.0 to 7.0,
while in hydroponic systems it is 5.5 to 6.2.

When pH drifts outside these ranges, calcium becomes insoluble
and unavailable even if it is present in abundance. Testing and
adjusting pH should always be the first step when addressing
suspected calcium deficiency. Many growers waste time and money
adding calcium amendments when the real problem is simply pH
being too low or too high for uptake.

For soil-based grows, the most effective amendments are
agricultural lime (calcium carbonate) for raising pH and adding
calcium simultaneously, or gypsum (calcium sulfate) for adding
calcium without affecting pH. Dolomite lime provides both calcium
and magnesium. In living soil systems,
[[dynamic-accumulator-plants-calcium-and-magnesium]] such as
comfrey, dandelion, and nettle can be chopped and dropped as
mulch to slowly release calcium as they decompose.

Eggshells are a popular home remedy, though they take
considerable time to break down and should be powdered and
composted first for meaningful effect. In hydroponic and coco
coir grows, [[cal-mag-supplementation]] is the standard approach.
Most bottled nutrient lines include a cal-mag product, or you can
prepare your own from calcium nitrate and magnesium sulfate
(Epsom salt).

Coco coir is particularly prone to calcium and magnesium issues
because the material naturally binds these cations during the
manufacturing process, creating a temporary demand that must be
met with supplementation during the first few weeks of growth.
The [[teaming-with-nutrients-ph-testing-and-soil-amendment]]
guide emphasizes that calcium availability is also influenced by
soil biology and microbial activity.

In the short term, foliar sprays of calcium nitrate or calcium
chloride can provide rapid relief to affected plants, though they
should not be relied upon as the sole solution. Addressing the
root cause through pH correction and proper amendments will
produce lasting results. Building healthy soil biology through
composting, mulching, and avoiding excessive tillage improves
long-term calcium availability and reduces the likelihood of
deficiency recurring.

## Key Points
- Calcium deficiency shows on new growth first: curled leaves, brown spots, tip burn, and stunted tips
- Check pH first; [[cannabis-nutrient-lockout-and-ph]] is the most common cause, not actual deficiency
- In soil, use lime or gypsum; in hydroponics, use [[cal-mag-supplementation]] products
- [[dynamic-accumulator-plants-calcium-and-magnesium]] provide slow-release calcium in organic systems
- Foliar calcium sprays offer quick relief but address the root cause with pH correction and amendments
- Coco coir requires early [[cal-mag-supplementation]] due to natural cation binding during manufacturing
