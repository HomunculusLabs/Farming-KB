# How to Get Rid of Slugs and Snails Organically

## Short Answer
Slugs and snails can be controlled organically using a combination of beer 
traps, copper
barriers, diatomaceous earth, handpicking, encouraging natural predators, and 
creating
habitat that favors predators over pests. A multi-pronged approach is most 
effective, as no
single method provides complete control of these persistent garden pests. The
[[cervantes-slugs-snails]] page covers slug and snail identification and organic
 management
strategies specifically for cannabis gardens.

## Detailed Explanation

Slugs and snails are among the most damaging garden pests, capable of destroying
 seedlings
overnight and riddling mature plants with unsightly holes. They feed primarily 
at night and
during damp conditions, making them difficult to catch in action. Their soft 
bodies require
moisture for survival and locomotion, which means they are most problematic 
during rainy
seasons, in heavily irrigated gardens, and in areas with heavy mulch or dense 
ground cover.
Understanding their behavior and lifecycle is the foundation of an effective 
organic control
program.

Beer traps are one of the simplest and most popular organic slug control methods
 available
to home gardeners. Bury a shallow container (a yogurt cup or tuna can works 
well) so its rim
sits at soil level, and fill it halfway with beer. The fermentation yeasts 
attract slugs,
which crawl in, become intoxicated, and eventually drown. Empty and refill traps
 every two
to three days to keep them effective. Any beer works, but cheaper varieties with
 strong yeast
content tend to be most effective. Some gardeners substitute a mixture of water,
 yeast, and
sugar when beer is not available or when they prefer not to use alcohol. 
Position traps every
3 to 5 feet throughout the affected area and near vulnerable plants. The
[[cho-natural-pest-control-methods]] page covers beer trap placement and 
alternative bait
formulations for slug control.

Copper barriers exploit a natural sensitivity that slugs and snails have to 
copper metal.
When they come into contact with copper tape or flashing, it delivers a mild 
electric-like
shock that repels them without killing them. Wrap copper tape around the rims of
 raised beds,
individual pots, and plant stems to create an effective barrier. Copper barriers
 are most
effective when the copper surface is clean and free of oxidation. While the 
initial cost is
higher than other methods, copper tape is durable and reusable across multiple 
growing
seasons, making it cost-effective over time. The [[container-gardening-guide]] 
page
discusses integrating copper barriers into container gardening setups for 
comprehensive pest
protection.

Diatomaceous earth (DE) is a mechanical insecticide made from fossilized aquatic
 organisms
called diatoms. The microscopic sharp edges of diatom shells cut through the 
soft bodies of
slugs and snails on contact, causing dehydration and death. Apply a ring of DE 
around
vulnerable plants or across known slug travel routes to create a protective 
barrier.
Diatomaceous earth must remain dry to maintain its cutting properties; it loses
effectiveness when wet and must be reapplied after rain or heavy watering. Food-
grade DE is
safe for gardens and poses minimal risk to beneficial insects when applied as a 
targeted
barrier rather than broadcast across the entire garden. The
[[comparison-neem-oil-vs-diatomaceous-earth]] page compares DE with other 
organic pest
control options for different garden situations.

Handpicking is labor-intensive but highly effective, especially for small 
gardens with
manageable plant numbers. Go out with a flashlight one to two hours after dark, 
when slugs
and snails are most actively feeding. Wear gloves and drop collected pests into 
a bucket of
soapy water to ensure they do not escape or return. Consistent nightly patrols 
for one to
two weeks can dramatically reduce populations. Check under leaves, near the base
 of plants,
under pots, and in any dark, moist hiding spots where slugs congregate during 
daylight hours.
The [[cervantes-biological-pest-control-predators]] page discusses how 
handpicking
complements biological control methods for comprehensive pest management.

Encouraging natural predators is the most sustainable long-term approach to slug
 management
in any garden ecosystem. Ground beetles, rove beetles, firefly larvae, frogs, 
toads, garter
snakes, and certain bird species all prey on slugs and snails at various life 
stages. Create
habitat for these predators by providing ground cover such as flat rocks, logs, 
and leaf
litter, a water source like a small [[faires-pond-design-and-aquaculture]], and 
avoiding
broad-spectrum pesticides that kill beneficial species alongside target pests. 
The
[[beneficial-insects-guide]] and [[beneficial-insect-habitat]] pages detail how 
to attract
and support slug predators in your garden ecosystem for natural, ongoing pest 
control.

## Key Points
- Use beer traps, copper barriers, and diatomaceous earth as physical and mechanical controls
- Handpick at night with a flashlight for immediate population reduction over 1 to 2 weeks
- Attract natural predators like ground beetles, frogs, and toads by providing habitat and avoiding pesticides
- Modify habitat by removing debris, reducing mulch near vulnerable plants, and watering in the morning
- Iron phosphate baits (Sluggo) are an effective organic option safe around pets, wildlife, and edible crops
