# How Do I Identify and Control Aphids on Cannabis?

## Short Answer
Aphids are small, soft-bodied insects that cluster on cannabis stems and
leaf undersides, sucking sap and excreting honeydew that promotes sooty
mold growth. They reproduce rapidly through parthenogenesis, with a single
female capable of producing dozens of live young per week. Control requires
an integrated approach combining predatory insects, organic sprays, and
cultural hygiene practices within a structured [[integrated-pest-management]]
program.

## Detailed Explanation

Aphids are among the most pervasive cannabis pests, capable of establishing
large colonies in a matter of days under favorable conditions. They come in
several colors including green, black, brown, and yellow, with
aphids-cannabis|cannabis aphids (Phorodon cannabis) being a
species-specific pest that has become increasingly problematic in commercial
cultivation. Adults are typically 1 to 3 millimeters long and often go
unnoticed until populations reach damaging levels. They congregate on new
growth, stem nodes, and the undersides of leaves where they pierce plant
tissue and extract phloem sap using their specialized piercing-sucking
mouthparts.

The damage from aphid feeding operates on two levels. Direct feeding causes
leaf curling, yellowing, and stunted growth as the plant loses nutrients
and water to the feeding insects. Indirect damage comes from honeydew
excretion, a sticky sugar-rich substance that coats leaves below the
feeding site and promotes the growth of black sooty mold fungi. This mold
can interfere with [[photosynthesis-and-chloroplast-function]]
and reduce overall plant vigor. In severe infestations, aphids can also
transmit viral pathogens between plants, including cucumber mosaic virus
and other potyviruses that may affect cannabis. Even when viruses are not
transmitted, the sheer biomass of aphid colonies can physically deform new
growth and significantly reduce harvest quality.

Aphid biology makes them particularly challenging to control through
chemical means alone. Most cannabis-attacking aphids are viviparous,
meaning females give birth to live clones of themselves without mating.
Under favorable conditions, a single aphid can produce 50 to 80 offspring
in her lifetime, and those offspring begin reproducing within a week. This
exponential growth means that catching an infestation early is absolutely
critical. Some species also produce winged individuals (alates) when
populations become crowded, allowing them to disperse to new plants or
grow areas. Regular scouting, especially of [[cannabis-vegetative-growth-stage]] plants, helps detect colonies before they spread beyond control.

Biological control agents are highly effective against aphids and form the
backbone of most successful cannabis IPM programs. Lady beetles
(Hippodamia convergens) are voracious aphid predators, with adults and
larvae consuming 50 or more aphids per day. Green lacewing larvae, sold
commercially as "aphid lions," are equally aggressive predators that impale
aphids on their large mandibles and drain their body contents. Parasitic
wasps in the genus Aphidius lay their eggs inside aphid bodies, turning
them into mummified husks that release new wasps to continue the cycle.
These predators are most effective when released preventively and supported
by [[mollison-designers-insectary-plants-and-beneficial-insect-habitat]] such as
flowering umbels and sweet alyssum that provide nectar and pollen for adult
predators to sustain themselves when aphid populations are low.

Organic spray options include neem|neem oil, insecticidal soap,
pyrethrin-based products, and fatty acid salts. These work best when
applied directly to aphid colonies, ensuring thorough coverage of leaf
undersides and stem crevices where aphids congregate. Some growers use a
strong stream of water to physically dislodge aphids from plants, though
this must be done carefully to avoid damaging delicate growth. For
persistent infestations, systemic options like [[jadam-all-purpose-pesticide]] can provide longer-lasting control when applied as a soil
drench, as the plant takes up the active compounds systemically. Garlic
and chili-based sprays also show repellent effects against aphids and can
be used as part of a rotation strategy to prevent resistance.

Prevention focuses on exclusion and strict hygiene protocols. Inspect all
new plants, clones, and equipment before introducing them to the grow
space. Quarantine new plant material for at least one week and monitor
for pest activity before integrating with existing plants. Screen
ventilation intakes with fine mesh to prevent winged aphids from entering
indoor grows. Eliminate weed hosts in and around the growing area, and
maintain a clean environment free of plant debris where aphids can
overwinter. In outdoor settings, planting [[companion-planting-deterrents]] such as garlic, chives, nasturtiums, and alliums
around the cannabis canopy can help repel aphid colonies through volatile
compound release.

## Key Points
- Aphids cluster on new growth and leaf undersides, causing curling,
  yellowing, and honeydew excretion that promotes sooty mold
- A single female aphid can produce 50 to 80 live offspring per week
  without mating, leading to rapid population explosions
- Lady beetles, lacewing larvae, and Aphidius parasitic wasps provide the
  most effective biological control when released preventively
- neem|Neem oil and insecticidal soap provide effective contact control
  when applied thoroughly to leaf undersides
- Prevention through screening, quarantine, inspection of new plants, and
  weed management is essential for long-term control
