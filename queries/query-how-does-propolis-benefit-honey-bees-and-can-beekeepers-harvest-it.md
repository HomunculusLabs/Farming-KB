# How Does Propolis Benefit Honey Bees and Can Beekeepers Harvest It?

## Short Answer

**Propolis** (from Greek *pro* = "before" + *polis* = "city") is a resinous substance that honey bees collect from tree buds, sap flows, and other botanical sources. Bees use it as a structural sealant, antimicrobial agent, and immune defense for the hive. Beekeepers can harvest propolis as a valuable secondary product, though it comes at the cost of reduced hive hygiene if over-harvested.

## What Is Propolis?

Propolis is a complex mixture of:

- **Resins (50–60%):** Collected from tree buds (poplar, birch, alder, pine, chestnut, eucalyptus) and plant exudates
- **Waxes (30–40%):** Beeswax secreted by worker bees, mixed with the collected resins
- **Essential oils (5–10%):** Volatile aromatic compounds contributing to antimicrobial properties
- **Pollen (5%):** Incidental collection mixed with resin
- **Bioactive compounds:** Flavonoids (pinocembrin, galangin, chrysin), phenolic acids, terpenes, coumarins, and caffeic acid derivatives

The exact composition varies dramatically by region, season, and available plant species — a tropical propolis from Brazil (green propolis from *Baccharis dracunculifolia*) has a completely different chemical profile than temperate propolis from poplar buds in North America or Europe.

## How Bees Collect Propolis

1. **Foraging:** Specialized resin-foraging bees (typically older foragers, 15–20 days old) visit tree buds and wounds where resin exudes
2. **Collection:** They use their mandibles to break off small resin pieces and pack them into their **corbiculae** (pollen baskets on hind legs) — the same structures used for pollen transport
3. **Processing:** At the hive, other bees help unload the resin. They add wax and salivary secretions, kneading it into the final propolis mixture
4. **Application:** Bees use propolis to coat and seal specific areas of the hive

Resin foraging is physiologically demanding — the resin is sticky and difficult to handle. Bees that specialize in resin collection typically do so for only a few days before switching to nectar or pollen foraging.

## Benefits of Propolis for Honey Bees

### 1. Structural Sealant and Weatherproofing

Bees use propolis to:

- **Seal gaps and cracks** in the hive, reducing drafts and maintaining stable internal temperature
- **Smooth rough surfaces** inside the cavity, creating a more uniform and manageable living space
- **Reduce entrance size** — propolis deposits at the hive entrance narrow the opening, improving defense against wasps, robbing bees, and other intruders
- **Strengthen comb attachment** — propolis reinforces the junction between comb and hive surfaces

### 2. Antimicrobial Defense

This is propolis's most important biological function. Propolis inhibits the growth of:

- **Bacteria:** Active against *Paenibacillus larvae* (cause of American Foulbrood), *Melissococcus plutonius* (European Foulbrood), and secondary bacterial infections
- **Fungi:** Inhibits *Ascosphaera apis* (chalkbrood) and other pathogenic fungi
- **Viruses:** Demonstrated antiviral activity against Deformed Wing Virus (DWV) and other honey bee viruses
- **Protozoa:** May inhibit *Nosema* spp. (nosema disease) spore germination

The mechanism involves multiple bioactive compounds working synergistically — flavonoids disrupt bacterial cell membranes, phenolic acids interfere with microbial enzyme systems, and terpenes damage fungal cell walls.

### 3. Immune System Support

Recent research has revealed that propolis benefits bee **social immunity** in surprising ways:

- **Reduced immune gene expression:** Colonies with more propolis show lower baseline expression of immune-related genes in individual bees. This sounds negative but is actually beneficial — it means bees invest less energy in individual immunity because the colony environment is cleaner and less pathogenic.
- **Gut microbiome support:** Propolis residues in the hive may promote beneficial gut bacteria (*Snodgrassella alvi*, *Gilliamella apicola*) that are essential for bee health and nutrient processing.
- **Reduced physiological stress:** Bees in propolis-rich hives show lower levels of oxidative stress markers, suggesting a more balanced physiological state.

### 4. Mummification of Intruders

When a small intruder (mouse, beetle, lizard, wax moth larvae) enters the hive and dies or cannot be removed, bees coat the carcass entirely in propolis. This **mummification** prevents decomposition, bacterial growth, and odor, protecting the colony from disease. A propolis-mummified mouse can be preserved indefinitely inside a hive.

### 5. Varroa Mite Interaction

The relationship between propolis and Varroa destructor is complex:

- **Some evidence** suggests propolis residues in brood cells may inhibit Varroa reproduction, though results are inconsistent
- Propolis-rich hives tend to have lower mite loads in some studies, possibly due to overall improved colony health
- However, heavy propolis use makes Varroa monitoring and treatment more difficult (propolis glues monitoring boards and treatment strips in place)

## Harvesting Propolis

### When to Harvest

- **Late summer to early fall** (after the main nectar flow, before winter preparation) is ideal
- Avoid harvesting in spring when bees need propolis most for colony health and disease defense

### Harvesting Methods

**1. Propolis trap (grid method)**
- Place a flexible plastic or nylon grid with narrow slots over the top bars
- Bees fill the gaps with propolis to seal them
- Remove the grid, place it in a freezer for 30–60 minutes to harden the propolis
- Twist or flex the grid to snap off the frozen propolis pieces
- **Yield:** 50–200 g per trap per season
- **Pros:** Clean, easy, minimal comb disturbance
- **Cons:** Can annoy bees (they want those gaps sealed)

**2. Hive scraper collection**
- Scrape propolis deposits from hive bodies, frames, inner covers, and entrance reducers during routine inspections
- **Yield:** 20–100 g per hive per season
- **Pros:** No extra equipment; happens naturally during management
- **Cons:** Slow, messy, mixed with wood chips and wax

**3. Propolis mat or screen**
- Lay a fabric mat or wire screen on the top bars
- Bees coat the surface with propolis
- Remove, freeze, and peel/scrape off propolis
- Similar to the trap method but with a flat surface

### Post-Harvest Processing

1. **Clean:** Remove debris (wax, wood chips, bee parts)
2. **Store frozen:** Propolis keeps best at -20°C (-4°F) in airtight containers, protected from light
3. **For tinctures:** Soak cleaned propolis in 70–95% ethanol (1:5 to 1:10 ratio) for 2–4 weeks, shaking daily. Strain through a coffee filter. Store the tincture in amber glass bottles.
4. **For salves:** Melt propolis in a carrier oil (coconut, olive) at low heat (~60°C/140°F), strain, and combine with beeswax to set.

## Uses for Harvested Propolis

### Medicinal and Therapeutic
- **Antimicrobial tincture:** Used as a natural antiseptic for minor cuts, sore throats, and oral health
- **Anti-inflammatory:** Topical application for skin irritation, minor burns, and cold sores
- **Immune support:** Taken internally (tincture in warm water) during cold and flu season
- **Oral health:** Found in natural toothpaste and mouthwash formulations; inhibits *Streptococcus mutans*

### Practical
- **Wood finish and varnish:** Propolis dissolved in alcohol makes a beautiful, aromatic wood finish
- **Stringed instrument bow rosin:** Historically used by luthiers for violin bows
- **Candle and soap additive:** Adds antimicrobial properties and a pleasant honey-resin scent

## Important Considerations

### Don't Over-Harvest

Bees need propolis for colony health. Harvesting too aggressively:

- Weakens the colony's antimicrobial defense
- Increases susceptibility to pathogens (especially American Foulbrood and chalkbrood)
- Forces bees to spend foraging effort on resin collection instead of nectar and pollen
- A good rule of thumb: harvest no more than 30–50% of available propolis per season

### Allergy Warning

Propolis can cause **allergic contact dermatitis** in sensitive individuals, particularly beekeepers who handle it frequently. Symptoms include skin redness, itching, and eczema-like reactions. Always test a small amount on your inner wrist before using propolis tinctures topically or internally.

### Quality Varies

Propolis quality depends on:

- **Botanical source:** Poplar bud propolis (temperate) is the most studied; green propolis from Brazil (*Baccharis*) has unique anticancer properties studied extensively
- **Harvest timing:** Fall propolis tends to be richer in bioactive compounds than spring propolis
- **Contamination:** Avoid collecting propolis from hives treated with synthetic acaricides or antibiotics — these chemicals accumulate in propolis

## Key Takeaways

- Propolis is a resinous antimicrobial sealant that bees use for structural, defensive, and immune functions
- It inhibits bacteria, fungi, viruses, and may help with Varroa management indirectly through improved colony health
- Harvest using propolis traps or scraping during fall; never harvest more than 30–50% of available propolis
- Process into tinctures (ethanol extraction) or salves for medicinal use
- Propolis is one of several valuable non-honey hive products — alongside beeswax, pollen, royal jelly, and bee venom
