# How Do I Design and Plant a Living Fence?

A living fence is a dense, functional barrier made from living woody plants. **Technique**: Dig holes 30–40 cm deep. For bare-root plants, soak roots 1–2 hours, spread naturally, plant at original soil depth. For live stakes, plant 30–50 cm deep in moist soil. Mulch heavily (10–15 cm organic mulch, 5 cm from stems). Water thoroughly at planting.

## Step 7: Establish and Maintain

**Year 1**: Water weekly during dry periods. Keep 60 cm weed-free zone. Replace failures immediately. No pruning — let roots establish.

**Year 2**: First prune in late winter — cut back main stems by one-third to encourage branching. Begin training flexible stems horizontally for pleaching. Apply compost band along the fence line.

**Year 3**: Second prune — cut new growth by one-third to one-half. Begin gap filling. Test fence integrity; remove temporary fencing if stockproof.

**Years 4–10**: Annual pruning (cut previous year's growth by one-third). Level the top at desired height to encourage lateral density. Begin harvesting fodder, fruit, or firewood.

**10+ years**: Establish coppice rotation (8–15 year cycles). Hedge lay every 10–20 years to rejuvenate and fill base gaps.

## Common Mistakes

1. **Underestimating establishment time** — plan 3–5 years minimum for a full barrier
2. **Planting too few species** — mix at least 3–5 for resilience against disease
3. **Skipping temporary fencing** — livestock will destroy young plants
4. **Insufficient watering** — most failures come from drought stress in years 1–2
5. **Planting on property line** — plant 0.5–1 m inside your boundary
6. **Using invasive species** — check local lists; multiflora rose, autumn olive, Russian olive are problematic in many areas
7. **Not mulching** — 10–15 cm mulch is critical for moisture, weeds, and soil building
8. **Planting too shallow or too deep** — match the root collar to soil level

## Cost Expectations

Budget roughly $5–15 per linear meter for materials and planting labor. Maintenance costs decrease as the fence matures. By year 10–15, living fences typically cost less per year than wire fencing when accounting for replacement and repairs. Productive species can offset costs through fruit, fodder, and firewood harvests.
