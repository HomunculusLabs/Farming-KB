## How Do I Propagate Plants from Cuttings and Other Vegetative Methods?

## Short Answer

Vegetative propagation lets you create exact genetic copies of parent plants using cuttings, layering, division, and grafting techniques.
Unlike seed growing, vegetative methods preserve desirable traits such as flavor, yield, and disease resistance.
Most gardeners start with stem cuttings because they require minimal equipment and work for hundreds of species.
With the right timing, rooting medium, and humidity control, even beginners can achieve high success rates.

## Detailed Explanation

### What Is Vegetative Propagation?

Vegetative propagation produces new plants from vegetative parts of an existing plant rather than from seeds.
This includes stems, leaves, roots, and specialized structures like bulbs, tubers, and rhizomes.
Because no pollination or genetic recombination occurs, each new plant is a clone of the parent.
The [[cannabis-propagation-sexual-vs-asexual-clarke|sexual versus asexual propagation]] helps you choose the right method for each crop.

### Stem Cuttings: The Most Popular Method

Stem cuttings involve removing a piece of stem with at least one node and encouraging it to form roots.
Softwood cuttings come from new spring growth and root quickly but are delicate.
Semi-hardwood cuttings taken in midsummer offer a good balance of flexibility and toughness.
Hardwood cuttings from dormant winter wood are the most durable but take longer to root.

### Rooting Hormones and Root Development

Rooting hormones containing synthetic or natural auxins dramatically increase cutting success rates.
Indole-3-butyric acid (IBA) is the most common synthetic auxin found in commercial rooting powders and gels.
The [[hamilton-layering-propagation-techniques]] page details several layering variations suitable for different plant types.
Once roots fill the moss or soil contact zone, you sever the new plant from the parent and pot it independently.

### Division: Splitting Mature Plants

Division separates a mature plant into two or more sections, each with its own roots and shoots.
This technique works best for perennials that form clumps, including hostas, daylilies, ornamental grasses, and many herbs.
Spring-blooming plants are typically divided in fall, while summer-blooming plants divide best in early spring.
Each division should have at least three healthy shoots and a robust root system to ensure rapid establishment.
Dividing plants every three to five years rejuvenates overcrowded clumps and restores vigorous blooming.
The [[fruit-tree-grafting-guide]] covers whip-and-tongue, cleft, and bark inlay grafts for different scion and rootstock sizes.
Budding is a form of grafting that uses a single bud instead of a multi-bud scion piece.
The [[cannabis-grafting-intergeneric-humulus-clarke|Cannabis grafting]] has even been demonstrated between cannabis and hops, showing the technique's versatility.

### Tissue Culture and Micropropagation

Tissue culture propagates plants from tiny explants in sterile laboratory conditions on nutrient agar media.
This method produces thousands of identical clones in a small space, making it valuable for commercial operations.
