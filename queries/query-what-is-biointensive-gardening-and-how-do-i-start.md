# What is Biointensive Gardening and How Do I Start?

## Short Answer

Biointensive gardening is a small-scale, high-yield organic growing method developed by [[building-soil]] fertility over time.

This is different from permaculture's broader whole-property design approach, though both aim for sustainable food production.

## The Eight Principles of Biointensive Growing

Biointensive gardening rests on eight interconnected principles that work together as a system.

The first principle is deep soil preparation through [[comparison-double-digging-vs-no-till|Double-digging]] is the most labor-intensive step but only needs to be done once per bed if managed well.

The second principle is composting, with the system aiming to produce as much compost as possible on-site.

Each bed should ideally generate enough compost through crop residues to sustain its own fertility.

The third principle is close plant spacing, often in hexagonal patterns rather than straight rows.

This creates a living mulch that shades the soil, reduces evaporation, and suppresses weeds.

Close spacing can increase yields by two to four times compared to conventional row gardening.

## Deep Bed Preparation

The foundation of biointensive gardening is the deeply prepared raised bed.

Beds are typically 4 to 5 feet wide and as long as space allows, with paths between them for access.

The [[double-digging]] process involves removing the top 12 inches of soil, loosening the subsoil below, and replacing the topsoil mixed with compost.

This breaks up hardpan layers and allows roots to penetrate much deeper than in conventional tilling.

A well-prepared biointensive bed holds significantly more water and air in the soil profile, and subsequent seasons require only light surface forking.

## Crop Planning and Calorie Farming

One unique feature of biointensive gardening is its emphasis on calorie-dense crops.

John Jeavons found that root crops like potatoes, sweet potatoes, and parsnips produce the most calories per square foot.

Grain crops like wheat, oats, and quinoa are also important for a complete diet.

A typical biointensive plan allocates roughly 60% of growing area to calorie crops and 30% to calorie-efficient root crops.

The remaining 10% goes to vegetables for vitamins and minerals.

[[query-how-do-i-design-a-crop-rotation-plan-for-my-vegetable-garden|Designing a rotation plan]] ensures that nitrogen-fixing crops follow heavy feeders each season.

## Companion Planting and Intercropping

Biointensive gardening relies heavily on [[comparison-biointensive-vs-permaculture|Compared to permaculture]], biointensive is more structured and bed-focused.

Permaculture takes a broader whole-property design approach, while biointensive concentrates on maximizing bed productivity.

[[comparison-biodynamic-vs-biointensive|Biodynamic farming]] adds spiritual and astronomical elements that biointensive does not include.

| Feature | Biointensive | Square Foot | Permaculture |
|---------|-------------|-------------|--------------|
| Bed preparation | Double-dig 24 inches | 6-inch raised bed | Varies |
| Plant spacing | Hexagonal close | Grid squares | Natural patterns |
| Focus | Calorie crops | Any vegetables | Whole system |
| Composting | On-site intensive | Optional | Integrated |
| Seed saving | Emphasized | Not required | Encouraged |

## Getting Started: A Practical Timeline

Starting a biointensive garden is best done gradually rather than all at once.

Begin with one or two beds in your first season to learn the techniques without becoming overwhelmed.

Late winter or early spring is the ideal time to prepare beds while the soil is moist but not frozen.

Start your compost pile immediately, even before beds are dug, so you have finished compost available at planting time.

Choose easy calorie crops like potatoes and beans for your first season.

[[comparison-double-digging-vs-no-till|Deep soil preparation]] is the foundational step that enables everything else.
- Close hexagonal spacing creates a living mulch that conserves water and suppresses weeds.
- Calorie farming focuses on root crops and grains for dietary self-sufficiency.
- [[comparison-biointensive-vs-permaculture|Biointensive is more structured]] than permaculture, focusing on intensive bed management.
