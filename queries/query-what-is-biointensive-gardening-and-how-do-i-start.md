# What is Biointensive Gardening and How Do I Start?

## Short Answer

Biointensive gardening is a small-scale, high-yield organic growing method developed by John Jeavons at Ecology Action.

It uses deep soil preparation, close plant spacing, companion planting, and intensive composting to produce maximum food from minimal land.

The goal is to grow a complete diet in the smallest possible area while building soil fertility over time.

This is different from permaculture's broader whole-property design approach, though both aim for sustainable food production.

## The Eight Principles of Biointensive Growing

Biointensive gardening rests on eight interconnected principles that work together as a system.

The first principle is deep soil preparation through double-digging, which loosens soil to a depth of 24 inches.

This creates a friable root zone that allows plants to access nutrients and water far more effectively.

[[comparison-double-digging-vs-no-till|Double-digging]] is the most labor-intensive step but only needs to be done once per bed if managed well.

The second principle is composting, with the system aiming to produce as much compost as possible on-site.

Each bed should ideally generate enough compost through crop residues to sustain its own fertility.

The third principle is close plant spacing, often in hexagonal patterns rather than straight rows.

This creates a living mulch that shades the soil, reduces evaporation, and suppresses weeds.

Close spacing can increase yields by two to four times compared to conventional row gardening.

## Deep Bed Preparation

The foundation of biointensive gardening is the deeply prepared raised bed.

Beds are typically 4 to 5 feet wide and as long as space allows, with paths between them for access.

The double-digging process involves removing the top 12 inches of soil, loosening the subsoil below, and replacing the topsoil mixed with compost.

This breaks up hardpan layers and allows roots to penetrate much deeper than in conventional tilling.

A well-prepared biointensive bed holds significantly more water and air in the soil profile, and subsequent seasons require only light surface forking.

## Crop Planning and Calorie Farming

One unique feature of biointensive gardening is its emphasis on calorie-dense crops.

John Jeavons found that root crops like potatoes, sweet potatoes, and parsnips produce the most calories per square foot.

Grain crops like wheat, oats, and quinoa are also important for a complete diet.

A typical biointensive plan allocates roughly 60% of growing area to calorie crops and 30% to calorie-efficient root crops.

The remaining 10% goes to vegetables for vitamins and minerals.

[[comparison-crop-rotation-vs-cover-cropping|Crop rotation]] is essential to prevent soil depletion when growing heavy feeders.

[[query-how-do-i-design-a-crop-rotation-plan-for-my-vegetable-garden|Designing a rotation plan]] ensures that nitrogen-fixing crops follow heavy feeders each season.

## Companion Planting and Intercropping

Biointensive gardening relies heavily on [[comparison-companion-planting-vs-intercropping|companion planting and intercropping]] to maximize bed space.

Certain plant combinations improve growth, deter pests, or enhance flavor when grown together.

For example, beans fix nitrogen that benefits nearby heavy-feeding brassicas.

Basil planted near tomatoes is said to improve flavor and repel certain insects.

The close spacing in biointensive beds naturally creates these beneficial plant communities.

Polycultures are preferred over monocultures because they mimic natural ecosystems and reduce pest pressure.

## Seed Saving and Open-Pollinated Varieties

Biointensive gardening strongly encourages using open-pollinated seeds rather than hybrids.

Open-pollinated varieties allow gardeners to save seed from year to year, reducing costs over time.

Saved seeds gradually adapt to local conditions, becoming more resilient to regional pests and climate, which is why biointensive strongly encourages open-pollinated varieties over hybrids.

The biointensive method includes specific techniques for growing seed crops alongside food crops.

This closed-loop seed saving is a key part of the system's goal of self-sufficiency.

Hybrid seeds do not breed true and must be purchased fresh each season, while open-pollinated varieties adapt to local conditions over time.

## Whole System Integration

Biointensive gardening is not just a planting technique but a complete food-growing system.

It integrates composting, calorie farming, seed saving, and soil building into one closed-loop cycle.

[[comparison-biointensive-vs-permaculture|Compared to permaculture]], biointensive is more structured and bed-focused.

Permaculture takes a broader whole-property design approach, while biointensive concentrates on maximizing bed productivity.

[[comparison-biointensive-vs-square-foot-gardening|Square foot gardening]] shares the raised-bed concept but uses a fixed grid and lighter soil preparation.

[[comparison-biodynamic-vs-biointensive|Biodynamic farming]] adds spiritual and astronomical elements that biointensive does not include.

## Getting Started: A Practical Timeline

Starting a biointensive garden is best done gradually rather than all at once.

Begin with one or two beds in your first season to learn the techniques without becoming overwhelmed.

Late winter or early spring is the ideal time to prepare beds while the soil is moist but not frozen.

Start your compost pile immediately, even before beds are dug, so you have finished compost available at planting time.

Choose easy calorie crops like potatoes and beans for your first season.

[[query-what-is-the-difference-between-biointensive-and-no-till-gardening|Biointensive and no-till]] represent different philosophies on soil disturbance, and some gardeners combine elements of both.

Focus on building one skill per season rather than trying to implement all eight principles simultaneously.

## Key Points

- Biointensive gardening can produce two to four times more food per square foot than conventional methods.
- [[comparison-double-digging-vs-no-till|Deep soil preparation]] is the foundational step that enables everything else.
- Close hexagonal spacing creates a living mulch that conserves water and suppresses weeds.
- Calorie farming focuses on root crops and grains for dietary self-sufficiency.
- [[comparison-companion-planting-vs-intercropping|Companion planting]] reduces pest pressure and improves yields naturally.
- On-site composting closes the fertility loop and reduces external inputs.
- Open-pollinated seeds and seed saving ensure long-term garden resilience and cost savings.
- [[comparison-biointensive-vs-permaculture|Biointensive is more structured]] than permaculture, focusing on intensive bed management.
