**Actinorhizal N-fixation:** *Frankia* actinomycetes form nodules on roots of non-legume trees and [[mollison-designers-shade-grown-coffee-and-cacao-systems]]. Fixes 40–60 kg N/ha/year.
- **Calliandra calothyrsus** — Shrub/small tree with high leaf N (3–4%); excellent chop-and-drop mulch and bee forage. Tolerates acid soils and partial shade.
- **Albizia lebbeck** — Large canopy tree, good timber, shade, and fodder. Fixes moderate N; deciduous leaves recycle nutrients. Tolerates drought once established.
- **Flemingia macrophylla** — Shrub used widely in tropical [[root-competition-plant-spacing-solomon]]**: NFT roots can outcompete adjacent crops for water and phosphorus. Maintain adequate spacing or use root pruning/barriers in intensive systems.
- **Coppicing/pollarding**: Black locust, alder, leucaena, gliricidia, and caragana coppice vigorously (regrow from cut stumps). This lets you manage canopy, harvest biomass, and stimulate N-release.
- **Fodder value**: Leucaena, gliricidia, calliandra, desmanthus, and caragana provide high-protein leaf fodder. Check toxicity (e.g., mimosine in leucaena — plant low-mimosine cultivars or mix with other forages).
- **Timber value**: Black locust (fence posts, outdoor lumber), alder (furniture, turning wood), acacia (flooring, fuelwood), and carob (ornamental wood) have commercial timber value.
- **Invasive potential**: Black locust, Russian olive, autumn olive, leucaena, and mesquite are invasive in many regions. Research local regulations and consider non-invasive alternatives (goumi over autumn olive, caragana over black locust in sensitive areas).

## Integration Strategies

1. **N-fixing overstory**: Plant scattered alder or honey locust above food forest layers. Light-canopy species let 50–70% light through to fruit trees and shrubs below.
2. **Chop-and-drop mulch**: Coppice caragana, leucaena, or gliricidia 2–3× per [[comparison-sea-buckthorn-vs-aronia]] along waterways to filter runoff while adding N to streambank soils.

## Choosing the Right NFT for Your Site

Start with your USDA hardiness zone and annual rainfall, then filter by function. For **food-producing permaculture** prioritizing edibility: carob (pods), honey locust (pods), goumi (fruit), sea buckthorn (berries), caragana (seeds), and inga (pulp). For **pure biomass/mulch production**: black locust, alder, leucaena, and gliricidia are the fastest and highest-yielding. For **silvopasture** (trees + livestock): honey locust, mesquite, carob, leucaena, and caragana all provide fodder alongside N-fixation. For **cold climates** where options are limited, caragana + alder + silverberry form a reliable foundation trio.

Avoid planting invasive NFTs without a containment strategy. Black locust spreads aggressively by root suckers — coppice frequently and consider root barriers. Russian olive and autumn olive are prohibited in some jurisdictions. Leucaena can form monocultures in the tropics if not managed. When in doubt, choose native or naturalized alternatives with similar function.

## Getting Started

For first-time planters: order inoculant-specific seed from a reputable agroforestry nursery (e.g., Tripple Brook Farm, Oikos Tree Crops, or regional native plant societies). Plant in spring after frost, water for the first season, and expect visible nodulation within 6–12 weeks on healthy plants. Test for active nodules by digging carefully around the root zone — active nodules are pink or red inside (leghemoglobin), while inactive ones are green or white. Inoculant can also be applied as a slurry to bare-root seedlings at transplant time. Most commercial inoculants are peat-based and shelf-stable for 12–18 months when refrigerated.

With proper species selection and management, nitrogen-fixing trees form the biological backbone of any productive permaculture system — reducing inputs, building soil, and stacking yields for decades.
