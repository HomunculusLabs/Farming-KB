# How Do I Make Herbal Tinctures, Salves, and Extracts at Home?

Making your own herbal preparations is one of the most practical skills in herbalism. It transforms raw plant material into potent, shelf-stable medicines that preserve the active constituents of herbs for months or years. Whether you grow your own [[holzer-kitchen-garden-herbs-and-medicinal-plants]] or source dried herbs from a trusted supplier, understanding the fundamentals of extraction gives you control over quality, dosage, and ingredients. Home medicine-making also connects you directly to the plants you rely on, building a deeper relationship with your herbal allies and the land they come from.

## What Are the Main Types of Herbal Preparations?

Herbal preparations fall into categories based on the solvent used and the intended method of delivery. The four most accessible for home herbalists are **tinctures** (alcohol-based liquid extracts), **glycerites** (glycerin-based liquid extracts), **infused oils** (oil-based extracts for topical use), and **salves** (oil extracts thickened with beeswax for skin application). Each method extracts different compounds from plant material depending on solvent polarity, making the choice of preparation method important for getting the most from each herb. Water-based teas, vinegar extracts, and powdered capsules round out the toolkit available to the home herbalist.

## Tinctures: Alcohol-Based Liquid Extracts

Tinctures are the backbone of home herbal medicine. They use alcohol (typically vodka, brandy, or everclear depending on the herb) to extract both water-soluble and alcohol-soluble compounds, making them one of the most complete extraction methods available. A properly made tincture lasts 5 to 10 years or more. Their small size and long shelf life make them ideal for building a portable home apothecary, and the dropper bottle format allows precise dose adjustment. Tinctures are absorbed quickly through the mucous membranes in the mouth, making them faster-acting than capsules or teas for many applications.

### The Folk Method (Simplest Approach)

The folk method requires no precise measurements and is ideal for beginners. Fill a glass jar roughly one-half to two-thirds full with dried herb (or pack it full if using fresh herb, which contains more water). Pour alcohol over the herb until it is completely submerged, leaving about one inch of liquid above the plant material. Seal the jar tightly and store it in a cool, dark place. Shake the jar daily for 4 to 6 weeks. After steeping, strain the liquid through cheesecloth or a fine mesh strainer into a clean amber dropper bottle. Label with the herb name, alcohol percentage, and date.

### Choosing the Right Alcohol Strength

Not all herbs extract well at the same alcohol concentration. Roots, barks, and resins contain dense, stubborn compounds that require higher proof alcohol (80 to 95 percent, or 160 to 190 proof). Everclear or high-proof grain alcohol works well here. Leaves, flowers, and aerial parts extract well at 40 to 60 percent alcohol, making standard 80-proof vodka sufficient. Some herbs, like [[query-what-are-the-best-mushrooms-for-medicinal-use]], may benefit from a dual extraction process that uses both alcohol and hot water to capture both alcohol-soluble and water-soluble constituents.

### Weight-to-Volume Method (More Precise)

As your practice grows, you may want to switch from the folk method to the weight-to-volume (w/v) method for more consistent results. The standard ratio used by professional herbalists is 1:2, meaning 1 part dried herb by weight to 2 parts alcohol by volume. For example, 100 grams of dried echinacea root would be combined with 200 milliliters of alcohol. This produces a tincture with a known and reproducible concentration, which is essential for tracking dosages over time and comparing results between batches. Weigh your herb on a digital scale, place it in your jar, then measure the appropriate volume of alcohol using a graduated cylinder or measuring cup.

### Fresh vs. Dried Herb

Fresh herbs contain water, which dilutes your alcohol percentage. When using fresh plant material, account for this by using a higher proof alcohol. Dried herbs are more concentrated by weight and give you more control over the final alcohol content. Many herbalists prefer dried herbs for consistency, while others value fresh plants for their vitality and live enzymes. Either approach works as long as you adjust your solvent strength accordingly.

A useful rule of thumb: when using fresh herbs, use 100-proof vodka minimum. For very juicy plants like lemon balm or fresh peppermint, consider using 190-proof alcohol to ensure the final tincture maintains at least 25 to 30 percent alcohol by volume, which is the minimum needed for reliable preservation.

## Glycerites: Alcohol-Free Alternatives

Glycerites use vegetable glycerin instead of alcohol, making them suitable for children, people avoiding alcohol, and those with sensitive digestive systems. Glycerin is sweet, viscous, and primarily extracts water-soluble compounds. To make a glycerite, combine dried herb with a mixture of three parts glycerin to one part distilled water. Follow the same steeping process as a tincture: 4 to 6 weeks in a cool, dark place with daily shaking. Glycerites have a shorter shelf life than alcohol tinctures, typically lasting 1 to 2 years.

## Infused Oils: The Foundation for Topical Preparations

Infused oils extract lipid-soluble compounds from herbs for use in salves, massage oils, and skin care. They are particularly effective for herbs with anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, or wound-healing properties such as comfrey, calendula, plantain, and St. John's wort.

### Solar Infusion Method

Pack a clean glass jar with dried herb, filling it about two-thirds full. Cover completely with a carrier oil such as olive oil, [[jojoba]] oil, or sweet almond oil. Seal the jar and place it in a sunny windowsill for 4 to 6 weeks, shaking daily. The gentle warmth helps release the herb's compounds into the oil. Strain through cheesecloth into a clean amber bottle.

### Heat Infusion Method (Faster Results)

For a quicker extraction, combine herb and oil in a double boiler or slow cooker set to low heat (below 120 degrees Fahrenheit to avoid degrading the oil or burning the herbs). Simmer gently for 2 to 4 hours, stirring occasionally. Strain while still warm. This method produces a usable oil in a single day, though some herbalists argue the solar method produces a more complete extraction.

### Choosing a Carrier Oil

The carrier oil you select affects both the extraction quality and the shelf life of your finished product. Olive oil is the most accessible and affordable option, with good stability and natural antioxidant properties. It works well for most herb infusions. Jojoba oil is technically a liquid wax rather than an oil, and it is highly stable with a very long shelf life, making it ideal for preparations you want to keep for extended periods. Coconut oil produces a salve that is firm at room temperature and melts on contact with skin, which some people prefer for massage applications. Sweet almond oil and apricot kernel oil are lighter in texture and absorb readily, making them good choices for facial preparations and delicate skin.

### Important Safety Note

Always use **dried herbs** for oil infusions. Fresh plant material, especially garlic and those in the Apiaceae family, contains water that can promote bacterial growth and botulism in the anaerobic environment of sealed oil. If you must use fresh herbs, keep the oil refrigerated and use within 2 weeks. Additionally, label all infused oils with the preparation date and check for signs of spoilage (off smell, bubbling, cloudiness, or mold) before use. When in doubt, discard the batch and start fresh.

## Salves: Turning Infused Oils into Ready-to-Use Medicine

Salves (also called ointments or balms) combine infused herbal oil with beeswax to create a semi-solid preparation that is easy to apply to the skin. They are excellent for wounds, burns, rashes, muscle aches, and dry skin.

### Basic Salve Recipe

Start with 4 ounces of your finished infused herbal oil. Gently heat it in a double boiler over low heat. Add 0.5 to 1 ounce of grated beeswax (use more beeswax for a firmer salve, less for a softer one). Stir until the beeswax is completely melted. Remove from heat and let cool slightly. Add 10 to 20 drops of essential oil if desired for additional therapeutic benefit or fragrance. Pour into small tins or jars and let cool undisturbed until solid. Label with ingredients and date. Properly stored salves last 1 to 2 years.

### Testing Salve Consistency

Before pouring your entire batch into containers, test the consistency by placing a few drops of the warm salve mixture onto a cold plate or spoon. Let it cool for a minute, then test the texture with your finger. If it is too soft, add more beeswax in small increments and retest. If it is too hard, add a small amount of additional oil. This simple step prevents the frustration of ending up with a salve that is either too greasy or too stiff to spread.

### Adding Vitamin E for Preservation

Adding a few drops of vitamin E oil (approximately 1 drop per ounce of oil) acts as a natural antioxidant and extends the shelf life of your salve by preventing the carrier oil from going rancid.

## Decoctions and Infusions: Water-Based Extracts

Water-based preparations are the oldest form of herbal medicine and remain highly effective for many applications. An **infusion** is made by pouring hot (but not boiling) water over delicate plant parts like leaves and flowers, then steeping for 10 to 15 minutes. A **decoction** is used for tougher materials like roots, barks, and seeds, which are simmered in water for 20 to 30 minutes to break down cell walls and release their constituents. Water extracts only water-soluble compounds and have a short shelf life of 24 to 48 hours in the refrigerator, so they are best made fresh.

## Vinegar Extracts (Acetums)

Vinegar extracts, or acetums, use raw apple cider vinegar as the solvent. They are an accessible, low-cost option for those who want to avoid alcohol entirely, and they are particularly good for extracting minerals from calcium-rich herbs like nettle, oat straw, and horsetail. To make an acetum, fill a jar with dried herb and cover with raw apple cider vinegar (with the mother). Seal with a plastic lid or place a piece of parchment paper between a metal lid and the jar, since vinegar corrodes metal. Steep for 2 to 4 weeks in a cool, dark place, shaking daily. Strain and store in a glass bottle. Acetums last about 6 months to 1 year. The taste is sharp and tangy, so they work well blended into salad dressings or taken in small doses with honey.

## Capsules and Powders

Dried herbs can also be ground into fine powder and encapsulated for convenient dosing. This method works well for herbs with a neutral or tolerable taste that are taken in larger doses, such as turmeric, ashwagandha, or spirulina. A small electric coffee grinder dedicated to herb processing makes quick work of dried material. Fill empty gelatin or vegetarian capsules using a capsule-filling machine. While convenient, capsules bypass the digestive signaling that tasting bitter herbs can trigger, so some herbalists prefer tinctures or teas for their more holistic effect on digestion and absorption.

## Dosage Guidelines

Dosage varies significantly depending on the herb, the preparation method, and the individual. As a general starting point for tinctures made with the folk method, a common adult dose is 1 to 2 dropperfuls (approximately 30 to 60 drops) taken 2 to 3 times daily. Glycerites typically require slightly larger doses due to their milder extraction. Herbal teas are often consumed at 1 to 3 cups per day. Always research the specific herb you are working with, start with a low dose, and consult with a qualified herbalist or healthcare provider before using herbal preparations, especially if you are pregnant, nursing, or taking medications. Keep a simple journal noting the herb, dose, time taken, and any effects observed. This record becomes invaluable for dialing in what works for your body over time.

## Drying and Storing Herbs for Medicine Making

The quality of your final preparation depends heavily on the quality of your starting material. Harvest herbs at their peak potency, which varies by plant part. Flowers are typically harvested when fully open, leaves before the plant goes to flower, roots in fall after the plant has sent energy downward, and seeds when mature but before they shatter. Dry herbs in a well-ventilated area out of direct sunlight. A dehydrator set to 95 to 105 degrees Fahrenheit works well for most herbs. Store completely dried herbs in airtight glass jars away from light and heat. Properly dried and stored herbs maintain potency for 1 to 2 years.

## Common Herbs for Beginners and Their Best Preparation Methods

Choosing the right preparation method for each herb maximizes its therapeutic potential. Calendula is best as an infused oil or salve for skin healing and anti-inflammatory action. Echinacea root shines as a high-proof alcohol tincture for immune support. Peppermint and chamomile make excellent teas and infusions for digestive and nervous system support. St. John's wort produces a vivid red oil infusion effective for nerve pain and mood support, and a tincture that is widely studied for mild to moderate depression. Valerian root works well as a tincture for sleep and relaxation. Elderberry is traditionally prepared as a syrup or decoction combined with honey for cold and flu season. Start with three to five herbs, learn their properties deeply, and expand your apothecary gradually as your confidence and knowledge grow.

## Equipment Checklist for Home Medicine Making

Keep these supplies on hand for a smooth medicine-making practice: glass mason jars in multiple sizes, amber dropper bottles for tinctures, small tins or glass jars for salves, cheesecloth or fine mesh strainers, a double boiler, a digital scale for more precise measurements as your practice grows, and labels for every preparation. Clean, sanitize all equipment before use to prevent contamination and extend shelf life. Amber glass is preferred for storing tinctures and oils because it blocks UV light that degrades sensitive herbal compounds over time. Keep a dedicated notebook or digital log to record each batch: the herb, source, date started, solvent used, ratio, and any observations during the steeping process. This practice helps you refine your techniques and reproduce successful batches.

## Safety Considerations and Best Practices

Working with herbs requires the same respect as any form of medicine. Always correctly identify plants before using them, whether foraged or purchased. Use only food-grade or pharmaceutical-grade solvents. Label every preparation immediately with the herb name, solvent, date, and batch number. Never use alcohol that contains additives or is not intended for human consumption. Store all preparations out of reach of children and pets. Research potential herb-drug interactions before combining herbal preparations with prescription medications. Start with low doses and increase gradually while observing your body's response. Join a local or online herbalism community to learn from experienced practitioners and stay current with safety research.

## Building Your Home Apothecary Over Time

Building a functional home apothecary is a gradual process that deepens naturally with practice. Begin with five to ten versatile herbs that address your most common health needs, such as calendula for skin, chamomile for digestion and relaxation, echinacea for immune support, peppermint for headaches and digestion, and valerian for sleep. As you gain experience, expand into more specialized herbs based on your household's needs and the plants that thrive in your growing region. Keep a reference library of at least two to three reputable herbals, and cross-reference information before trying a new herb for the first time. Over the course of a year, even a modest home apothecary can replace many over-the-counter remedies with effective, self-made alternatives.
