# How to Fix Potassium Deficiency in Cannabis

## Short Answer
Potassium deficiency in cannabis causes brown or yellow edges on older leaves, curling
leaf tips, and weak stems that make plants susceptible to pests and diseases. Fix it
by adjusting pH, supplementing with potassium sulfate, kelp meal, or wood ash, and
ensuring adequate potassium levels during the heavy-feeding flowering stage.

## Detailed Explanation

Potassium (K) is one of the three primary macronutrients (NPK) and is essential for
cannabis plants in quantities second only to nitrogen. It regulates water movement
through the plant, activates over 60 enzymes, drives stomatal function, strengthens
cell walls, and plays a central role in the production and transport of sugars and
starches. During flowering, potassium demand increases dramatically as the plant
directs energy toward bud development and resin production. A well-fed cannabis plant
actually contains more potassium than any other mineral nutrient by weight.

Deficiency symptoms typically begin on the older, lower leaves and progress upward
as the plant mobilizes potassium from mature tissue to support new growth. Early
signs include brown or bronze discoloration along the leaf margins and tips, often
described as a scorching or burning appearance. As the deficiency worsens, leaves
may develop yellow or necrotic spots between the veins, curl upward or downward at
the margins, and eventually dry out and become brittle. The [[cannabis-potassium]]
page provides detailed symptom progression charts and photographs for accurate
diagnosis at each growth stage.

Several factors can contribute to potassium deficiency even when adequate potassium
is present in the growing medium. Excess calcium or magnesium in the soil can
competitively inhibit potassium uptake at the root zone. This is especially common
in grows using large amounts of [[query-what-is-cal-mag-and-when-do-cannabis-plants-need-it]]
without compensating with additional potassium. High sodium levels from poor-quality
water or accumulated salts can also interfere with potassium absorption. In
hydroponic systems, potassium deficiency can occur when the nutrient solution is
not changed frequently enough and potassium becomes depleted while other elements
remain in excess. The [[nutrient-lockout]] page explains these antagonistic
relationships in detail.

The flowering stage is when potassium deficiency most commonly becomes apparent in
cannabis. During weeks 3 through 8 of flower, plants are rapidly packing on calyxes
and producing trichomes, both of which require substantial potassium. Growers using
[[living-soil-cannabis]] should top-dress with potassium-rich
amendments like kelp meal, langbeinite, or wood ash at the transition to flowering.
For growers using bottled nutrients, switching to a bloom formula with elevated K
levels during this period is essential. The [[cannabis-flowering-stage-management]]
page covers nutrient scheduling through the bloom phase in detail.

Organic growers have several excellent options for correcting potassium deficiency.
[[kelp-meal-fertilizer]] is a favorite because it provides potassium along with a
full spectrum of micronutrients, natural growth hormones (cytokinins), and amino
acids that support overall plant health. Wood ash from hardwood fires is another
potent potassium source, but it also raises pH significantly, so it should be used
cautiously and only when soil pH is below the optimal range. Comfrey, a dynamic
accumulator that concentrates potassium in its leaves, makes an excellent potassium
tea when steeped in water for several days. The [[comfrey-plant-guide]] covers
cultivation and preparation of comfrey for garden use.

For hydroponic growers, potassium deficiency is typically addressed by ensuring the
nutrient reservoir contains adequate potassium relative to nitrogen and phosphorus.
Many growers increase their potassium-to-nitrogen ratio during late flowering to
support final bud swelling and terpene production. Monitoring electrical conductivity
and pH daily helps catch deficiencies before they become visible on the foliage.
The [[cannabis-hydroponic-systems]] page covers reservoir management best practices
for maintaining balanced nutrient delivery throughout the grow cycle.

Prevention of potassium deficiency starts with building a nutrient-rich soil or
selecting the right nutrient line. Soil growers should incorporate potassium-bearing
minerals like granite dust, greensand, or langbeinite into their base mix. Regular
top-dressing with compost or compost tea throughout the grow provides a steady
supply of potassium along with the microbial activity needed to make it available.
The [[cannabis-vegetative-stage]] and [[cannabis-flowering-stage]] pages both
emphasize the importance of proactive potassium management at every phase.

Environmental stress can exacerbate potassium deficiency symptoms. High temperatures
increase transpiration rates, which increases potassium demand as the plant moves
more water through its tissues. Plants grown under intense light or with CO2
supplementation also have elevated potassium requirements because their accelerated
metabolism drives faster nutrient cycling. If you are pushing your environment for
maximum yield, you need to push your potassium levels accordingly. The
[[cannabis-yield-optimization]] page discusses how environmental optimization
changes nutrient demand profiles.

## Key Points
- Potassium deficiency shows as brown or scorched leaf edges on older leaves,
  progressing upward as the plant mobilizes K to new growth
- Excess calcium, magnesium, or sodium can inhibit potassium uptake and cause
  apparent deficiency even when K is present in the medium
- Potassium demand peaks during flowering weeks 3 through 8, making this the most
  common time for deficiency to appear
- [[cannabis-potassium]] provides comprehensive symptom identification and treatment
  protocols for all growing mediums
- Organic sources like kelp meal, comfrey tea, and wood ash provide potassium along
  with beneficial micronutrients and growth hormones
