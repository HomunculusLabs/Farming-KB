# How Do I Grow Brassicas Successfully?

## Short Answer

Growing brassicas successfully requires cool weather, fertile slightly
alkaline soil, and proactive pest management.
Start with easy crops like kale before progressing to demanding ones
like cauliflower.
Plant in spring before last frost or in late summer for a fall
harvest, use row covers to exclude insects, and maintain a strict 3-4
year crop rotation to prevent clubroot disease.

## Choose the Right Brassicas for Your Skill Level

Steve Solomon's refinement scale is the best framework for choosing
which brassicas to grow.
At the bottom of the scale, [[brassica-growing-guide|kale]] is
closest to its wild ancestor and the most vigorous, cold-hardy, and
pest-resistant brassica.
It tolerates poor conditions, survives to 10F, and produces for months
from a single planting.

Collards and kohlrabi sit in the middle of the scale.
[[growing-brassicas-organically|Kohlrabi]] must be harvested young at
2-3 inches across or it becomes woody.
Cabbage and Brussels sprouts are more refined and require better soil
and consistent care.

At the top of the scale, [[solomon-growing-brassicas-guide|cauliflower]]
is the most demanding brassica with a weak root system intolerant of
heavy soils and heat. New gardeners should master kale before
attempting cauliflower.

## Prepare the Soil Properly

Brassicas are heavy feeders that need rich, fertile soil with a pH
between 6.5 and 7.0. If soil is acidic, broadcast lime in fall at
about 5 pounds per 100 square feet.

The soil should be firm and well-consolidated. Work in 2-3 inches of
finished compost plus a balanced organic fertilizer like blood, fish,
and bone meal. Brassicas benefit from following legumes in rotation,
as decomposing pea and bean roots release nitrogen.

For refined brassicas like broccoli and cauliflower, concentrate extra
fertility directly under each planting station using a raised bed or
hill method.
This localized enrichment supports their heavy feeding demands.

## Time Your Planting for Cool Weather

Brassicas are cool-season crops that perform best at 60-70F.
Most are started indoors 6-8 weeks before the transplant date and
hardened off for 7-10 days before moving outside.

For spring crops, transplant 2-4 weeks before the last expected frost.
However, fall planting is often more successful because fall brassicas
face less pest pressure and produce better quality.
Start fall crop seeds indoors in June-July and transplant in July-
August, about 6-8 weeks before the first fall frost.

[[cole-crop-harvest-and-storage|Cauliflower]] and Brussels sprouts
are especially reliable as fall crops.
Kale can be transplanted 4-6 weeks before last frost.

## Protect Against Pests with Row Covers

Brassicas attract a specialized suite of insects that can quickly
destroy unprotected plantings.
[[query-what-is-integrated-pest-management-for-organic-gardens|Floating row covers]] are the single
most effective pest prevention strategy.
Install them at transplant and leave them on until plants are well
established.

Cabbage white butterflies lay eggs that become green caterpillars
chewing through leaves and boring into heads.
Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt) spray kills caterpillars effectively.
Flea beetles shotgun small holes in seedling leaves but plants
generally outgrow the damage once established.

Cabbage root fly larvae attack roots and can kill young plants.
Brassica collars placed around each stem at soil level provide a
physical barrier.
Cabbage aphids cluster in heads and are difficult to wash out; use
insecticidal soap and encourage ladybugs and lacewings.

## Prevent Clubroot Through Crop Rotation

Clubroot is the most serious brassica disease, causing root swelling
that stunts or kills plants. It persists for decades with no cure.

Maintain a minimum 3-4 year rotation between brassica plantings in any
given bed. Liming soil to pH 6.5-7.0 discourages the pathogen.
Growing seedlings in 4-inch pots until roots fill the container
produces plants robust enough to resist infection.

Choose clubroot-resistant varieties where available, though avoid
planting cauliflower in areas with known clubroot since it is the most
susceptible brassica.
See [[hamilton-brassica-growing-organic|Hamilton's organic methods]]
for comprehensive rotation planning.

## Harvest at the Right Time

Harvest timing directly affects eating quality and storage life.

| Crop | Harvest Indicator | Storage |
|------|------------------|---------|
| Broccoli | Florets tight, blue-green | 1 week fridge |
| Cabbage | Head solid and firm | 2 months cold |
| Cauliflower | Curd full, before separating | 2 weeks fridge |
| Brussels sprouts | Firm, 1-1.5 inches | 3 weeks fridge |
| Kale | Any time, outer leaves first | Months in field |

All brassicas store best at 32-40F with 90-95% humidity in
perforated plastic bags.
Freeze surplus broccoli and cauliflower promptly, blanching first.
Brussels sprouts and kale improve in flavor after frost, as cold
converts starches to sugars.

## Key Points

- Start with [[brassica-growing-guide|kale]] before attempting
  demanding brassicas like cauliflower
- Maintain soil pH 6.5-7.0 with lime applications and provide
  rich, firm soil with plenty of organic matter
- Plant fall brassicas in July-August for the most reliable results
  with less pest pressure
- Use [[query-what-is-integrated-pest-management-for-organic-gardens|floating row covers]] from
  transplant to exclude cabbage butterflies and flea beetles
- Practice strict [[crop-rotation-principles|3-4 year crop
  rotation]] to prevent clubroot
- Harvest broccoli before florets separate, cabbage when heads are
  solid, and [[cole-crop-harvest-and-storage|sprouts after frost]]
- Try Asian greens like mizuna and tatsoi for fast, less demanding
  alternatives to heading brassicas
