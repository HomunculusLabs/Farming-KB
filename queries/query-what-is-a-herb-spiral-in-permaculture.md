# What Is a Herb Spiral in Permaculture?

## Short Answer
A herb spiral is a compact, vertical garden structure shaped like a rising spiral that
grows a wide variety of culinary and medicinal herbs in a small footprint. It is one
of the most iconic elements of [[permaculture-principles]] design, using height,
orientation, and materials to create multiple microclimates within a single feature.

## Detailed Explanation

The herb spiral is a raised bed built in a spiral pattern, typically ascending from
ground level to roughly one meter in height at the center. Stone, brick, or other
solid materials form the retaining walls of the spiral, and the structure is filled
with well-draining soil and organic matter. The result is a three-dimensional garden
that packs a tremendous amount of diversity into a space as small as one to two
square meters. The concept was popularized by Bill Mollison and later featured
prominently in Toby Hemenway's work; see [[gaias-garden-herb-spiral-and-natural-patterns]]
for a detailed discussion of the natural patterns that inspired this design.

The genius of the herb spiral lies in its ability to create distinct microclimates
along its length. The top of the spiral is the driest and warmest zone, receiving
full sun exposure and benefiting from rapid drainage through the elevated soil
column. The base of the spiral, by contrast, is cooler, shadier, and retains more
moisture. The north-facing side (in the Northern Hemisphere) stays cooler and more
shaded than the south-facing side. This gradient means you can place
Mediterranean herbs like rosemary and thyme at the sunny top, moisture-loving herbs
like mint and parsley at the damp base, and shade-tolerant varieties on the cooler
north side. The [[herb-spiral-microclimate-design]] page explores these microclimate
zones in greater depth.

Building a herb spiral is a relatively straightforward project that can be
completed in a single weekend with basic materials. A typical construction involves
marking a circular area roughly 1.5 to 2 meters in diameter, laying the first
course of stones or bricks in a spiral pattern beginning at the perimeter, and then
stacking successive courses upward toward the center. Good drainage is critical, so
a base layer of gravel or rubble is often laid down before adding topsoil enriched
with compost. The stones also serve as a thermal mass, absorbing heat during the
day and releasing it slowly at night, which extends the growing season for
frost-sensitive herbs. For step-by-step building instructions, the
[[herb-spiral-construction]] page provides a comprehensive guide covering materials,
tools, and techniques.

The design of a herb spiral is not arbitrary. It embodies several core
[[permaculture-principles]], most notably the principle to [[use-edges-and-value-the-marginal]].
In a spiral, the edge between any two microclimates is maximized relative to the
area enclosed, creating more niches for different plant species. The spiral also
demonstrates the principle of stacking functions: it provides food, medicine,
habitat for beneficial insects, aesthetic beauty, and efficient water use all in a
single structure. Mollison's own design notes, captured in
[[mollison-designers-herb-spiral-design-construction]], emphasize the functional
rationale behind every dimension of the spiral.

Water management is another important consideration. Herb spirals can be designed
with a small pond or bog garden at the base where the spiral meets the ground.
This pond collects runoff from the spiral above, creating a wetland microzone that
supports water-loving plants and attracts frogs and beneficial insects. The
water then slowly infiltrates back into the surrounding soil, reducing overall
irrigation needs. This integration of water features is a hallmark of thoughtful
[[herb-spiral-design-guide]].

Herb spirals are frequently compared to other compact permaculture garden elements.
For a discussion of how they differ from keyhole beds in terms of shape, function,
and planting strategy, see [[comparison-herb-spiral-vs-keyhole-beds]]. Similarly,
[[comparison-banana-circle-vs-herb-spiral]] contrasts the herb spiral with the
banana circle, a tropical permaculture element designed around a different set of
climate conditions and plant families. These comparisons help designers choose the
right element for their specific site and goals.

When planning your own spiral, consider the overall [[herb-garden-design]] context.
The herb spiral works best when integrated into a larger kitchen garden layout near
the house, since the most frequently harvested herbs should be the most accessible.
It also pairs well with [[permaculture-herb-spiral-design]] strategies that consider
companion planting, succession, and seasonal rotation within the spiral's zones.

## Key Points

- A herb spiral is a vertical, spiral-shaped raised bed that grows many herbs in a
  compact space, typically 1-2 square meters in footprint.
- The structure creates multiple microclimates, from dry and sunny at the top to
  moist and shaded at the base, allowing diverse herbs to coexist; see
  [[herb-spiral-microclimate-design]] for details.
- Construction involves stacking stones or bricks in a rising spiral with a gravel
  drainage base and compost-enriched soil; full instructions are on the
  [[herb-spiral-construction]] page.
- The spiral design embodies the permaculture principle to [[use-edges-and-value-the-marginal]]
  by maximizing edge effect and stacking multiple functions into one element.
- Herb spirals can be compared to other permaculture garden features such as keyhole
  beds and banana circles to determine the best fit for a given site; see
  [[comparison-herb-spiral-vs-keyhole-beds]] and [[comparison-banana-circle-vs-herb-spiral]].
