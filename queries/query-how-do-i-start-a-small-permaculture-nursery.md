# How Do I Start A Small Permaculture Nursery?

## Short Answer

Start a small permaculture nursery by choosing a clear plant niche, building reliable propagation space, tracking seed and cutting sources, and producing healthy plants on a predictable schedule.
A nursery is not just a bench of seedlings; it is a system for propagation, potting, watering, hardening off, labeling, recordkeeping, and distribution.
Focus first on hardy locally useful plants such as perennials, support species, pollinator plants, rootstock, and staple annual starts.
Use [[permaculture-nursery-design]] and [[seed-starting-reference]] as the practical foundation.
The best small nursery grows plants that match local climate, customer needs, and the grower's capacity.

## Detailed Explanation

### Define The Nursery Purpose

A nursery can serve a home garden, a farm enterprise, a restoration project, a food forest, or a community plant network.
The purpose determines what species you grow and how much infrastructure you need.
[[mollison-designers-plant-nursery-establishment]] frames nursery establishment as part of broader site development.
A home nursery can be simple and seasonal.
A sales nursery needs consistent labeling, sanitation, scheduling, and legal awareness.
A restoration nursery needs local genetics, larger numbers, and careful provenance records.

### Choose Species Strategically

Do not try to grow every interesting plant in the first season.
Start with species that solve real design problems in your area.
[[mollison-plant-nursery-species-selection]] is useful for matching plant choices to climate, function, and site goals.
Good early categories include nitrogen fixers, dynamic accumulators, hedgerow shrubs, perennial vegetables, culinary herbs, and pollinator plants.
Annual vegetable starts can provide quick turnover, but perennials may better support permaculture design work.
Local demand matters because unsold plants still need water, space, and care.

### Build Propagation Zones

A nursery needs different microclimates for germination, rooting, potting, and hardening off.
[[mollison-designers-plant-nursery-propagation-bed-design]] covers the logic of propagation beds and protected growing areas.
Seed flats need even moisture and stable temperature.
Cuttings often need shade, humidity, and clean media.
Potted plants need drainage, airflow, and easy access for watering.
Hardening-off areas expose plants gradually to sun, wind, and temperature swings before planting or sale.

### Manage Seeds And Germination

Seed work is the heartbeat of a small nursery.
[[seed-starting-reference]] covers timing, containers, media, and seedling care for practical production.
Use fresh seed when possible and test older seed before relying on it.
Record sowing date, seed source, germination rate, potting date, and losses.
Some species need stratification, scarification, soaking, smoke treatment, or light exposure.
[[jadam-seed-treatment-and-nursery-management]] is useful for natural-farming approaches to seed preparation and early vigor.

### Design Watering And Drainage

Nursery plants die quickly from both drought and waterlogging.
[[nursery-management]] emphasizes routine care because container plants have small root zones and limited buffers.
Group plants by water need so irrigation can be efficient.
Use benches, gravel, pallets, or well-drained ground cloth to keep pots from sitting in mud.
Hand watering works at very small scale, but timers and simple drip or micro-sprayers improve consistency.
Drainage water should not create slippery paths, mosquito habitat, or nutrient runoff problems.

### Use Clean Media And Sanitation

Propagation success depends on clean enough media, tools, containers, and workflow.
Reuse pots only after removing roots, soil crusts, and disease residues.
Avoid bringing pest-infested plants into the propagation zone.
Keep sick plants separate until you know whether they can recover.
Label every tray and pot because unlabeled plants become management debt.
A small nursery can stay low-tech, but it cannot stay disorganized for long.

### Schedule Potting And Up-Potting

Plants should move to larger containers before they become root-bound.
[[mollison-designers-plant-nursery-seedling-production]] connects seedling production with timing and plant quality.
Too-early potting wastes media and space.
Too-late potting slows growth and creates stressed plants.
Make a weekly nursery walk to flag trays that need thinning, feeding, potting, or hardening.
A simple calendar is enough if it includes sowing windows, expected germination, transplant dates, and sales or planting dates.

### Connect Nursery And Seed Saving

A resilient nursery eventually needs its own seed, cutting, and division sources.
[[seed-saving-storage]] helps with maintaining viable seed between seasons.
[[permaculture-designers-manual-plant-nursery]] treats nurseries as living infrastructure that supports site succession.
Save seed from healthy, locally adapted plants when appropriate.
Keep records so you know which seed lots germinate well and which lines perform poorly.
For woody plants, maintain mother plants for cuttings, scion wood, and divisions.

### Start Small And Iterate

The first season should test workflow rather than maximize inventory.
Grow fewer species well instead of many species poorly.
Track which plants germinate reliably, which sell or transplant well, and which consume too much labor.
Improve bench layout, water access, shade percentage, and labeling before expanding.
A nursery becomes easier when every plant has a predictable place and next step.
The goal is a repeatable production rhythm, not a one-time burst of enthusiasm.

## Key Points
