# What Is Fish Hydrolysate and How Do I Use It as Fertilizer?

## Short Answer
Fish hydrolysate is a liquid [[complete-organic-fertilizer-cof-recipe-solomon]] made by enzymatically
breaking down whole fish or fish byproducts into a nutrient-rich liquid.
Unlike fish emulsion, hydrolysate is produced using cold processing that
preserves amino acids, proteins, and beneficial oils. It provides a
balanced source of nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, and micronutrients
while feeding the [[soil-food-web]] uses to build humus, cycle nutrients, and
suppress pathogens. In this way, fish hydrolysate works more like a
pre-digested food source for soil biology than a conventional fertilizer.

From a soil science perspective, fish hydrolysate supports the
[[soil-microbiology-basics]] of how fish hydrolysate works are
fascinating. When applied to soil, the amino acids and peptides in
hydrolysate serve as an immediate food source for bacteria, which rapidly
multiply and begin processing the organic nitrogen into ammonium.
Nitrifying bacteria then convert ammonium to nitrate, which plant roots
can absorb. Fungi in the soil also benefit from the fatty acids and
complex [[fungal-volatile-organic-compounds-communication]], leading to increased [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-colonization-patterns]]
and improved [[arbuscule-isolation-and-nutrient-exchange-mechanisms]] networks. This cascading biological
response is what makes hydrolysate so much more than a simple nutrient
source.

[[ingham-compost-tea-application-rates-and-timing]] and methods vary depending on the product concentration
and the crop being grown. As a general guideline, most commercial fish
hydrolysate products are diluted at a rate of 1 to 4 ounces per gallon of
water and applied as a soil drench every 2 to 4 weeks during the growing
season. It can also be used as a foliar spray at half strength, which
provides faster nutrient uptake through leaf tissue. For cannabis plants
in vegetative growth, fish hydrolysate provides an excellent nitrogen
boost without the salt buildup associated with synthetic fertilizers.

One consideration with fish hydrolysate is its odor, which can be quite
strong immediately after application. Products that are fully hydrolyzed
and stabilized tend to have less odor than lower-quality emulsions. Some
gardeners combine hydrolysate with [[nutrient-cycling-in-soil]] because it represents a way of returning
animal-derived nutrients to the soil in a form that supports biological
activity. In natural ecosystems, fish are a major nutrient pathway —
think of salmon runs that fertilize entire forest ecosystems. By using
fish hydrolysate in our gardens, we are mimicking this natural nutrient
cycling process and closing loops that industrial agriculture typically
breaks.
