# What Is Cation Exchange Capacity and Why Does It Matter?

## Short Answer
Cation Exchange Capacity (CEC) is a measure of a soil's ability to hold and
exchange positively charged nutrient ions (cations) such as calcium, magnesium,
potassium, ammonium, and many micronutrients. Soils with higher CEC can retain
more nutrients against leaching and buffer against rapid pH changes, making them

Understanding CEC also helps explain why sandy soils require more frequent
fertilization than clay soils. Sand has very low CEC (often below 3 meq/100g)
because sand particles are primarily quartz, which carries no electrical charge.

irrigation event, meaning growers must either feed more frequently at lower

increase the soil's nutrient-holding capacity. Conversely, high-CEC clay soils can
hold large nutrient reserves but may make those nutrients less immediately available
to plants, requiring careful pH management and sometimes chelated nutrient
formulations.

The [[ozier-pdc-composting-and-soil-building]] availability is also governed by CEC. Micronutrients
like iron, manganese, zinc, copper, and boron exist as cations in soil solution and
are subject to the same exchange dynamics as macronutrients. In low-CEC soils,
these trace elements are easily leached beyond the root zone, leading to deficiency
symptoms even when adequate quantities were originally applied. High-CEC soils hold
trace elements more securely but may bind them too tightly under certain pH
conditions, making them unavailable through precipitation reactions rather than
simple cation competition.
