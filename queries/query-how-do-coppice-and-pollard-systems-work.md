# How Do Coppice and Pollard Systems Work?

Coppicing and pollarding are ancient, sustainable methods of harvesting wood by cutting trees at the base (coppice) or above browsing height (pollard) and letting them regrow. With the right approach, a single tree can produce useful wood for decades—even centuries—without ever needing replanting.

## What is coppicing?

### What exactly is coppicing?

Coppicing means cutting a tree down to ground level, typically in winter while dormant. The stump (called a *stool*) responds by sending up multiple new shoots from the base. These shoots grow quickly because they're fueled by an established root system. After several years, the shoots are harvested and the cycle begins again. This has been practiced across Europe for thousands of years to produce fence posts, hurdles, firewood, and charcoal.

### What is pollarding, and how is it different?

Pollarding follows the same principle but the cut is made higher up—usually 1.5 to 3 metres above ground. This keeps the new growth out of reach of grazing animals like deer, cattle, and sheep. Pollarding was historically used in wood-pastures and commons where livestock roamed freely among the trees. The result is a distinctive "lollipop" shape: a trunk topped with a crown of straight poles.

## How do trees survive being cut down?

### Don't the trees die when you cut them?

Most broadleaf trees don't just survive cutting—they thrive on it. Beneath the bark, trees carry *dormant buds* at the base of the trunk and along the branches. When the tree is cut, these buds are triggered into growth, producing new shoots in a process called *epicormic growth*. The existing root system continues to supply water and nutrients, so the regrowth is far faster than a newly planted seedling would achieve.

### Does this work for all trees?

No. The ability to coppice or pollard depends on whether a species readily produces epicormic shoots. Most temperate broadleaves do this well. Conifers generally do not—felling a pine at the base usually kills it. There are a few exceptions (some yews and junipers), but as a rule, stick to deciduous hardwoods.

## The Management Cycle

### What does a full coppice cycle look like?

1. **Cut** – Harvest all poles from the stool during the dormant season (November–March).
2. **Regrow** – The stool sprouts multiple new shoots. These grow rapidly in the first few years.
3. **Harvest** – After the chosen rotation length, return and cut again.
4. **Repeat** – A well-managed stool can be productive for many decades, sometimes centuries.

### How do I choose the right rotation length?

The rotation (time between cuts) depends on what product you want:

| Rotation | Product | Typical Species |
|----------|---------|-----------------|
| 1–3 years | Pea sticks, thatching spars, basketry willow | Willow, hazel |
| 5–7 years | Hedge stakes, hurdle poles, bean poles | Hazel, sweet chestnut |
| 10–15 years | Fence posts, small timber, firewood | Oak, ash, sweet chestnut |
| 20–30 years | Larger timber, charcoal | Oak, ash |

Shorter rotations produce thinner, more flexible material; longer rotations produce thicker, heavier poles. You can also divide your woodland into equal compartments and cut one each year for a steady annual supply.

## Species Selection

### Which species work best for coppicing?

- **Hazel** (*Corylus avellana*) – The classic coppice species. Fast-growing, flexible shoots ideal for hurdles, thatching spars, and bean poles.
- **Sweet chestnut** (*Castanea sativa*) – Extremely durable wood, excellent for fencing and outdoor posts. Popular in southeast England.
- **Ash** (*Fraxinus excelsior*) – Strong, straight poles good for tool handles and firewood (note: ash dieback may affect viability in some regions).
- **Oak** (*Quercus robur/petraea*) – Long rotation (15–25 years) for durable timber and charcoal.
- **Willow** (*Salix* spp.) – Very fast growth, ideal for short-rotation biomass, basketry, and riverbank stabilisation.

### Are some species better for pollarding?

Yes. Species with strong, decay-resistant wood work best for pollarding because the elevated cut point is more exposed to rot and weather. Oak, ash, sweet chestnut, and hornbeam are all traditional pollard trees. Willow and lime also work well and were historically pollarded for fodder.

## Getting Started

### How do I start a coppice or pollard system?

1. **Plant or identify** suitable trees. Stool spacing of 2–3 metres is typical for hazel coppice.
2. **Wait** for the trees to establish—usually 5–10 years for planted stock, or longer for natural regeneration.
3. **Make the first cut** in winter during dormancy. Cut coppice stools at ground level; cut pollards at your chosen height above a sturdy branch point.
4. **Angle the cut** slightly so rainwater runs off the face, reducing rot risk.
5. **Maintain** by recutting on your chosen rotation.

### What tools do I need?

A sharp bow saw or pruning saw handles most coppice work. For larger stools or pollard trunks, a chainsaw speeds things up considerably. Keep a billhook handy for snedging (removing side branches). Wear gloves, eye protection, and sturdy boots.

## Integration with Other Land Uses

### Can I combine coppicing with grazing?

Absolutely—this is one of the oldest agroforestry systems in Europe. The key is to keep livestock away from freshly cut coppice stools until the shoots are tall enough to withstand (or be out of reach of) browsing. Temporary electric fencing works well. Alternatively, use *standard trees* (uncut mature trees scattered through the coppice) for shade and shelter while animals graze the understorey between cutting cycles.

### What about combining with orchards or gardens?

Coppiced hazel or willow can provide pea sticks, bean poles, and fruit cage supports for orchards and vegetable gardens. Pollarded trees make excellent landscape features and shade trees without casting the dense shade of an unmanaged canopy. Many smallholders integrate a short-rotation coppice area alongside their main crops.

## Common Mistakes

### What should I watch out for?

- **Cutting outside the dormant season** – Cuts in summer stress the tree, increase bleeding, and invite disease and insect damage.
- **Leaving stools too long** – Over-mature stools become hollow and may decline. Stick to your rotation.
- **Damaging the stool** – Avoid tearing bark or splintering the cut. A clean, angled cut heals best.
- **Overcrowding** – Thin excessive shoots to leave the strongest 5–15 per stool, depending on your product and rotation.
- **Ignoring deer and rabbits** – New shoots are irresistible to browsers. Fencing or tree guards may be necessary, especially in the first year after cutting.

## Coppice vs. Pollard: Which Should I Choose?

### When should I coppice, and when should I pollard?

**Choose coppicing when:**
- The site has no browsing pressure from livestock or deer.
- You want the simplest, lowest-maintenance system.
- You're producing smaller-diameter material (hurdles, bean poles, firewood bundles).

**Choose pollarding when:**
- Livestock graze among the trees and would eat new coppice shoots.
- You want the aesthetic of elevated tree canopies (avenues, parkland).
- You're managing wet woodlands or watermeadows where ground-level cutting is impractical.
- You need to preserve the grass layer beneath the trees for hay or pasture.

In practice, many traditional landscapes used both systems side by side—coppice in enclosed woodland compartments and pollards in open commons and wood-pastures.
