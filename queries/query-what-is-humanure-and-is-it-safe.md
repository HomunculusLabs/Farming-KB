# What Is Humanure and Is It Safe?

## Short Answer

Humanure is human waste that has been composted through a thermophilic process to produce safe, nutrient-rich soil amendment.
When properly managed, humanure composting eliminates pathogens and produces a material that is safe for garden use.
The [[humanure-composting]] page provides the foundational principles of safe humanure management.

## The Science of Humanure Composting

Humanure composting relies on thermophilic microorganisms that generate sufficient heat to destroy pathogens.
The [[humanure-composting-systems]] page covers the engineering of composting toilet systems for pathogen destruction.
Temperatures above 122 degrees Fahrenheit for at least 24 hours are generally sufficient to kill most human pathogens.
A well-managed humanure compost pile will reach temperatures of 120 to 160 degrees Fahrenheit during active decomposition.
The [[mollison-designers-compost-toilet-humanure-systems]] page shows Bill Mollison's design for safe composting toilets.
The combination of high temperature, sufficient time, and microbial competition makes properly composted humanure safe.
Multiple independent studies have confirmed that properly managed thermophilic composting eliminates pathogens effectively.
The [[permaculture-designers-manual-composting-toilet-design]] page covers thermophilic composting toilet design principles.
Composting transforms human waste from a hazardous pollutant into a valuable resource that builds soil fertility.

## Composting Toilet Systems

Composting toilets are the primary technology for collecting and processing humanure safely.
The [[composting-toilet-systems]] page provides an overview of commercial and DIY composting toilet options.
The [[composting-toilets]] resource covers different types including self-contained, centralized, and urine-diverting models.
Simple DIY systems can be built from five-gallon buckets with tight-fitting lids and sawdust cover material.
These bucket systems cost under twenty dollars to build and work well for individual households or small families.
The [[mollison-designers-composting-toilets-and-humanure]] page covers Mollison's composting toilet designs for permaculture.
Urine-diverting toilets separate liquid from solid waste, making composting easier and more efficient.
Urine contains the majority of the nutrient value in human waste and can be diluted and used directly as fertilizer.
Diluted urine at a ratio of one part urine to ten parts water is an excellent nitrogen-rich liquid fertilizer.

## The Humanure Process

The humanure composting process begins with collecting waste in a carbon-rich cover material like sawdust or straw.
Each deposit is covered with sufficient carbon material to prevent odors and provide the carbon needed for decomposition.
The carbon-to-nitrogen ratio should be approximately 30 to one for optimal thermophilic composting conditions.
The [[humanure-composting-systems]] page details the step-by-step process from collection to finished compost.
Full compost bins are set aside for a curing period of at least six months to a year after active composting.
During this curing period, any remaining pathogens are eliminated by time, temperature, and microbial competition.
Most guidelines recommend a minimum curing period of one year for maximum safety assurance.
The [[mollison-designers-compost-toilet-humanure-systems]] resource covers management schedules and monitoring protocols.
Finished humanure compost looks and smells like rich garden soil with no resemblance to the original waste material.
A proper curing period ensures that even the most resistant pathogens and parasite eggs are completely destroyed.
The entire process is aerobic, meaning it relies on oxygen-loving organisms and produces no methane or foul odors.
This is in stark contrast to anaerobic sewage treatment which produces methane, a potent greenhouse gas.

## Safety Considerations and Best Practices

Proper humanure composting requires attention to temperature, time, and management to ensure complete pathogen destruction.
The [[comparison-composting-toilets-vs-humanure]] page compares different approaches to managing human waste safely.
Never use unfinished or partially composted humanure on food crops that will be eaten raw.
This is the single most important safety rule for humanure composting and should never be compromised.
The [[humanure-composting]] resource emphasizes the importance of adequate carbon cover material and proper curing time.
Compost should be turned regularly to maintain aerobic conditions and distribute heat evenly throughout the pile.
Monitoring pile temperature with a compost thermometer confirms that pathogen-killing temperatures are achieved.
A long-stemmed compost thermometer is an essential tool for any humanure composting system.
The [[comparison-composting-toilets-vs-greywater-systems-detailed]] page shows how humanure systems integrate with greywater recycling.
Children, elderly, and immunocompromised individuals should not handle raw humanure or unfinished compost.
When in doubt, extend the curing period rather than risk using incompletely composted material.
An extra six months of curing is a small price to pay for the assurance of complete pathogen destruction.

## Environmental Benefits

Humanure composting prevents water pollution by keeping human waste out of sewers and waterways.
Conventional sewage treatment consumes enormous amounts of energy and often fails to remove all contaminants.
Pharmaceutical residues, microplastics, and nutrient pollution are significant problems with centralized sewage systems.
The [[permaculture-designers-manual-composting-toilet-design]] page covers the ecological rationale for humanure recycling.
Composting toilets use no water, saving thousands of gallons per household per year compared to flush toilets.
The average flush toilet uses over five thousand gallons of clean water per person per year to transport waste.
Humanure composting closes the nutrient loop by returning the fertility in human waste back to the soil.
This nutrient recycling is essential for sustainable agriculture, as human waste contains significant quantities of nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium.
Flushing these nutrients down the toilet creates a linear waste stream that depletes soil fertility over time.
