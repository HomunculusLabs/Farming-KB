# How Does the Nitrogen Cycle Work in a Garden?

## Short Answer
The nitrogen cycle in a garden converts atmospheric nitrogen (N2) into
plant-available forms through biological fixation by rhizobia bacteria on
legume roots, mineralization of organic matter by decomposer organisms, and
fertilizer inputs. Plants absorb nitrogen primarily as ammonium (NH4+) or
nitrate (NO3-). Excess nitrogen leaches away or volatilizes as gas, while
nitrogen is returned to soil through plant residue decomposition, compost
incorporation, and microbial biomass turnover.

## Detailed Explanation

Nitrogen is the most abundant element in Earth's atmosphere at 78%, yet it
is often the most limiting nutrient in garden soils because plants cannot
use atmospheric nitrogen (N2) directly. The [[nitrogen-cycle-in-soil|nitrogen cycle]] is the series
of biological and chemical processes that convert inert atmospheric nitrogen
into biologically available forms that plants can absorb through their roots.
Understanding this cycle allows gardeners to manage nitrogen more
efficiently, reducing fertilizer costs, preventing water pollution from
nutrient runoff, and building self-sustaining soil fertility over time.

The cycle begins with nitrogen fixation, the conversion of N2 gas into
ammonium (NH4+). Biological nitrogen fixation is performed by two groups of
organisms in garden soils: free-living bacteria like Azotobacter and
Clostridium that live independently in the soil, and symbiotic rhizobia
bacteria that form nodules on the roots of legume plants. When you grow
legumes like beans, peas, clover, vetch, or fava beans in your garden, their
root nodules house billions of rhizobia that convert atmospheric nitrogen
into plant-available ammonium. This is why [[cover-crops]] and
[[green-manure-guide|green manures]] based on legumes are so valuable: they literally pull
fertilizer out of thin air. The efficiency of this process depends on proper
inoculation of legume seeds with the correct Rhizobium strain and adequate
soil conditions including pH between 6.0 and 7.0 and sufficient molybdenum
and cobalt for the nitrogenase enzyme to function properly.

Mineralization is the process by which soil organisms convert organic
nitrogen compounds (proteins, amino acids, and nucleic acids) in organic
matter into inorganic ammonium that plant roots can absorb. This is the
primary mechanism by which compost, manure, [[cover-crops]], and plant
residues release nitrogen for crop uptake. [[decomposition]] by bacteria,
fungi, and [[actinomycetes-soil-biology]] breaks down complex organic
molecules, releasing ammonium in a process called ammonification. The rate
of mineralization depends on temperature, moisture, soil pH, and the
carbon-to-nitrogen ratio of the organic material being decomposed. Materials
with a low C:N ratio (like fresh green manures or [[comfrey-plant-guide|comfrey]] leaves)
mineralize quickly and release nitrogen rapidly, while high C:N materials
(like wood chips or straw) can temporarily tie up available nitrogen as soil
microbes use it to break down carbon-rich material, a process called
immobilization.

Nitrification is a two-step microbial process that converts ammonium to
nitrate, the form most readily absorbed by most garden plants. First,
Nitrosomonas bacteria oxidize ammonium to nitrite (NO2-), then Nitrobacter
bacteria convert nitrite to nitrate (NO3-). Nitrate is highly mobile in
soil and prone to leaching below the root zone during heavy rain or
over-irrigation, which is why split nitrogen applications or slow-release
organic sources are recommended over large single doses of soluble nitrogen
fertilizer. In [[cannabis-living-soil-guide|living soil]] systems, the
steady release of nitrogen through biological mineralization and nitrification
provides a consistent supply that naturally matches plant demand throughout
the growing season.

Plants absorb nitrogen through their roots as either ammonium or nitrate.
Ammonium is positively charged and binds to negatively charged soil particles,
making it less mobile but less available in alkaline soils above pH 7.5.
Nitrate is negatively charged and does not bind to soil particles, making it
highly mobile and readily available but also prone to leaching loss. The
[[cannabis-nitrogen]] requirements are highest during the vegetative growth
stage when the plant is building leaf and stem tissue rapidly. During
flowering, nitrogen demand drops significantly while phosphorus and potassium
demand increases. Understanding these changing requirements throughout the
plant's life cycle prevents both deficiency and [[cannabis-nutrient-burn-and-toxicity]].

Nitrogen is lost from garden systems through several pathways that gardeners
should understand and manage. Leaching occurs when nitrate is washed below
the root zone by excess rainfall or irrigation, contaminating groundwater.
Volatilization occurs when ammonium-based fertilizers convert to ammonia gas
and escape to the atmosphere, particularly in alkaline soils or when
fertilizers are left on the surface. Denitrification occurs when
waterlogged, anaerobic soil conditions cause bacteria to convert nitrate
back into N2 and nitrous oxide gases, representing a complete loss of the
nitrogen from the system. Managing these losses through proper irrigation,
maintaining aerobic soil conditions with good [[fungal-contributions-soil-structure|soil structure]], using mulch
to reduce surface runoff, and timing nitrogen applications to match plant
demand ensures efficient nitrogen cycling in your garden.

## Key Points
- Legume [[cover-crops]] fix atmospheric nitrogen through rhizobia
  bacteria in root nodules, adding free fertilizer to your soil
- [[decomposition]] of organic matter by soil microbes releases ammonium
  through mineralization for plant uptake
- Nitrification converts ammonium to nitrate, the most plant-available but
  also most leachable nitrogen form
- Nitrogen is lost through leaching, volatilization, denitrification, and
  temporary immobilization by soil microbes
- Living soil systems provide steady nitrogen through biological cycling
  rather than relying on soluble synthetic inputs
