# What Are Dung Beetles and How Do They Benefit Soil and Pasture Health?

## What are dung beetles?

Dung beetles are a cosmopolitan group of beetles whose larvae and adults feed primarily on feces. They belong to three major taxa within the superfamily Scarabaeoidea:

- **Scarabaeinae** (true dung beetles) — the most diverse and ecologically dominant group within family Scarabaeidae
- **Aphodiinae** (aphodiine dung beetles) — also within Scarabaeidae; many species are small and dwell within dung pats
- **Geotrupidae** (earth-boring dung beetles) — a separate family; often larger, associated with mammal dung and decaying matter

Over 7,000 species have been described worldwide, occupying every continent except Antarctica. They are [[keystone-species-and-trophic-cascades]] in grassland and pastoral ecosystems.

## What are the main functional types of dung beetles?

Dung beetles are classified by how they handle dung into three functional groups:

| Type | Behavior | Example Species | Key Traits |
|------|----------|----------------|------------|
| **Tunnelers** (paracoprid) | Bury dung directly beneath or near the pat | *Onthophagus taurus*, *Copris hispanus* | Dig vertical tunnels 10–30 cm deep; most effective at nutrient incorporation |
| **Dwellers** (endocoprid) | Live and breed within the dung pat itself | *Aphodius fimetarius*, *Euoniticellus fulvus* | Shallow processing; still aid [[decomposition]] [[mollison-designers-fish-pond-water-quality-and-aeration]] |
| **Rollers** (telecoprid) | Form dung balls and roll them away to bury elsewhere | *Scarabaeus sacer* (sacred scarab), *Sisyphus rubrus* | Often pair-bonded; bury at varying distances; reduce surface dung rapidly |

A healthy pasture benefits from all three types working in complementary niches.

## How does dung burial improve [[teaming-with-nutrients-composting-and-nutrient-cycling]]?

1. **Rapid incorporation** — Tunnelers can bury up to 80% of a dung pat within 48 hours, moving organic matter from the soil surface into the root zone.
2. **Nitrogen cycling** — Dung contains ~0.6–1.2% nitrogen. Unburied dung loses up to 80% of its nitrogen via volatilization (ammonia) and leaching. Burial retains 60–90% of nitrogen in plant-available forms (ammonium, nitrate).
3. **Phosphorus cycling** — Dung phosphorus (~0.2–0.6%) is similarly preserved and made bioavailable when incorporated into soil rather than sitting on the surface where it can be washed away.
4. **Microbial activation** — Dung beetle tunnels create hotspots of microbial activity, accelerating decomposition and humus formation.

## How do dung beetles improve soil structure?

- **Soil aeration** — Tunneling creates macropores (2–10 mm diameter) that persist for months, improving root penetration and gas exchange.
- **Water infiltration** — Studies show 2–5× higher infiltration rates in soils with active dung beetle populations compared to compacted, beetle-free pasture.
- **Reduced surface runoff** — Faster dung removal means less nutrient-rich runoff into waterways, protecting water quality.

## How do dung beetles improve pasture hygiene?

Dung pats are breeding grounds for pests. Dung beetles suppress these by:

- **Destroying habitat** — Rapid burial eliminates the moist environment required by horn flies (*Haematobia irritans*) and face flies (*Musca autumnalis*).
- **Killing parasites** — Burial subjects gastrointestinal parasite eggs and larvae (e.g., *Haemonchus*, *Cooperia*, *Trichostrongylus*) to desiccation, predation, and unfavorable soil conditions. Studies report 70–95% reduction in parasite larval survival.
- **Competitive displacement** — Beetle larvae outcompete pest larvae for resources within the dung.

Integrated parasite management programs increasingly recognize dung beetles as a biological control ally.

## Do dung beetles contribute to carbon sequestration?

Yes. By incorporating dung-derived organic carbon into soil profiles (often below 20 cm), dung beetles:

- Increase soil organic carbon (SOC) stocks in pasture soils by an estimated 0.1–0.4 t C/ha/year in active populations.
- Slow decomposition rates at depth, promoting long-term humus formation.
- Reduce methane emissions from surface dung pats, which are a measurable but often overlooked source of agricultural greenhouse gas.

## What is the economic value of dung beetles in livestock systems?

- Australian estimates value dung beetle services at **AUD $2–3 billion/year** across the beef and dairy sectors (improved pasture productivity, reduced fertilizer needs, pest control).
- US studies estimate **$380 million/year** in benefits from reduced parasite loads and pasture improvement in cattle systems.
- Individual farms with robust beetle populations report **5–15% higher pasture growth rates** compared to degraded populations, translating to higher stocking capacity without additional inputs.

## What are the major threats to dung beetles?

| Threat | Mechanism | Severity |
|--------|-----------|----------|
| **Ivermectin and macrocyclic lactones** | Excreted in feces at sub-lethal to lethal concentrations; kills adult beetles and larvae for weeks after treatment | Critical — primary driver of population collapse globally |
| **Other veterinary pharmaceuticals** | Synthetic pyrethroids, benzimidazoles, and tetracyclines show documented toxicity to dung beetles | Moderate to high |
| **Habitat loss** | Conversion of pasture to cropland removes dung supply; removal of hedgerows and trees removes nesting habitat | High |
| **Invasive species** | *Onthophagus taurus* (non-native in some regions) can outcompete native species; invasive dung beetles alter ecosystem function | Moderate (context-dependent) |
| **Climate change** | Drought and temperature extremes reduce beetle activity windows and survival | Increasing |

## What conservation and reintroduction programs exist?

- **Australia** — CSIRO's Dung Beetle Ecosystem Engineers (DBEE) program has introduced multiple *Onthophagus* and *Bubas* species from Europe and Africa to fill seasonal and climatic gaps in beetle activity.
- **USA** — USDA-ARS has released *Onthophagus taurus* and *O. gazella* for biological control of horn flies; several states maintain rearing programs.
- **Europe** — Rewilding and organic farming networks promote beetle-friendly practices; the IUCN lists several Scarabaeinae species as threatened.
- **New Zealand** — Deliberate introductions in the 2010s (*Onthophagus* spp.) to reduce dung accumulation and improve pastoral productivity.

## How can farmers support dung beetle populations?

- **Minimize ivermectin use** — Where possible, use treatments with shorter environmental persistence (e.g., moxidectin has lower dung residue than ivermectin) or treat only during periods of low beetle activity (winter in temperate zones).
- **Time treatments strategically** — Avoid broad-spectrum parasiticides during peak beetle season (spring–autumn in most temperate regions).
- **Maintain dung availability** — Avoid excessive stocking density that compacts soil; keep some paddocks with livestock year-round to sustain beetle food supply.
- **Preserve habitat** — Retain trees, hedgerows, and uncultivated margins for beetle nesting and overwintering.
- **Avoid cultivation of beetle-active paddocks** — Tilling destroys tunnel networks and buried brood masses.
- **Monitor populations** — Simple pitfall traps or dung-baited traps can assess species diversity and abundance.

## Key Takeaways

- Dung beetles are ecosystem engineers that transform livestock waste into soil fertility.
- They save farmers billions annually through improved pasture growth, reduced fertilizer needs, and biological pest control.
- Ivermectin residues in dung are the single greatest threat; pharmaceutical stewardship is the most impactful conservation action.
- Healthy dung beetle communities require all three functional types (tunnelers, dwellers, rollers) for maximum ecosystem benefit.
- Intentional farm management — treatment timing, habitat preservation, and species monitoring — can sustain and restore these invaluable insects.
