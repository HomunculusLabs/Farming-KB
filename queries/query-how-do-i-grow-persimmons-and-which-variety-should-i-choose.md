# How Do I Grow Persimmons and Which Variety Should I Choose?

## Short Answer

Persimmons are among the most forgiving fruit trees a gardener can grow, once you understand the critical distinction between astringent and non-astringent varieties.

Astringent types like Hachiya must ripen to a jelly-soft stage before they are edible, while non-astringent types like Fuyu can be eaten crisp like an apple straight from the tree.

They tolerate a wide range of soils and resist most pests and diseases, producing reliable crops with minimal intervention.

Choosing the right variety for your climate and your palate is the single most important decision you will make.

## Understanding Astringent vs Non-Astringent Varieties

The most important thing to understand about persimmons is that they fall into two completely different eating categories.

**Astringent varieties** contain high levels of soluble tannins that make the unripe fruit mouth-puckeringly bitter.

These must be allowed to fully ripen, often until they feel like a water balloon, before the tannins break down and the flesh becomes sweet and jelly-like.

The [[comparison-tannins-vs-alkaloids-vs-glycosides|tannins]] in astringent persimmons are water-soluble and decrease as the fruit softens through a process related to [[comparison-loquat-vs-persimmon-cultivation|fruit maturation chemistry]].

Popular astringent varieties include Hachiya, Saijo, and Tamopan, along with the native American [[diospyros-virginiana|Diospyros virginiana]].

**Non-astringent varieties** lose their tannins earlier in the ripening process and can be eaten while still firm and crisp.

Fuyu is the most widely grown non-astringent type, prized for its sweet, mild flavor and apple-like texture.

Other excellent non-astringent choices include Jiro, Izu, and the compact Maekawa-Jiro for smaller spaces.

The [[persimmon|persimmon species Diospyros kaki]] is the primary cultivated Asian species, while [[diospyros-virginiana|American persimmon]] offers cold hardiness and a unique wild flavor.

## Choosing the Right Variety for Your Climate

Persimmons are remarkably adaptable, but some varieties perform far better in certain climates than others.

Asian persimmons ([[persimmon|Diospyros kaki]]) thrive in USDA hardiness zones 7 through 10 and need roughly 100 to 200 chill hours depending on the cultivar.

The American persimmon ([[diospyros-virginiana]]) is far more cold-tolerant, surviving winters down to zone 5 with temperatures as low as minus 25 degrees Fahrenheit.

In hot southern climates (zones 9 and 10), non-astringent varieties like Fuyu and Jiro perform reliably because they do not require the prolonged softening period that astringent types need.

In cooler northern regions, American persimmons and cold-hardy Asian selections like Saijo are safest.

The [[comparison-persimmon-vs-pawpaw|persimmon vs pawpaw comparison]] shows that both are excellent choices for cold-climate food forests, but persimmons are generally easier to establish.

When planning your [[backyard-food-forest-design|backyard food forest]], consider planting both a non-astringent variety for fresh eating and an astringent one for drying and cooking.

## Site Selection and Planting

Persimmons prefer full sun, meaning at least six to eight hours of direct sunlight per day for optimal fruit production.

They are not particularly fussy about soil type, tolerating clay, loam, and sandy soils as long as drainage is adequate.

A soil pH between 6.0 and 7.5 is ideal.

Avoid planting in low-lying frost pockets where cold air settles.

When designing your [[backyard-orchard-design|backyard orchard]], space standard persimmon trees 15 to 20 feet apart and dwarf varieties 10 to 12 feet apart.

Dig a planting hole twice as wide as the root ball but no deeper than the root flare.

Water thoroughly at planting and apply a three-inch layer of mulch around the base, keeping it a few inches away from the trunk to prevent bark rot.

## Pollination Requirements

This is where many new growers get confused, because persimmon pollination needs vary dramatically by variety.

Most Asian persimmon varieties are parthenocarpic, meaning they set seedless fruit without any pollination at all.

However, some varieties like Chocolate and Maru require cross-pollination from a male tree to produce properly shaped and filled fruit.

If a pollinated astringent fruit develops seeds, it may have a different flavor and texture, sometimes with a spicy or cinnamon-like quality.

The [[hamilton-fruit-tree-selection-pollination|fruit tree selection and pollination]] guidelines suggest planting a male pollinator if you are growing varieties known to benefit from it.

American persimmons ([[diospyros-virginiana]]) are dioecious, meaning individual trees are either male or female, and you need at least one male for every eight to ten female trees.

If you are integrating persimmons into a [[backyard-food-forest-design|food forest design]], include insectary plantings to support the small beetles and bees that pollinate persimmon flowers.

## Ongoing Care and Maintenance

Persimmon trees are famously low-maintenance compared to most other fruit trees.

They rarely need a formal spraying program because they have very few serious pest or disease problems.

Prune young trees during their dormant season to establish a strong scaffold structure.

Mature trees need only light pruning to remove dead wood, improve air circulation, and maintain an open canopy.

Fertilize sparingly, as excessive nitrogen promotes vegetative growth at the expense of fruit production.

A balanced annual compost application around the drip line is usually sufficient for established trees.

Young trees need regular watering during their first two to three growing seasons to develop deep root systems.

After that, persimmons are moderately drought-tolerant and can survive extended dry periods with occasional deep watering.

## Harvesting and Using Persimmons

Timing your harvest depends entirely on whether you are growing astringent or non-astringent varieties.

Non-astringent types like Fuyu are harvested when they reach full color but are still firm, typically from September through November.

They can be eaten fresh, sliced into salads, added to baked goods, or dried into sweet chips.

Astringent types like Hachiya should be left on the tree until they turn deep orange-red and then allowed to soften further at room temperature.

The softening process, sometimes called bletting, converts the bitter tannins and produces an incredibly sweet, custard-like flesh perfect for puddings, breads, and preserves.

Persimmons can also be dried whole, which concentrates their sugars and creates a chewy, date-like snack.

The [[faires-food-drying-and-no-energy-storage|food drying and no-energy storage]] guide describes methods that work well for persimmons in a homesteading context.

## What About Growing Persimmons in Containers?

Dwarf persimmon varieties can be grown successfully in large containers, making them accessible to gardeners without ground space.

Choose a compact variety like Izu or Maekawa-Jiro and plant it in a minimum 20-gallon pot with well-draining potting mix.

Container-grown persimmons need more frequent watering and feeding than in-ground trees because their root zone is limited.

Move the container to a sheltered location or unheated garage during winter in colder climates.

## Key Points

- Choose between astringent varieties (Hachiya, Saijo) for cooking and non-astringent (Fuyu, Jiro) for fresh eating
- Asian persimmons ([[persimmon|Diospyros kaki]]) suit zones 7 through 10; American persimmons ([[diospyros-virginiana]]) handle zone 5
- Most Asian varieties are parthenocarpic and do not need a pollinator
- Plant in full sun with well-drained soil and avoid frost pockets
- Prune lightly during dormancy and fertilize sparingly with compost
- Harvest non-astringent types while firm; let astringent types soften until jelly-like
- Persimmons have very few pest and disease problems, making them ideal for [[backyard-food-forest-design|food forests]]
- Dried persimmons are a delicious, shelf-stable storage method
