## How to Design [[plant-guild]] is a group of plants deliberately assembled to support a central element — usually a fruit tree — by performing complementary ecological functions. Rather than planting a tree in isolation with grass beneath it, a guild builds a miniature ecosystem around it. Each member provides at least one of the following: nitrogen fixation, nutrient accumulation, pest deterrence, pollinator attraction, mulch production, or ground cover to suppress weeds. The concept originates from permaculture and draws heavily on natural forest-edge ecosystems where plants co-evolved to support one another.

### What are the 7 layers of a food forest?

Understanding vertical layers helps you pack more productivity into less space:

1. **Canopy layer** — Tall fruit and nut trees (standard apples, pears, chestnuts).
2. **Understory layer** — Smaller fruit trees on dwarf or semi-dwarf rootstock.
3. **Shrub layer** — Berry bushes, currants, gooseberries, hazelnuts.
4. **Herbaceous layer** — Perennial herbs, vegetables, and flowers (comfrey, borage, yarrow).
5. **Ground cover layer** — Low creepers that suppress weeds (clover, thyme, creeping juniper).
6. **Rhizosphere layer** — Root crops and bulbs (garlic, daikon radish, [[dynamic-accumulators]] are deep-rooted plants that mine minerals (calcium, potassium, phosphorus, magnesium, trace minerals) from subsoil layers and bring them to the surface through their leaves. ### Which plants repel pests and attract pollinators?

**Pest-repelling plants:** garlic, chives, onions, nasturtium, marigold, tansy, wormwood (use sparingly — allelopathic), rue, pyrethrum daisy, calendula. Alliums repel borers and aphids; nasturtiums trap aphids and repel codling moth; marigolds deter root-knot nematodes.

**Pollinator attractors:** borage (honeybee favorite), lavender, rosemary, thyme, yarrow, echinacea, bee balm, sunflowers, sweet alyssum, phacelia. Aim for continuous bloom from early spring through fall so pollinators always have forage. Include native wildflowers when possible — they [[query-how-to-attract-and-support-native-bee-pollinators]] bee species more effectively than ornamentals.

### How do root patterns affect guild spacing?

Design guilds to minimize root competition by understanding root architecture:

- **Fruit trees** — Most feeder roots are in the top 18-24 inches and extend 1.5-2× the canopy width. Avoid placing heavy feeders within the inner drip line.
- **Shallow-rooted plants** (clover, thyme, creeping herbs) — Safe beneath the canopy; they don't compete deeply.
- **Deep taproot plants** (comfrey, daikon radish, dandelion, chicory) — Mine nutrients from below the tree's feeder root zone, making them ideal guild members.
- **Nitrogen-fixing shrubs** — Place 3-6 feet from the trunk; their roots will intermingle without intense competition.
- **Bulbs** (garlic, daffodils, crocus) — Active in winter/early spring when the tree is dormant; minimal competition.

### How do I design for my climate zone?

- **USDA Zones 3-4:** Focus on cold-hardy apples, pears, plums, cherries, seaberry, haskap, Siberian pea shrub. Guilds need [[cervantes-wind-protection-outdoor-cannabis]] and cold-hardy understory.
- **Zones 5-6:** Broadest fruit tree selection — apples, pears, peaches, plums, cherries, pawpaw, persimmon, elderberry. Comfrey, clover, and borage thrive.
- **Zones 7-8:** Add figs, citrus (hardier types like kumquat, satsuma), pomegranate, loquat, olives. Subtropical guild plants become options.
- **Zones 9-10:** Citrus, avocado, mango, guava, banana. Guild plants include sweet potato (ground cover), pigeon pea (nitrogen fixer), lemongrass, moringa.

Check the USDA Plant Hardiness Zone Map (source listed above) and consult local extension offices for regional variety recommendations. Microclimates — south-facing walls, frost pockets, wind corridors — can shift your effective zone by a full step.

### Example Guild Designs

**Apple Tree Guild (Zone 5-6, ~15-foot circle):**
- Center: Semi-dwarf apple (e.g., Liberty or Freedom — disease-resistant)
- Understory ring (3-5 ft): Comfrey (4-6 plants), borage, yarrow, chives
- Ground cover: White clover (living mulch), creeping thyme
- Bulb layer: Garlic and daffodils (rodent deterrent)
- Nitrogen support: Lupine or Siberian pea shrub at the north edge
- Spring bulbs: Crocus and daffodils for early pollinator forage

**Peach Tree Guild (Zone 6-8, ~12-foot circle):**
- Center: Semi-dwarf peach (e.g., Reliance or Frost — cold-hardy)
- Herbaceous ring: Comfrey (fewer — 3-4, peaches are sensitive to competition), borage, chamomile, nasturtium
- Ground cover: Dutch white clover, sweet alyssum
- Soil breaker: Daikon radish (let bolt and decompose in place)
- Pest support: Garlic chives around the trunk base

**Citrus Tree Guild (Zone 9-10, ~10-foot circle):**
- Center: Mandarin or Meyer lemon
- Herbaceous: Comfrey, calendula, nasturtium, yarrow
- Ground cover: Sweet alyssum, creeping rosemary
- Nitrogen fixer: Pigeon pea or lupine at the edge
- Pest control: Lemongrass (repels whitefly), marigolds

### What are common mistakes to avoid?

1. **Overcrowding** — Too many plants compete for water and nutrients. Start with 5-8 companions and expand as you observe what thrives.
2. **Ignoring mature sizes** — A Siberian pea shrub or seaberry will grow large; give it room or plan to coppice regularly.
3. **Planting invasive species** — Autumn olive, Russian olive, and some clovers are invasive in certain regions. Check your local noxious weed list.
4. **Neglecting pollination needs** — Many fruit trees require a second variety for cross-pollination. Include pollinator partners in your guild plan.
5. **Under-mulching** — Mulch is critical for moisture retention and weed suppression in young guilds. Use chop-and-drop from comfrey, plus wood chip or straw mulch until the ground cover fills in.
6. **Failing to observe and adapt** — Every site is different. Note what works and what doesn't each season, and adjust your guild composition accordingly.

### Where can I learn more?

- **Gaia's Garden** by Toby Hemenway — the best introduction to home-scale guild design and permaculture principles.
- **Edible Forest Gardens** (Vols. 1 & 2) by Dave Jacke & Eric Toensmeier — the most comprehensive reference on food forest theory, [[hemenway-garden-design-process-observation-and-visioning]], and plant palettes.

- **Your local cooperative extension** — offers free soil testing, variety recommendations, and pest management guidance specific to your county.
- **Plants for a Future** (pfaf.org) — searchable database of 7,000+ edible and useful plants with [[comparison-companion-planting-reference-vs-jadam-monoculture-critique]] and guild information.
