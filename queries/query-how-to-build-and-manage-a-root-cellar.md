# How to Build and Manage a Root Cellar

## Short Answer

A root cellar is an underground or partially underground storage space that uses the natural insulating properties of the earth to maintain cool, stable temperatures.

It keeps temperatures between 32 and 40 degrees Fahrenheit with high humidity, ideal for storing root crops and hardy vegetables through winter without electricity.

Proper design includes ventilation, drainage, and humidity management for long-term food storage.

## Detailed Explanation

### Why Root Cellars Work

The earth maintains a relatively constant temperature below the frost line.

This is typically around 50 to 55 degrees Fahrenheit at depths of 6 to 8 feet.

A root cellar uses this natural thermal mass to keep food cool in summer and above freezing in winter.

Combined with proper ventilation and humidity management, this creates optimal storage conditions for many crops.

The [[root-cellaring-and-no-energy-food-storage]] guide explains the physics behind earth-sheltered storage in detail.

Understanding these principles allows you to adapt root cellar designs to your specific climate, soil type, and topography.

Even in warm climates, root cellars can significantly extend the storage life of harvested crops.

### Site Selection

The ideal root cellar site has good drainage, is on a slope if possible, and is close to your kitchen for convenient access.

North-facing hillsides are preferred because they receive less direct sunlight.

The [[root-cellar-design-and-construction]] resource covers site evaluation criteria including soil type, water table depth, and proximity to utilities.

Poor drainage is the most common cause of root cellar failure.

If water accumulates around or inside your cellar, it will rot your stored food and compromise the structure.

Test your site by digging a test hole and observing water accumulation after heavy rain.

If water is a problem, consider drainage tiles, a sump pump, or an alternative site entirely.

### Construction Methods

Root cellars can be built using several approaches depending on your budget and skills.

Options include excavating into a hillside, digging a pit and covering it, building a concrete block structure underground, or converting a basement corner.

The [[underground-house-and-earth-berm-construction]] reference provides techniques for earth-sheltered building that apply directly to root cellar construction.

A simple pit cellar can be built in a weekend by digging a hole, lining it with straw, and covering it with an insulated lid.

More permanent structures use concrete, stone, or cinder block walls with a waterproof membrane on the exterior.

The floor should be dirt or gravel, not concrete, as this helps maintain natural humidity levels.

### Ventilation System

Proper ventilation is essential for two reasons.

First, it removes ethylene gas produced by ripening fruits, which causes vegetables to spoil prematurely.

Second, it allows you to regulate temperature and humidity inside the cellar.

A basic two-pipe system with an intake pipe near the floor and an exhaust pipe near the ceiling creates passive airflow.

This airflow is driven by temperature differences between the cellar interior and the outside air.

The intake pipe brings in cool outside air.

Warm air inside rises and exits through the exhaust pipe.

Both pipes should have adjustable dampers so you can control airflow based on outside conditions.

On cold nights, open the intake to cool the cellar; on warm days, close it to retain coolth.

### Temperature and Humidity Management

Different crops require different storage conditions for optimal longevity.

Root vegetables like carrots, beets, and potatoes prefer 32 to 40 degrees Fahrenheit with high humidity around 90 to 95 percent.

Winter squash and onions prefer cooler and drier conditions, around 50 to 60 degrees with 60 to 70 percent humidity.

The [[permaculture-food-storage-and-preservation]] guide recommends partitioning your root cellar into zones with different conditions.

A simple curtain or partition can create a drier zone for onions and squash.

Monitor temperatures daily using a thermometer placed at carrot height, not eye level.

### What to Store

Root cellars are ideal for storing root vegetables, hardy greens, cabbage, apples, pears, and fermented foods.

Potatoes, sweet potatoes, carrots, beets, turnips, and parsnips all store well for months in proper conditions.

The [[ultra-low-cost-food-preservation]] reference describes how to combine root cellaring with other preservation methods.

Not all foods are suitable for root cellar storage.

Tomatoes, tender greens, and most fruits other than apples and pears require different conditions.

Store ethylene-producing fruits like apples away from vegetables.

Ethylene gas causes neighboring vegetables to sprout or spoil prematurely.

### Ongoing Management

Regular monitoring is essential for successful root cellaring.

Check temperatures daily during seasonal transitions.

Inspect stored food weekly for signs of rot or sprouting.

Remove any spoiled items immediately, as rot can spread quickly in the confined space of a cellar.

The [[canning-and-preserving]] guide covers complementary preservation methods for produce that cannot be stored fresh.

Maintain cleanliness by sweeping floors and removing debris between storage seasons.

Scrub shelves and walls with a mild vinegar solution to prevent mold growth.

Keep the cellar dark, as light causes potatoes to turn green and produce toxic solanine.
