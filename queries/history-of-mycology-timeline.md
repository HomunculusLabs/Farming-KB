# History of Mycology Timeline

Chronological milestones in the scientific study of fungi, from ancient observations to modern genomics.
Draws from history of mycology timeline, [[fungal-evolution]], and entity pages for key mycologists.

## Ancient and Prehistoric Era

- **~7000 BCE — Tassili cave paintings (Sahara):**
  Earliest known depictions of mushroom-headed figures with electrified auras,
  associated with dancing shamans of the Round Head civilization.
  Discovered in the Tassili-n-Ajjer plateau, Algeria.
  Referenced by [[terence-mckenna]] in Food of the Gods.

- **~3300 BCE — Otzi the Iceman:**
  Mummified body found in the Italian Alps carrying two species of polypore mushrooms —
  birch polypore (Piptoporus betulinus) for wound treatment and tinder,
  and tinder conk (Fomes fomentarius) for fire-starting.

- **~1500 BCE — Mesoamerican mushroom stones:**
  Over 300 ceramic mushroom stones from Guatemala's Pacific slope and highlands,
  with the oldest dating to approximately 1000-500 BCE.
  Anthropologists debate whether these represent actual mushroom cults
  or symbolic fertility objects.

- **~500 BCE — Rig Vedic Soma:**
  [[r-gordon-wasson]] proposed (1968) in Soma: Divine Mushroom of Immortality
  that the divine Soma of Vedic ritual was [[allegro-amanita-muscaria-properties-and-pharmacology]].
  This remains debated among scholars.
  See [[amanita-muscaria-guide]] and [[soma-hypothesis]].

- **~300 BCE — [[mckenna-food-of-the-gods-kykeon-and-eleusinian-mysteries]]:**
  Annual initiation rites at Eleusis honoring Demeter and Persephone,
  lasting nearly 2,000 years.
  Participants included Aristotle, Plato, Homer, and Sophocles.
  Wasson, [[albert-hofmann]], and Carl Ruck proposed in The Road to Eleusis (1978)
  that the kykeon potion contained ergot ([[bloomfield-ergot-claviceps-purpurea-ergotism]]).
  See [[entheogen-culture]].

## Classical and Medieval Period (0-1600 CE)

- **First century CE — Dioscorides:**
  Greek physician Pedanius Dioscorides documented [[comparison-chaga-vs-turkey-tail-medicinal-mushrooms-comprehensive]]
  in De Materia Medica, including larch agaric (Fomitopsis officinalis)
  for intestinal ailments and sweating sickness.
  His work remained the standard pharmacopeia for 1,500 years.

- **Medieval period — Mycophilic vs mycophobic cultures:**
  Mushroom knowledge preserved in herbals and monastic manuscripts across Europe.
  Fungi largely classified as "plants without roots" — a misconception
  that persisted until the 18th century.
  A cultural divide emerged: mycophobic cultures (English, Irish, Germanic)
  developed fear of mushrooms while mycophilic cultures (Slavic, Italian, Asian)
  developed rich foraging traditions.
  This divide was documented by Wasson in Mushrooms, Russia and History (1957).

## Early Modern Mycology (1600-1900)

- **1600s — La Quintinie and Paris mushroom caves:**
  Jean de La Quintinie (1624-1688), director of royal gardens at Versailles,
  developed systematic European [[accessible-mushroom-cultivation-for-disabilities]]
  in underground quarries and cellars near Paris using horse manure substrate.
  See [[historical-mushroom-cultivation]].

- **1729 — Pier Antonio Micheli:**
  Italian botanist first to study fungal spores microscopically,
  establishing that fungi reproduce by spores rather than spontaneous generation.
  Published Nova Plantarum Genera.
  This marks the beginning of mycology as a science.

- **1801 — Christiaan Hendrik Persoon:**
  South African-born Dutch mycologist published Synopsis Methodica Fungorum,
  laying the groundwork for systematic mycology and establishing
  the binomial naming convention for fungi that persists today.

- **1821-1832 — Elias Magnus Fries:**
  Swedish mycologist published Systema Mycologicum in three volumes,
  the foundation of fungal taxonomy.
  His classification of agarics by spore color dominated mycology
  for over a century.

- **Mid-1800s — Miles Joseph Berkeley:**
  English priest and naturalist, "father of British mycology."
  Described thousands of fungal species from collections worldwide.
  Established that fungi cause plant diseases, founding plant pathology.

- **1860 — Mordecai Cooke:**
  Published The Seven Sisters of Sleep, treating psychoactive fungi
  (Amanita muscaria) alongside opium, hashish, and other narcotics.
  See [[mordecai-cooke]] and [[victorian-mycology]].

- **1868 — Woolhope Club forays:**
  First systematic fungus forays in Britain, organized by the
  Woolhope Naturalists' Field Club in Hereford.
  These forays became annual events and helped establish
  British mycology as a serious scientific pursuit.

- **1875 — Heinrich Anton de Bary:**
  German botanist established that fungi cause plant diseases,
  founding modern plant pathology.
  Coined the term "symbiosis."

- **1885 — Albert Bernhard Frank:**
  German botanist coined the term "mycorrhiza" (fungus-root)
  for the symbiotic association between fungi and plant roots.
  Foundational to understanding [[mycorrhizal-fungi]] and [[fungal-symbiosis-types]].

## Early 20th Century (1891-1938)

- **1891 — William Falconer:**
  Published Mushrooms: How to Grow Them, one of the first comprehensive
  American cultivation manuals.
  See [[william-falconer]] and [[historical-mushroom-cultivation]].

- **1894 — Julius Palmer:**
  Published About Mushrooms: A Guide to Forays,
  combining foraging guidance with cultivation advice.
  See [[julius-palmer]].

- **1894 — James Cuthill:**
  Published A Treatise on the Cultivation of the Mushroom,
  practical instructions for British market gardeners covering
  bed preparation, spawn storage, and growing methods.
  See [[james-cuthill]].
