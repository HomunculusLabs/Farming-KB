# What Are the Benefits of Growing Calendula and How Do I Use It?

## Short Answer

[[calendula-officinalis|Calendula]] ([[calendula-officinalis]]), commonly called pot marigold, is one of the most versatile plants you can grow in a garden.

It serves simultaneously as a companion plant that attracts beneficial insects, a medicinal herb with powerful skin-healing properties, and an edible flower for culinary use.

Calendula is easy to grow from seed, tolerates a wide range of conditions, and blooms prolifically from spring through fall with minimal care.

Every part of the plant has a practical use, making it an essential addition to [[permaculture]] gardens, homesteads, and even container setups.

## Detailed Explanation

### Companion Planting and Pest Protection

Calendula is widely recognized as an excellent [[companion-planting|companion plant]] for vegetable gardens and food forests.

Its bright orange and yellow flowers attract pollinators and predatory insects that help keep pest populations in check.

Aphids are often drawn to calendula plants, which can serve as a trap crop that keeps them away from your prized vegetables.

This sacrificial role is a form of [[trap-cropping|trap cropping]], where the calendula acts as a decoy plant.

The sticky resin on calendula leaves may also deter certain pests like whiteflies and thrips from nearby crops.

Planting calendula alongside tomatoes, brassicas, and peppers is a common strategy in organic gardens.

### Soil Health and Dynamic Accumulation

Calendula is considered a mild [[query-what-are-dynamic-accumulator-plants-and-how-do-i-use-them|dynamic accumulator]], drawing nutrients from deep soil layers into its leaves and stems.

While not as vigorous as [[comparison-comfrey-vs-dandelion|comfrey]] in this role, calendula still contributes useful biomass when chopped and dropped as mulch.

The decomposing plant material adds organic matter and trace minerals back to the soil surface.

Calendula can also serve as a [[comparison-living-mulch-vs-cover-crops|living mulch]] between larger crops, suppressing weeds and protecting bare soil.

Its relatively shallow root system helps prevent soil erosion without competing aggressively with neighboring plants.

### Pollinator Support

The open flower structure of calendula makes nectar and pollen easily accessible to a wide variety of pollinators.

Native bees, honeybees, hoverflies, and butterflies all regularly visit calendula blooms.

Including calendula in a [[query-how-to-create-a-biodiverse-pollinator-garden|biodiverse pollinator garden]] extends the foraging season with reliable, long-lasting blooms.

Calendula pairs well with other insectary plants like [[yarrow]], dill, and alyssum for maximum pollinator attraction.

Hoverflies attracted to calendula are particularly valuable because their larvae are voracious aphid predators.

### Medicinal Properties

The medicinal use of calendula dates back centuries and is supported by modern phytochemical research.

Key active compounds include triterpenoids (faradiol), flavonoids like [[quercetin|quercetin]], and carotenoids that contribute to its anti-inflammatory and wound-healing effects.

Calendula preparations are most commonly used topically for skin conditions including minor wounds, burns, rashes, and sunburn.

A [[comparison-arnica-vs-calendula|comparison with arnica]] shows calendula is gentler and safer for open wounds, while arnica is better for bruising.

Internal use of calendula tea has traditionally supported digestive health and lymphatic function.

### Culinary and Edible Flower Uses

Calendula petals are one of the most popular [[comparison-scented-geraniums-vs-edible-flowers|edible flowers]] for home cooks.

The petals have a slightly peppery, tangy flavor that works well in salads, soups, and rice dishes.

They have historically been used as a natural food coloring, imparting a vibrant golden-yellow hue to butter, cheese, and baked goods.

Calendula petals can be dried and stored for year-round use in teas and culinary applications.

The flowers also make a visually stunning garnish for desserts, cocktails, and charcuterie boards.

### Making Calendula Preparations

Creating your own calendula products is straightforward and rewarding.

An [[query-how-to-make-herbal-infused-oils-at-home|herbal-infused oil]] is the foundation for most calendula preparations.

Dried calendula flowers are steeped in a carrier oil like olive or coconut oil for several weeks.

This infused oil can be used directly on the skin or incorporated into a [[query-how-to-make-herbal-salves-and-balms-at-home|salve or balm]] by adding beeswax.

For internal use, a simple tea can be made by steeping dried petals in hot water for ten minutes.

A [[query-how-to-make-herbal-tinctures-salves-and-extracts-at-home|tincture]] offers a concentrated extract for longer-term storage and precise dosing.

### Growing Calendula Successfully

Calendula is one of the easiest flowers to grow from seed, making it ideal for beginners starting a [[query-how-do-i-start-a-medicinal-herb-garden|medicinal herb garden]].

Seeds can be direct-sown in early spring or started indoors four to six weeks before the last frost.

Calendula prefers full sun but tolerates partial shade, especially in hotter climates.

It grows well in average to poor soil and does not require heavy fertilization.

Regular deadheading encourages continuous blooming throughout the growing season.

Calendula readily self-seeds, so once established it will often return year after year without replanting.

## Key Points

- Calendula serves as a [[companion-planting|companion plant]], medicinal herb, and edible flower all in one.
- It attracts pollinators and beneficial insects while potentially acting as a [[trap-cropping|trap crop]] for aphids.
- [[calendula-officinalis|Calendula]] contains triterpenoids and flavonoids like [[quercetin|quercetin]] that support wound healing and reduce inflammation.
- Infused oils and salves are the most practical home preparations for skin care applications.
- Calendula is one of the simplest flowers to grow from seed and will often self-seed in the garden.
- It functions as a mild [[query-what-are-dynamic-accumulator-plants-and-how-do-i-use-them|dynamic accumulator]] and can serve as living mulch.
- The petals are edible and add both color and a peppery flavor to culinary dishes.
- Compared to [[comparison-arnica-vs-calendula|arnica]], calendula is safer for open wounds and more versatile for daily skin care.
