# How do legumes add nitrogen to soil?

## Short Answer

Legumes add nitrogen to soil by partnering with rhizobia bacteria that live in root nodules and convert atmospheric nitrogen gas into plant-usable ammonium.
The crop does not magically leak all of that nitrogen into the bed while it is growing.
Most of the fixed nitrogen is first held in the legume's leaves, stems, roots, nodules, and seeds.
The soil benefit appears when roots shed cells, nodules slough off, residues decompose, or the grower terminates the crop as a mulch or green manure.
That is why [[legume-nitrogen-fixation-mechanism]], [[rhizobia-legume-nodule-formation]], and [[nitrogen-availability-in-legumes]] matter more than the simple slogan that beans feed everything nearby.

## Detailed Explanation

### The basic partnership

Legume nitrogen fertility begins with a biological trade between plant roots and bacteria.
The plant feeds sugars to compatible rhizobia, and the bacteria use that energy to run the nitrogenase enzyme.
Nitrogenase turns atmospheric nitrogen gas into ammonium, which can be built into amino acids and proteins.
The physical home for this exchange is the root nodule described in [[rhizobia-legume-nodule-formation]].
The broader plant-microbe exchange is covered by [[rhizobium-legume-symbiosis]].
This partnership is one form of [[biological-nitrogen-fixation-agriculture]].
It is also part of the larger [[nitrogen-cycle-in-soil]].

### Why nodules matter

Healthy nodules are the visible sign that the crop is fixing nitrogen rather than only taking nitrate from the soil.
A newly dug plant may show nodules on fine roots when the correct bacteria are present.
Active nodules are often pink or reddish inside because leghemoglobin regulates oxygen around the nitrogenase enzyme.
White, green, or tiny inactive nodules suggest weak fixation, poor compatibility, or environmental stress.
Nodule formation depends on crop species, rhizobia strain, soil pH, oxygen, moisture, and available mineral nitrogen.
High soluble nitrogen can reduce fixation because the plant no longer needs to pay carbon to bacteria.
That tradeoff is central to [[legume-nitrogen-dynamics]].

### Where the nitrogen goes first

The first destination for fixed nitrogen is the legume itself.
Nitrogen is invested in leaf growth, root growth, nodules, flowers, pods, and seed protein.
A soybean, bean, pea, clover, vetch, alfalfa, or tree legume can contain a lot of nitrogen by the end of its growth cycle.
However, living neighbor plants usually do not receive all of that nitrogen immediately.
Some nitrogen enters the soil through fine root turnover and nodule decay during the season.
More becomes available after residues are chopped, grazed, composted, mulched, or incorporated.
The practical timing question is explained in [[nitrogen-availability-in-legumes]].

### The myth of instant nitrogen sharing

The common myth is that planting beans beside corn directly feeds the corn during the same season.
That can happen only in limited amounts and under specific biological conditions.
Most annual grain legumes direct their fixed nitrogen toward their own seed crop before harvest.
If the gardener removes the pods, much of the nitrogen leaves the bed with the harvest.
If the gardener leaves roots and residues, more nitrogen cycles back into the soil after decomposition.
This is why [[legume-nitrogen-myth-beans-feed-corn]] is an important corrective to companion-planting oversimplifications.
Legumes are excellent fertility builders, but they are not instant liquid fertilizer.

### Species and yield differences

Different legumes fix very different amounts of nitrogen.
Short-season beans may fix modest amounts, especially if harvested heavily.
A vigorous clover, vetch, pea, sunn hemp, lupin, or alfalfa stand can accumulate much more biomass and nitrogen.
Perennial or tree legumes can also support longer-term fertility systems when they are periodically pruned.
The expected range depends on species, climate, inoculation, biomass production, and management intensity.
For crop-by-crop expectations, [[legume-nitrogen-yields-by-species]] is more useful than a single universal number.
For garden planning, [[growing-beans-peas-legume-guide]] and [[growing-legumes-organically]] connect the biology to actual crops.

### Cover crops and green manures

Legumes give the clearest soil fertility return when they are grown as cover crops or green manures.
A cover crop protects soil, feeds microbes, and captures sunlight during a window when the bed might otherwise be bare.
A green manure is terminated so the accumulated biomass decomposes and releases nutrients.
Legume green manures are especially useful before demanding crops such as corn, brassicas, cucurbits, or heavy-feeding cannabis.
Mixtures of grasses and legumes can balance nitrogen fixation with carbon-rich residue and erosion control.
The design logic overlaps with [[cover-crops-green-manures]], [[green-manure-guide]], and [[cover-crop-selection-guide]].
Termination timing matters because flowering plants often contain more biomass, while younger tissues decompose faster.

### Trees, guilds, and perennial systems

In permaculture systems, nitrogen-fixing legumes are often used as support plants rather than short-term vegetable crops.
A legume tree, shrub, or coppiced support species can be pruned repeatedly and dropped as mulch.
Those prunings feed the soil surface, fungi, bacteria, earthworms, and nearby crop roots over time.
This is the logic behind [[gaias-garden-nitrogen-fixers-guild-design]].
Tree and shrub systems also appear in [[permaculture-nitrogen-fixing-trees]] and [[permaculture-plants-legume-trees-acacia-carob-locust]].
Pasture and agroforestry designs use similar cycling when animals graze, trample, and manure legume-rich forage.
The relevant pasture context is covered by [[mollison-designers-legume-shade-tree-pasture-systems]].

### Inoculation and soil conditions

A legume only fixes well when the right bacterial partner is present and conditions allow nodulation.
Some soils already contain compatible rhizobia for common crops.
Other soils need a species-specific inoculant, especially for new crops, degraded soils, or recently imported seed.
Inoculant should be fresh, protected from heat and sun, and matched to the crop group.
Soil pH that is too acidic or alkaline can suppress rhizobia and reduce fixation.
Waterlogged soil limits oxygen, while drought reduces plant carbon flow to nodules.
The inoculation side is linked through [[mollison-designers-legume-tree-inoculation-and-nitrogen-distribution]] and [[biofertilizers-and-soil-inoculants]].

### How to manage for actual soil fertility

Use legumes when you want a biological nitrogen source rather than a purchased soluble fertilizer.
Choose the species by season, climate, biomass potential, termination method, and next crop demand.
Inoculate when the crop is new to the site or when nodulation has been weak in previous plantings.
Let the crop produce enough biomass before termination if the goal is nitrogen contribution.
Leave roots in place whenever possible because roots and nodules decompose exactly where soil structure needs them.
Chop-and-drop residues on the surface in no-till systems, or shallowly incorporate them when rapid mineralization is needed.
Pair legume residues with carbonaceous mulch when you want slower release and better humus formation.

### What legumes do not replace

Legumes do not replace balanced soil testing or attention to phosphorus, potassium, calcium, sulfur, and trace minerals.
They also do not replace compost, mulch, irrigation management, or soil structure work.
Nitrogen fixation can be weak if the plant is stressed, if nodules are absent, or if biomass is too low.
Legumes can even compete with the main crop for water, light, and nutrients if placed poorly.
Their best role is as part of a whole fertility system that includes [[nitrogen-plant-nutrition]], [[plant-macronutrient-functions-nitrogen-phosphorus-potassium]], and soil biology.
Viewed that way, legumes are fertility engines, habitat plants, cover crops, forage, mulch factories, and crop rotations at the same time.
