# What Is Ergot and How Has It Shaped History, Medicine, and Agriculture?

## Short Answer

Ergot is a fungal disease of cereal grains caused by [[claviceps-purpurea]] and related [[claviceps-paspali]] species that produce potent alkaloids affecting humans and livestock.
Throughout history, ergot contamination has caused mass poisonings known as ergotism, influenced religious and cultural movements, and given rise to one of the most important drugs of the twentieth century: [[lsd]].
Understanding ergot is essential for grain farmers, historians of medicine, and anyone interested in how fungi have shaped human civilization.
This page explores the biology, history, medical significance, and agricultural management of this remarkable fungus.

## What Is Ergot?

Ergot refers to the dark, hardened sclerotia (resting bodies) of fungi in the genus *Claviceps* that replace the grains of cereals and grasses.
The most important species is [[claviceps-purpurea]], which infects rye, wheat, barley, and other small grains during flowering.
When you see ergot in a field, the infected grain heads contain dark purple-black growths that protrude from the florets in place of normal seeds.
The fungus produces a range of [[ergot-alkaloid-chemistry-history|ergot alkaloids]] — including ergotamine, ergocristine, and ergocryptine — that are toxic to humans and animals but also medically valuable.
These alkaloids are classified as [[singh-ergot-alkaloid-fungal-secondary-metabolites|fungal secondary metabolites]] and belong to the larger family of indole-derived compounds.
A single ergot sclerotium can contain dozens of different alkaloids, each with distinct pharmacological effects on blood vessels, the nervous system, and smooth muscle.
The composition of alkaloids varies by host plant, as seen in studies of [[ergot-of-barley-and-wheat-alkaloid-composition|ergot on different grain hosts]].
Beyond the toxic species, researchers have also identified beneficial compounds like [[ergothioneine]], an antioxidant found in mushrooms and certain bacteria.
The [[ergothioneine-mushroom-antioxidant|ergothioneine research]] has revealed that this ergot-derived compound may protect cells from oxidative damage and inflammation.

## The Ergot Life Cycle and Grain Infection

The ergot fungus overwinters as sclerotia on the ground or mixed with harvested grain.
In spring, sclerotia germinate to produce tiny mushroom-like structures called stromata that release ascospores into the air.
These spores infect the florets of cereal plants during flowering, particularly in cool, wet conditions that prolong the flowering period.
Rye is the most susceptible cereal because it is an open-pollinated species with a long flowering window that exposes florets to infection for extended periods.
Once inside the floret, the fungus produces a sweet sticky fluid called honeydew that contains millions of conidia (asexual spores).
Insects are attracted to the honeydew and carry conidia to other florets, spreading the infection across a field and to neighboring fields.
As the grain matures, the fungal mass hardens into the characteristic dark purple-black sclerotium that is harvested along with the grain.
Modern grain cleaning equipment removes most sclerotia, but small fragments can persist in flour and animal feed.

## Ergotism: St. Anthony's Fire

Consumption of ergot-contaminated grain causes ergotism, a devastating disease known since the Middle Ages as St. Anthony's Fire.
There are two forms of ergotism: convulsive, which causes spasms, hallucinations, and delirium, and gangrenous, which constricts blood vessels so severely that limbs turn black and die.
Major epidemics swept through Europe from the tenth through the eighteenth centuries, killing tens of thousands of people who ate contaminated rye bread.
The worst recorded outbreak killed over 50,000 people in the Rhine Valley in 857 CE, with similar catastrophic events recurring for centuries.
The [[schultes-ergot-st-anthonys-fire|historical record of ergotism]] documents how monasteries dedicated to St. Anthony became centers for treating afflicted pilgrims.
Modern grain cleaning and food safety regulations have nearly eliminated epidemic ergotism in developed nations.
However, sporadic livestock poisonings still occur in regions where grain screening is less rigorous or in wet growing seasons when ergot pressure is high.
Livestock that consume ergot-contaminated feed may develop lameness, gangrene of the extremities, reduced milk production, and reproductive failure.
The economic impact of ergot on livestock operations can be severe, particularly in years with favorable conditions for fungal infection.
The [[bloomfield-ergot-claviceps-purpurea-ergotism|ergotism literature]] provides detailed accounts of major historical outbreaks and their social consequences.

## The Salem Witch Trials Connection

One of the most provocative theories in American history links ergot poisoning to the Salem witch trials of 1692.
Caporael's 1976 hypothesis proposed that the convulsive symptoms, hallucinations, and bizarre behavior of the accused "witches" were consistent with convulsive ergotism from contaminated rye.
Salem's climate and agricultural conditions in 1691 were favorable for ergot, with a cold, wet growing season that would have promoted infection of the rye crop.
The [[bloomfield-salem-witch-trials-ergot-poisoning-caporael-hypothesis|Salem ergot hypothesis]] remains debated among historians, with some arguing the timing, symptoms, and geographic patterns fit ergotism while others point to social and political factors.
Regardless of whether ergot caused the Salem events, the theory highlights how fungal toxins can produce psychological symptoms easily misinterpreted as supernatural phenomena.
Related theories explore [[mckenna-ergot-and-witches-sabbath-theory|ergot's role in European witch beliefs]] and other folk traditions involving altered states of consciousness.

## From Ergot to LSD: A Medical Revolution

The most famous chapter in ergot's history began in 1938 when Swiss chemist [[albert-hofmann]] synthesized [[lsd]] (lysergic acid diethylamide) from ergot alkaloids at Sandoz laboratories.
Hofmann's [[albert-hofmann-early-career-ergot-research-sandoz|early career at Sandoz]] was focused on isolating and characterizing the active compounds in ergot for pharmaceutical development.
His research into [[ergot-and-lysergic-acid-history|lysergic acid derivatives]] was initially aimed at developing circulatory stimulants from the ergot alkaloid [[ergotamine|ergotamine]].
In 1943, Hofmann accidentally absorbed a tiny amount of LSD through his fingertips and experienced the first intentional psychedelic effects, leading to his famous bicycle ride home.
The dose that produced these effects was astonishingly small — just 25 micrograms — demonstrating the extraordinary potency of ergot-derived compounds.
This discovery launched decades of psychiatric research into LSD-assisted therapy, consciousness studies, and the pharmacology of serotonin receptors.
Ergot alkaloids also gave rise to important non-psychedelic medicines, including drugs for migraines, postpartum hemorrhage, and Parkinson's disease.
The [[non-psilocybin-neurotropic-fungi-and-ergot-alkaloids|classification of ergot among neurotropic fungi]] illustrates how diverse fungi produce compounds that affect the human nervous system.
The [[ergot-alkaloids-eleusinian-mysteries|dual nature of ergot]] — as both poison and medicine — makes it one of the most pharmacologically significant fungi in history.

## The Eleusinian Mysteries and Ergot

Ancient Greek scholars have proposed that the secret potion kykeon used in the Eleusinian Mysteries contained ergot-derived alkaloids.
The [[eleusinian-mysteries-ergot-theory|ergot theory of Eleusis]] suggests that participants drank a barley-based beverage contaminated with *Claviceps* species that produced visionary experiences.
Wasson, Hofmann, and Ruck's influential 1978 hypothesis is documented in [[kykeon-potion-and-ergot-theory|kykeon research]] and debated in [[entheogenic-eleusis-theory-scholarly-reception|scholarly critiques of the Eleusinian ergot theory]].
The [[demeter-ergot-symbiosis-eleusinian-symbolism|symbolic connection between Demeter, grain agriculture, and ergot]] reflects how deeply fungal ecology is woven into agricultural mythology.
The [[kykeon-composition-and-ergot-alkaloid-hypothesis|chemical analysis of proposed kykeon recipes]] has produced mixed results, with some researchers questioning whether ergot alkaloids would survive the brewing process.
Alternative theories explored in [[ergot-amanita-neurotropic-fungi-groups|comparisons of neurotropic fungi]] suggest other psychoactive species may also have played a role.
The [[cordyceps-claviceps-sacred-neurotropic-fungi-mexico|tradition of sacred neurotropic fungi in Mexico]] provides a cross-cultural parallel for how grain-associated fungi became incorporated into religious practice.
The [[ergotized-beer-theory|ergotized beer hypothesis]] proposes a similar connection between fermented grain beverages and ritual altered states in other ancient cultures.
These theories collectively illustrate how deeply ergot has been woven into the fabric of human ritual and spiritual life throughout history.

## Ergot in Modern Agriculture

Modern grain farmers manage ergot through a combination of crop rotation, resistant varieties, and thorough grain cleaning.
Regulatory limits for ergot sclerotia in grain are strictly enforced in most countries, typically measured in parts per billion of ergot alkaloids.
Biological control approaches, including [[ergot-fungi-claviceps-and-cordyceps-as-neurotropic-organisms|research into hyperparasitic fungi]], offer potential for reducing ergot infection in the field.
Fields with a history of ergot should be rotated to non-host crops for at least one year to allow sclerotia in the soil to degrade.
Mowing grasses along field borders reduces the reservoir of wild ergot hosts that can re-infect cereal crops.
The related grass weed [[lolium-temulentum-darnel-and-ergot|darnel (Lolium temulentum)]] is itself susceptible to ergot and can serve as a reservoir for infection of nearby cereal crops.
Farmers using [[plants-of-the-gods-claviceps-ergot-alkaloids|traditional grain varieties]] may face higher ergot risk, requiring careful monitoring during wet growing seasons.
Organic and heritage grain producers face particular challenges because they cannot use fungicide seed treatments and often grow older, more susceptible varieties.
The [[ergot-fungi-claviceps-ergot-alkaloids|agricultural impact of ergot alkaloids]] extends beyond food safety to include feed contamination that can devastate livestock herds.

## Key Points

- Ergot is caused by [[claviceps-purpurea]] and produces potent alkaloids that can be both toxic and medicinal.
- Epidemic ergotism killed thousands in medieval Europe and may have influenced the [[bloomfield-salem-witch-trials-ergot-poisoning-caporael-hypothesis|Salem witch trials]].
- [[albert-hofmann]] derived [[lsd]] from ergot alkaloids, launching modern psychedelic research.
- The [[eleusinian-mysteries-ergot-theory|Eleusinian Mysteries]] may have used ergot-contaminated barley in their sacred rites.
- [[claviceps-paspali|Claviceps paspali]] and other species expand the ergot story beyond cereal grains to forage grasses.
- Ergot remains an agricultural concern requiring careful grain management and quality control.
- [[ergothioneine|Mushroom-derived ergothioneine]] shows that ergot-related compounds can be beneficial rather than toxic.
- Ergot alkaloids continue to yield important medicines for migraines, circulation, and neurological disorders.
