# How to Identify and Fix Iron Deficiency in Cannabis

## Short Answer
Iron deficiency in cannabis causes interveinal chlorosis on new leaves, leaving the veins green while the tissue between them turns pale yellow. It is almost always caused by incorrect pH rather than an actual lack of iron in the soil or medium. Fixing the root cause pH issue allows the plant to absorb iron again.

### Why Iron Deficiency Is So Common

Iron deficiency is one of the most frequently diagnosed nutrient problems in cannabis cultivation, affecting growers across all experience levels and all growing methods. The reason for its prevalence is simple: iron is present in virtually all growing media and nutrient solutions, but it is also one of the most pH-sensitive nutrients. A relatively small upward drift in pH, sometimes as little as 0.3 to 0.5 points, can make iron unavailable to the plant. Because pH drift is common and often gradual, iron deficiency can appear seemingly overnight even in gardens that have been running smoothly for weeks. The good news is that this also means iron deficiency is one of the easiest problems to prevent and correct once you understand the pH connection.

## Detailed Explanation
Iron is a micronutrient that cannabis needs in small amounts for chlorophyll synthesis, enzyme function, and electron transport during photosynthesis. Despite being required only in trace quantities, a deficiency can rapidly impact plant health and vigor. The most important thing to understand about [[cannabis-iron-nutrient]] is that it is generally available in most growing media, so the real problem is usually accessibility, not absence.

The classic symptom of iron deficiency is interveinal chlorosis that appears on the newest growth first, at the top of the plant. Unlike nitrogen deficiency which affects older, lower leaves, iron deficiency works from the top down. The youngest leaves develop yellowing between the veins while the veins themselves remain dark green, giving the leaf a distinctive skeletal or netted appearance. In severe cases, the leaves can turn almost white, and growth stalls noticeably. These symptoms can be confused with [[cannabis-manganese-nutrient]] deficiency since both cause interveinal chlorosis on new growth, but manganese deficiency typically affects a slightly wider band of tissue and may include small brown necrotic spots.

The single most common cause of iron deficiency is pH that is too high. In soil, iron becomes increasingly unavailable above pH 6.5 to 7.0. In hydroponic systems or soilless media, the optimal range is narrower, between 5.5 and 6.0. When pH drifts upward, iron precipitates into forms the roots cannot absorb, even when plenty is present. This is why careful [[soil-ph-management]] is essential for preventing and correcting the deficiency. A simple pH meter or test kit can confirm whether this is the issue.

It is also possible to mistake iron deficiency for other problems. [[cannabis-chemical-burn-vs-deficiency]] is a common diagnostic confusion, especially when growers apply excessive cal-mag products that raise pH as a side effect. Light burn from overly intense LEDs can cause bleaching at the canopy top that resembles iron chlorosis, but the leaf texture and vein color differ. Always check pH and examine the pattern of symptoms across the plant before applying any corrective treatment.

To fix iron deficiency, first correct the pH of your growing medium. In soil, drench with a slightly acidic solution around pH 6.0 to bring the root zone back into range. In hydroponic reservoirs, adjust the nutrient solution to pH 5.5 to 5.8. Once pH is corrected, iron already present in the medium or nutrient solution becomes available again, and new growth should recover within a week. For faster results, [[cannabis-foliar-feeding]] with a chelated iron product at a low concentration can provide a direct temporary supply to affected leaves while the root zone pH stabilizes.

Prevention is straightforward: maintain proper pH at all times, use chelated iron in your nutrient regimen, and avoid over-application of alkaline amendments like calcium carbonate. In living soil systems, maintaining a healthy microbial population helps keep iron and other micronutrients cycling in available forms. Regular monitoring of runoff pH in container grows catches drift before deficiency symptoms appear.

### Differentiating Iron Deficiency from Similar Problems

Accurate diagnosis is critical because treating for iron deficiency when the problem is actually something else wastes time and can make the real issue worse. Several other cannabis problems produce symptoms that look similar to iron chlorosis at first glance. [[cannabis-manganese-nutrient]] deficiency also causes interveinal chlorosis on new growth, but the yellowing tends to be more diffuse and is often accompanied by small brown or tan spots on the affected leaves. Manganese deficiency is also less common than iron deficiency and typically occurs at lower pH levels rather than high pH.

Magnesium deficiency causes interveinal chlorosis too, but it appears on older, lower leaves first and progresses upward, the opposite pattern of iron deficiency. This distinction in where symptoms appear first is one of the most reliable diagnostic clues: if the newest leaves at the top are pale while older leaves below are still green, think iron. If the oldest leaves at the bottom are yellowing while new growth stays green, think nitrogen or magnesium.

Light stress from overly intense LED grow lights can bleach or yellow the newest leaves near the light source, mimicking iron deficiency. However, light-stressed leaves typically show a more uniform yellowing or white bleaching without the distinctive green-vein pattern of true iron chlorosis. The leaf tissue may also feel papery or crisp rather than soft. Raising the light height or reducing intensity corrects light stress but does nothing for a genuine iron deficiency caused by pH lockout.

### Iron in the Nutrient Regimen

Most complete cannabis nutrient lines include iron in chelated form, typically as iron DTPA or iron EDDHA. Chelation protects the iron molecule from precipitating out of solution at higher pH values, making it available over a wider pH range than plain iron sulfate. Iron EDDHA is the most stable chelate and remains available up to pH 9.0, while iron DTPA is effective up to approximately pH 7.5. Using a nutrient line that includes chelated iron provides a safety margin against pH-related lockout.

For organic growers, iron can be supplemented through organic sources like kelp meal, which contains trace minerals including iron, or through compost teas made with diverse organic materials. Some growers add a small amount of iron sulfate to their soil mix as a preventative measure, though this should be done sparingly because excess iron can interfere with phosphorus and manganese uptake. In living soil systems where the microbiome is active and diverse, iron is typically cycled efficiently by soil bacteria and fungi, making deficiency rare as long as pH is within range.

### Foliar Feeding for Quick Correction

When iron deficiency is confirmed and the root zone pH is being corrected, [[cannabis-foliar-feeding]] with a chelated iron spray can provide rapid relief. Mix a chelated iron product at one-quarter to one-half the label strength and spray the affected leaves until runoff, coating both the top and underside of the foliage. Apply in the early morning or evening when stomata are open and the lights are not at peak intensity to avoid leaf burn. Results are typically visible within three to five days as the chlorotic leaves begin to regain green color in the interveinal tissue. Note that foliar iron does not fix the underlying cause, so root zone pH correction must still be performed. Foliar feeding is a bandage, not a cure.

### Long-Term Prevention Strategy

The most reliable approach to preventing iron deficiency is consistent pH management combined with a well-rounded nutrient program. In soil grows, test runoff pH weekly and adjust your feeding solution to keep the root zone between 6.0 and 6.5. In hydroponic and coco grows, check reservoir or runoff pH every one to two days. Keep a log of your pH readings over time to identify trends before they become problems. If your water source is naturally alkaline, consider adding a small amount of pH-down to your plain water before using it, even between feedings. This proactive approach to pH management prevents not only iron deficiency but most other micronutrient lockout issues as well.

## Key Points
- Iron deficiency causes interveinal chlorosis on new growth at the top of the plant, with green veins and yellow tissue between them
- The root cause is almost always high pH, not an actual iron shortage in the medium
- Maintain soil pH between 6.0 and 6.5, or hydroponic pH between 5.5 and 6.0
- Foliar feeding with chelated iron provides fast relief while you correct root zone pH
- Manganese deficiency looks similar but may include small necrotic spots on affected leaves

### Understanding Chelated Iron Products

Chelated iron products differ in their stability and effectiveness depending on the chelating agent used. Iron EDTA is the least stable and effective at higher pH, losing effectiveness above pH 6.5. Iron DTPA is moderately stable and works up to approximately pH 7.5, making it suitable for most soil grows. Iron EDDHA is the most stable chelate available and remains available up to pH 9.0, making it the best choice for alkaline soils or hydroponic systems where pH tends to drift upward. When selecting a chelated iron product, check the label to identify which chelating agent is used and match it to your growing conditions. EDDHA products are typically more expensive but provide the most reliable results in challenging pH situations.

### Iron Deficiency During Flowering

Iron deficiency that appears during the flowering phase can be particularly concerning because the plant's energy is directed toward bud development rather than vegetative growth. New leaves that emerge during early flowering may show chlorosis if pH has drifted, and because the plant is not producing much new foliage during this stage, the deficiency is often limited to a few leaves near the top. Correcting pH promptly prevents the problem from worsening, but severely affected leaves will not recover their green color and will need to be trimmed during harvest preparation. Avoid aggressive iron supplementation during mid to late flowering, as excess iron can affect the flavor and smoothness of the final product.

### pH Drift: Why It Happens and How to Prevent It

pH drift is the gradual shift of root zone pH away from the target range over time, and it is the underlying cause of most iron deficiency cases. In soil grows, pH drift is typically caused by the accumulation of fertilizer salts, the decomposition of organic amendments, or the use of alkaline water. Peat-based soil mixes tend to become more acidic over time, while coco coir can drift alkaline as calcium and magnesium carbonate residues from the manufacturing process slowly dissolve. In hydroponic reservoirs, pH drift is caused by the differential uptake rates of different nutrients: as plants absorb more of certain ions than others, the remaining solution becomes unbalanced and pH shifts.

Preventing pH drift is easier than correcting it after symptoms appear. In soil, mixing dolomite lime into the soil at a rate of one to two tablespoons per gallon of soil provides long-term pH buffering. In coco, pre-buffering with a cal-mag solution before planting establishes a stable starting point. In hydroponic reservoirs, larger reservoir volumes change pH more slowly than small ones, and regular partial water changes prevent the buildup of imbalanced ion concentrations that cause drift. Monitoring pH every one to two days in hydroponic systems and weekly in soil systems catches drift early enough for easy correction.

### Iron and Other Micronutrient Interactions

Iron does not exist in isolation within the plant's biochemistry. Its availability and function are closely tied to other nutrients. Excessive phosphorus can interfere with iron uptake and transport within the plant, a condition called phosphorus-induced iron deficiency that mimics true iron deficiency but cannot be solved by adding more iron. High levels of manganese, zinc, or copper can also compete with iron for absorption sites on root surfaces. Similarly, an excess of calcium, often from over-application of cal-mag products, can raise soil pH as a secondary effect and indirectly lock out iron. This interconnectedness is why blanket supplementation without diagnosis often causes more problems than it solves: adding iron when the real issue is phosphorus excess does nothing to help the plant and may worsen the imbalance. Always diagnose based on symptoms, pH readings, and nutrient history before applying corrective treatments.
