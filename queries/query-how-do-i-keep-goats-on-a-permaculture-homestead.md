# How do I keep goats on a permaculture homestead?

## Short Answer
Keep goats only if you can provide excellent fencing, dry shelter, daily observation, and a clear job for them in the farm system.
Goats are browsers rather than lawnmowers, so they fit best where brush control, hedgerow pruning, dairy, meat, or manure cycling are valuable.
A resilient design combines [[goat-keeping]], [[goat-husbandry]], [[permaculture-goat-raising]], and planned rotations rather than treating goats as low-maintenance pets.
The biggest beginner mistakes are weak fences, parasite buildup, overstocking, and assuming goats can live on weeds alone.
Use them where their browsing behavior solves a real design problem and where you can move them before they damage bark, soil, or infrastructure.

## Detailed Explanation

### Start with the job goats will do

Goats are most useful when they have a specific role in the homestead economy.
Common roles include brush conversion, milk, meat, manure, firebreak maintenance, and educational livestock handling.
[[mollison-goats-and-browsing-systems-in-permaculture]] frames goats as browsing animals that can turn shrubby growth into food and fertility.
[[mollison-designers-goat-management-and-browsing-systems]] emphasizes that this power must be managed with boundaries.
If the farm only needs grass mowing, sheep or cattle may fit better than goats.
If the farm has blackberry, bramble, scrub, or coppice regrowth, goats may be the right tool.
The design question is not whether goats are good animals, but whether their behavior matches the site need.

### Fencing is the foundation

Goat systems usually fail at the fence before they fail at the feed trough.
Goats test gaps, lean on weak panels, climb objects near fences, and follow each other through small openings.
[[goat-keeping-guide]] and [[goat-husbandry-detailed-guide]] both point toward secure perimeter fencing before herd expansion.
Electric netting can work for temporary rotations when animals are trained and power is reliable.
Permanent woven wire or high-tensile electric lines are better for night paddocks and boundary fences.
Gates need the same attention as fence lines because goats quickly learn latch weaknesses.
Good fencing makes rotational browsing realistic instead of turning every day into animal recovery.

### Shelter should be dry, simple, and easy to clean

Goats dislike wet bedding and suffer when they are forced to stand in mud.
A three-sided shed, raised loafing area, or well-drained barn bay can be enough in mild climates.
[[goat-husbandry]] stresses that shelter should protect animals from wind, rain, and chronic dampness.
Bedding should be managed as a fertility stream rather than as waste.
Deep bedding can become compost feedstock when mixed with urine, manure, and carbon materials.
The best shelter site is uphill from runoff, close enough for daily care, and far enough from gardens to avoid accidental browsing.
Shelter placement should support chores, manure collection, and emergency handling.

### Feed them as browsers, not garbage disposals

Goats can eat a wide variety of plants, but that does not mean every weed pile is safe feed.
They need mineral access, clean water, adequate roughage, and energy that matches lactation, growth, or pregnancy.
[[permaculture-designers-manual-goat-management]] treats browsing as a design resource that must be balanced with health needs.
Woody browse, tree hay, hedge trimmings, and managed coppice can reduce purchased feed.
Poisonous plants, moldy hay, pesticide residues, and abrupt diet changes can create serious problems.
Dairy goats usually need more nutritional support than goats kept mainly for brush control.
The feed plan should be seasonal, written down, and adjusted by body condition rather than by hope.

### Rotate to manage parasites and vegetation

Rotational browsing protects plants, soil, and goats at the same time.
Leaving goats too long in one paddock strips bark, compacts gateways, and increases parasite exposure.
[[goat-cattle-integration]] shows one way goats can be combined with larger grazing systems instead of managed in isolation.
Rotations work best when goats are moved before preferred browse is exhausted.
Rest periods let shrubs regrow, break parasite cycles, and give soil cover time to recover.
Mobile water, shade, mineral stations, and portable fencing make small paddock moves practical.
The goal is controlled impact, not continuous access.

### Protect trees, gardens, and infrastructure

Goats can be helpful around woody edges, but they can also ring-bark young fruit trees quickly.
Orchards need guards, exclusion zones, or very short supervised browsing windows.
[[faires-duck-pig-and-goat-systems]] is useful because it compares goats with other animals that have different disturbance patterns.
Goats are not ideal inside vegetable gardens unless every crop is already harvested or fully protected.
Compost piles, feed rooms, greenhouse plastic, and nursery trees should be outside goat reach.
Designing with goats means assuming they will investigate everything edible, climbable, or fragile.
The safest systems place goats where their curiosity is useful rather than destructive.

### Plan health care before animals arrive

Goat health is easier to maintain than to rescue after neglect.
Hoof trimming, parasite monitoring, vaccination decisions, mineral balance, and kidding support should be understood before purchase.
[[goat-husbandry-detailed-guide]] provides the kind of basic care framework that beginners should study first.
Local veterinary support matters because parasites, minerals, and diseases vary by region.
New goats should be quarantined before joining an existing herd.
Good records for weight, kidding, milk, treatments, and moves reveal problems earlier than memory does.
Daily observation is the most important health tool on a small homestead.

### Compare goats with other livestock

Goats overlap with sheep, cattle, pigs, and poultry, but they are not interchangeable.
[[comparison-goat-husbandry-vs-sheep-husbandry]] highlights the difference between browsers and grazers.
[[comparison-cattle-husbandry-vs-goat-husbandry]] helps clarify scale, infrastructure, and forage differences.
Goats are often better for brush and small-scale dairy than cattle.
Sheep may be better for grass-dominant pasture.
Poultry may be better for insect control and garden fertility cycling.
A good permaculture design chooses animals by function rather than by charm.

## Key Points
- Use [[goat-keeping]] as a starting point for basic housing, feeding, and daily care.
- Study [[goat-husbandry]] before buying animals, not after problems appear.
- Treat [[permaculture-goat-raising]] as a design pattern for fertility, browse, and labor.
- Use [[mollison-designers-goat-management-and-browsing-systems]] to think about boundaries and controlled browsing.
- Compare goats with sheep through [[comparison-goat-husbandry-vs-sheep-husbandry]] when the site is mostly grass.
- Compare goats with cattle through [[comparison-cattle-husbandry-vs-goat-husbandry]] when land base and infrastructure are limiting.
- Consider [[goat-cattle-integration]] only after the basic goat system is stable.
- Remember that secure fencing is animal welfare, labor savings, and crop protection at the same time.
- Keep stocking rates conservative until forage recovery and parasite pressure are known.
- Design goats into the farm as browsers with responsibilities, not as weed-eating machines.
