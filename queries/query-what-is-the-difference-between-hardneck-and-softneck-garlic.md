# What Is the Difference Between Hardneck and Softneck Garlic?

## Short Answer

Hardneck garlic ([[allium-sativum-entity]] var. ophioscorodon) produces a stiff
central flowering stalk called a scape, has fewer but larger cloves with
richer flavor, and grows best in [[query-best-kiwifruit-varieties-for-cold-climates]].
Softneck garlic (Allium sativum var. sativum) lacks a stiff central stalk,
has more but smaller cloves, stores much longer, and tolerates milder
winters.

## Clove Structure and Size

Hardneck garlic heads typically contain 4 to 12 cloves arranged in a
single ring around the central stalk. The cloves are large, easy to peel,
and have a distinct crescent shape. Because there are fewer cloves, each
one carries more weight, making hardneck garlic efficient for cooking
when you want bold garlic flavor from fewer cloves.

Softneck garlic heads hold 12 to 20 or more cloves arranged in multiple
concentric layers. The individual cloves are smaller and can be harder to
peel, but you get more cloves per head. The inner cloves may be quite
tiny, which some cooks find inconvenient. Artichoke-type softnecks have
irregularly sized cloves, while Silverskin types are more uniform.

## Flavor Profiles

Hardneck garlic is generally considered to have richer, more complex, and
more nuanced flavor. Rocambole varieties are prized by chefs for their
deep, earthy taste. Porcelain types like Music offer bold heat. Purple
Stripe varieties are excellent for roasting, developing sweet caramelized
notes. The flavor is often described as "garlicky" in a way that
softneck sometimes is not.

Softneck garlic tends to be milder, sometimes sharper, and less complex.
Silverskin varieties are the strongest-flavored softnecks but still lack
the depth of hardneck types. The milder flavor profile makes softneck
popular for raw applications where a subtler garlic presence is desired.

## Scapes

One of the unique features of hardneck garlic is the scape, a coiling
flowering stalk that emerges in late spring. These scapes are edible and
highly valued in cooking. They taste like a cross between garlic and
scallions and are excellent in stir-fries, pesto, soups, and grilled
removing scapes also redirects the plant's energy into bulb development,
typically increasing bulb size by 15 to 25 percent. Softneck garlic does
not produce scapes.

## Storage Duration

This is where softneck garlic clearly excels. Softneck varieties
typically store for 6 to 9 months under proper conditions, and
Silverskin types can last up to 12 months. Their tight, multi-layered
wrapper structure protects the cloves from moisture loss and disease.

Hardneck garlic stores for 4 to 6 months at most. The single ring of
cloves wrapped in fewer protective layers means the heads dry out and
deteriorate faster. Rocambole types are the shortest keepers at just 3
to 4 months. This means hardneck garlic should be eaten first.

## Growing Differences

Planting and care are similar for both types. Hardneck garlic should be
planted slightly deeper, about 3 inches, compared to 1 to 2 inches for
softneck. Spacing is 6 inches apart for hardneck and 4 inches for the
smaller softneck cloves.

Hardneck garlic benefits from removing scapes in late spring once they
have made one to two complete coils. This is an additional maintenance
step that softneck growers do not need. However, the scapes themselves
are a bonus crop that softneck cannot provide.
