# How Do I Make Compost Tea?

## Short Answer

Compost tea is a liquid extract made by steeping high-quality compost in aerated water, sometimes with added nutrients, to multiply beneficial microorganisms.
The most common and effective form is [[actively-aerated-compost-tea|Actively Aerated Compost Tea (AACT)]], which uses an air pump to maintain dissolved oxygen above 6 mg/L throughout the brew.
A typical AACT brew takes 24-36 hours and requires quality compost, dechlorinated water, an air pump, a brewing vessel, and food sources for the microbes.
The goal is to produce a dense suspension of bacteria, fungi, protozoa, and nematodes that can be applied as a soil drench or foliar spray.
Good compost tea can dramatically improve soil biology, suppress plant diseases, and boost nutrient cycling in gardens, farms, and cannabis grows.

## What Is Compost Tea?

Compost tea is fundamentally a way to take the microbial life found in good compost and multiply it into a liquid form that is easy to apply over large areas.
There are several distinct types of compost tea, each with different production methods and microbial profiles.
Understanding the differences is essential before you start brewing.

| Type | Aeration | Brew Time | Microbial Profile |
|------|----------|-----------|-------------------|
| AACT (actively aerated) | Continuous air pump | 24-36 hours | High bacteria and fungi |
| Passive tea | No aeration | 3-14 days | Variable, often anaerobic |
| Fermentative tea | No aeration, added sugar | 2-7 days | Bacteria dominant, some LAB |
| Extract tea | Short extraction, minimal brew | 1-4 hours | Organisms from compost only |

The [[aerobic-vs-fermentative-compost-tea|aerobic vs. fermentative compost tea]] distinction is important because anaerobic conditions can produce compounds toxic to plants.
Most growers prefer AACT because it reliably produces diverse, aerobic microbial populations that are beneficial rather than harmful.

## Choosing the Right Compost

The quality of your starting compost is the single most important factor in making effective compost tea.
Even the best brewing equipment and ingredients cannot compensate for compost that lacks microbial diversity.

The [[aact-compost-quality-standards-ingham|compost quality standards]] established by the Soil Foodweb Institute provide clear benchmarks.
Your compost should be mature, dark, crumbly, and earthy-smelling with no traces of ammonia or sour odors.
It should have been through a proper hot composting process that reached temperatures above 131°F (55°C) for at least three days to kill pathogens.
Worm castings are another excellent base material that can be used alone or blended with compost.
For the highest fungal diversity, look for compost made with woody materials, leaf mold, or fungal-dominated piles.

## Essential Brewing Equipment

A basic AACT setup requires only a few components, but each one plays a critical role.
The brewing vessel can be anything from a 5-gallon bucket to a 55-gallon drum, provided it is food-grade and clean.
The air pump must be sized appropriately for your vessel volume — a general rule is 0.05 CFM per gallon of tea.
Air stones or diffusers break the airflow into fine bubbles, which dramatically improves oxygen transfer compared to open tubing.
A mesh bag (200-400 micron) holds the compost and food sources while allowing microorganisms to pass into the surrounding water.
A dissolved oxygen meter is not strictly required but is highly recommended for monitoring brew health.
The [[actively-aerated-compost-tea|AACT brewing]] process depends on maintaining oxygen levels above 6 mg/L throughout the entire brew cycle.

## Feeding the Microbes

Microorganisms in compost tea need food to multiply during the brew cycle.
The [[aact-brewing-ingredients-ingham|brewing ingredients]] you choose directly influence which microbial groups thrive.
Bacterial foods include simple sugars like molasses, fish hydrolysate, and kelp meal.
These ingredients promote rapid bacterial growth and are ideal for annual vegetables, grasses, and cannabis during the vegetative stage.
Fungal foods include more complex carbohydrates like humic acids, fish bone meal, oatmeal, and rock dust.
These support the growth of beneficial fungi, which are especially important for perennials, trees, and cannabis during flowering.
A balanced approach uses both types of food to create a diverse microbial community.

## Bacterial vs. Fungal Dominant Teas

You can steer your compost tea toward either a bacterial-dominant or fungal-dominant profile depending on what you are growing.
The [[aact-bacterial-dominant-tea|bacterial-dominant tea]] uses simple sugars (especially unsulfured molasses) as the primary food source.
Bacterial teas are ideal for brassicas, leafy greens, and cannabis in early vegetative growth.
They are also effective as foliar sprays for disease suppression on leaf surfaces.
The [[aact-fungal-dominant-tea|fungal-dominant tea]] relies on complex food sources and often includes small amounts of compost from woody, fungal-dominated piles.
Fungal teas benefit perennials, fruit trees, shrubs, and cannabis during the flowering phase when root zone fungal associations are most important.
Most growers alternate between the two or blend them depending on the crop and growth stage.

## The Brewing Process Step by Step

Start by filling your brewing vessel with dechlorinated water at room temperature.
Chlorine in tap water will kill the very microorganisms you are trying to grow, so let the water sit for 24 hours or use a chlorine filter.
Add the air pump and diffusers, then turn on the pump and verify vigorous bubbling before adding any organic materials.
Place your compost (approximately 1 cup per 5 gallons) into the mesh bag along with any solid food sources.
Submerge the bag in the water and secure it so it does not block the air diffusers.
Add liquid food sources like molasses or kelp extract directly to the water.
Maintain the brew at ambient temperature between 65-80°F (18-27°C) for 24-36 hours.
If the tea develops a sour or putrid smell at any point, the brew has gone anaerobic and should be discarded immediately.

## Application Methods

Compost tea can be applied as a soil drench or as a foliar spray, and each method serves a different purpose.
As a soil drench, tea inoculates the root zone with beneficial organisms that improve nutrient cycling and suppress soil-borne pathogens.
Apply soil drenches at a rate of roughly 5 gallons per 1,000 square feet, or 1-2 cups per potted plant.
As a foliar spray, tea coats leaf surfaces with protective bacteria that compete with pathogenic organisms for space and nutrients.
The [[aact-disease-suppression-mechanisms-ingham|disease suppression mechanisms]] of compost tea include competitive exclusion, antibiosis, and induced systemic resistance in plants.
For foliar application, strain the tea through a fine mesh (400 microns or less) to prevent clogging spray equipment.
Apply foliar teas in the early morning or late evening to avoid UV damage to the microorganisms.
Always use compost tea within 4 hours of removing the aeration to prevent the microbial community from shifting anaerobic.

## Common Problems and Troubleshooting

Even experienced brewers encounter problems from time to time.
The [[aact-troubleshooting-ingham|AACT troubleshooting guide]] covers the most frequent issues in detail.
A foul smell (rotten egg, sewage, or sour vomit) indicates anaerobic conditions — this tea should not be applied to plants.
Foam on the surface is generally a sign of healthy bacterial activity, but excessive foam may indicate too much protein or surfactant in the brew.
If your tea lacks foam entirely, the microbial population may be too low, which often points to poor compost quality or insufficient food.
Temperature extremes slow microbial growth and can cause the tea to stall.
Brown or discolored tea is normal, but black tea with a septic smell has gone bad.
Always trust your nose — healthy compost tea has an earthy, sweet, or slightly yeasty aroma.

## Key Points

- High-quality compost is the foundation of effective compost tea — no ingredient can compensate for poor starting material ([[aact-compost-quality-standards-ingham]])
- Actively Aerated Compost Tea (AACT) with continuous aeration is the safest and most reliable brewing method ([[actively-aerated-compost-tea]])
- Bacterial foods like molasses promote bacterial growth; fungal foods like humic acids and oatmeal promote fungal growth ([[aact-brewing-ingredients-ingham]])
- Always dechlorinate your water before brewing — chlorine kills beneficial microorganisms on contact
- Apply tea within 4 hours of removing aeration to prevent anaerobic shifts and loss of efficacy
- Compost tea suppresses plant diseases through competitive exclusion, antibiosis, and induced resistance ([[aact-disease-suppression-mechanisms-ingham]])
- A foul smell means the brew has gone anaerobic — discard it immediately rather than risk harming your plants ([[aact-troubleshooting-ingham]])
- Balance bacterial and fungal teas based on crop type and growth stage for best results ([[aact-bacterial-dominant-tea]])
