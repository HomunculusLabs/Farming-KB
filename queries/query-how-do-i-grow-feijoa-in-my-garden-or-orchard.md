## How do I grow feijoa in my garden or orchard?

## Short Answer

Feijoa (Acca sellowiana), also known as pineapple guava, is an evergreen shrub or small tree that produces aromatic, egg-shaped fruit with a sweet-tart flavor reminiscent of pineapple, strawberry, and guava.
It thrives in USDA zones 8-11, tolerates light frost, and is remarkably drought-resistant once established, making it an excellent choice for Mediterranean and subtropical gardens.
With proper variety selection, cross-pollination, and basic care, you can expect reliable harvests of 20-50 kg of fruit per mature tree each season.

## Detailed Explanation

### Climate and Hardiness

Feijoa is native to southern Brazil, Uruguay, Paraguay, and northern Argentina, where it grows in subtropical highland conditions.
The plant is remarkably adaptable and survives temperatures as low as -10°C (14°F) when fully dormant, though flowers and young fruit are damaged below -4°C (25°F).
It grows best in regions with cool winters and warm summers, performing exceptionally well in Mediterranean climates like those of New Zealand, coastal California, and parts of Australia.
Hot, humid tropical lowlands are not ideal — feijoa prefers some winter chill (100-200 hours below 7°C) for optimal flowering.
See [[feijoa-and-ugni-cool-climate-myrtaceous-fruits]] for a comparison with its cool-climate myrtaceous relative, the Chilean guava.

### Variety Selection

Dozens of named cultivars exist, bred primarily in New Zealand and California for fruit size, flavor, and yield.
Popular varieties include 'Apollo' (large fruit, self-fertile but better with cross-pollination), 'Gemini' (early-season, excellent flavor), 'Mammoth' (very large fruit from New Zealand), and 'Unique' (reliably self-fertile).
For best fruit set, plant at least two different cultivars that flower at the same time — cross-pollination significantly increases fruit size and yield.
Some varieties like 'Coolidge' and 'Unique' are partially self-fertile, but even these produce more and larger fruit when cross-pollinated.
The species [[feijoa-sellowiana]] describes the wild type from which all cultivated varieties derive.

### Site Selection and Soil Preparation

Feijoas prefer full sun to partial shade, with at least 6 hours of direct sunlight for optimal fruit production.
They tolerate a wide range of soil types but grow best in well-drained, slightly acidic to neutral soil (pH 5.5-7.0).
Heavy clay soils should be improved with organic matter and drainage amendments — see [[soil-ph]] for pH testing and adjustment guidance.
Space trees 3-5 meters apart for a hedge or orchard row, or 5-7 meters apart as individual specimen trees.
Prepare planting holes twice the width of the root ball and incorporate compost, but avoid placing concentrated fertilizer directly against the roots.
Good site selection also supports [[backyard-orchard-design]] principles for long-term productivity.

### Planting and Establishment

Plant feijoas in spring after frost danger has passed, or in autumn in mild-winter regions where the soil stays warm enough for root growth.
Container-grown plants transplant readily, but water them deeply before planting and avoid disturbing the root ball excessively.
Mulch the root zone with 5-10 cm of organic mulch, keeping it a few centimeters away from the trunk to prevent rot.
[[chop-and-drop-mulching]] with pruned branches and leaf litter is a low-maintenance way to maintain the mulch layer sustainably.
Water newly planted trees deeply once or twice per week for the first growing season.
Reduce watering frequency in the second year as roots establish, transitioning to deep, infrequent irrigation.

### Watering and Fertilizing

Once established, feijoas are notably drought-tolerant, making them suitable for [[drought-tolerant-planting-design]] schemes.
However, consistent moisture during flowering and fruit development (spring through summer) significantly improves fruit size and quality.
[[drip-irrigation]] is ideal for feijoas, delivering water directly to the root zone while keeping foliage dry.
Apply a balanced organic fertilizer in early spring as new growth begins, and a lighter application in mid-summer.
Avoid excessive nitrogen, which promotes vegetative growth at the expense of flowering and fruit quality.
Mature trees benefit from an annual application of compost or well-rotted manure spread under the canopy.

### Pruning and Training

Feijoas can be grown as large shrubs, small multi-trunk trees, or even formally trained as hedges and screens.
Prune in late winter or early spring before new growth starts, removing dead, damaged, or crossing branches.
Open the center of the plant to improve air circulation and light penetration, which reduces disease risk and improves fruit quality.
For formal training along fences or walls, refer to [[espalier-fruit-tree-training-and-design]] techniques adapted for feijoa's naturally bushy habit.
Light tip-pruning in summer after harvest encourages branching and keeps the plant compact.
Avoid heavy pruning that removes more than a third of the canopy in any single year.
See [[fruit-tree-pruning]] for general principles that apply to feijoa and other evergreen fruit trees.

### Pollination and Fruit Set

Feijoa flowers are showy with white petals and prominent red stamens, attracting bees, birds, and other pollinators.
Planting pollinator-attracting companion plants nearby supports fruit set — see [[beneficial-insects-pollinators]] and [[pollinator-habitat]] for design ideas.
While some varieties are self-fertile, most benefit enormously from cross-pollination with a different cultivar.
Birds (especially starlings and blackbirds) are effective pollinators in some regions, but bees are the primary pollinators in most gardens.
If you have room for only one tree, choose a self-fertile variety like 'Unique' and plant pollinator-friendly flowers nearby.
Poor fruit set is often caused by inadequate cross-pollination, frost damage to flowers, or water stress during the critical flowering period.

### Pest and Disease Management

Feijoas are relatively pest-free compared to most fruit trees, which is one of their great advantages for organic gardeners.
Scale insects, mealybugs, and thrips can occasionally infest new growth, but are usually controlled by natural predators.
[[companion-planting]] with insectary species helps maintain a balanced ecosystem that keeps pest populations in check.
Fungal diseases like leaf spot and fruit rot can occur in humid climates with poor air circulation.
Prune to open the canopy, avoid overhead watering, and remove fallen fruit promptly to minimize disease pressure.
In most climates, feijoas require no spraying program, making them ideal for organic orchard management — see [[fruit-tree-care-and-orchard-management]] for holistic care approaches.

### Harvesting and Using Feijoas

Fruit typically ripens in autumn (March-May in the Southern Hemisphere, October-November in the Northern Hemisphere).
Feijoas are unusual among fruit in that they drop from the tree when fully ripe — simply collect them from the ground daily during harvest season.
Alternatively, gently squeeze fruit still on the tree — ripe fruit yields slightly to pressure.
The flesh inside is juicy and aromatic, with a flavor often described as a blend of pineapple, strawberry, and mint.
Use fresh fruit within a few days, or process into jams, chutneys, smoothies, desserts, and feijoa wine.
Feijoas are rich in vitamin C, dietary fiber, and antioxidants, with iodine content that is unusually high among fruits.

## What About Growing Feijoas in Containers?

Feijoas grow well in large containers (40-60 liters minimum), making them suitable for patios and small gardens.
Choose a dwarf or compact variety like 'Bambina' and use a high-quality potting mix with good drainage.
Container-grown plants need more frequent watering and feeding than in-ground trees.
Move containers to a sheltered position or wrap with frost protection fabric during severe cold snaps — see [[cold-frame-gardening]] for general protection strategies.
Container feijoas may produce less fruit than ground-planted trees but still reward you with ornamental foliage, edible flowers, and a modest harvest.

## Key Points

- Feijoas are tough, adaptable evergreens that thrive in USDA zones 8-11 with minimal care once established.
- Plant at least two different cultivars for cross-pollination and significantly better fruit production.
- They tolerate drought, coastal conditions, and moderate frost, making them versatile landscape plants.
- Refer to [[feijoa]] and [[feijoa-sellowiana]] for detailed species information and botanical characteristics.
- [[permaculture-andean-subtropical-fruit-trees]] covers feijoa's role in Andean polyculture systems.
- [[permaculture-plants-fruit-trees-subtropical]] provides broader context for subtropical orchard planning.
- Use [[drip-irrigation]] for efficient water delivery during the critical flowering and fruiting period.
- Maintain tree health with [[soil-ph]] monitoring and organic mulch under the canopy.
- Feijoas fit naturally into [[chop-and-drop-mulching]] and sustainable orchard management systems.
