     9|# How Do Fungi Contribute to Carbon Sequestration?
    10|
    11|## Short Answer
    12|Fungi sequester carbon through multiple pathways including mycorrhizal
    13|networks that transfer plant carbon into stable soil organic matter, production
    14|of glomalin and other recalcitrant compounds that resist decomposition, and
    15|direct incorporation of atmospheric carbon into fungal biomass. Mycorrhizal
    16|fungi alone are estimated to store over 13 gigatons of carbon globally in
    17|soils.
    18|
    19|## Detailed Explanation
    20|Fungi play a critical and often underappreciated role in the global carbon
    21|cycle, serving as both decomposers that release carbon dioxide and as
    22|ecosystem engineers that lock carbon into soil for decades or centuries.
    23|Understanding how fungi sequester carbon is essential for climate change
    24|mitigation strategies, regenerative agriculture, and [[biochar]] soil
    25|management. The fungal kingdom contributes to carbon sequestration through
    26|several distinct but interconnected mechanisms that operate at different scales
    27|and timeframes.
    28|
    29|Mycorrhizal fungi represent the single largest terrestrial carbon sink among
    30|soil organisms. Plants allocate a substantial portion of their
    31|photosynthetically fixed carbon, typically 10 to 40 percent, to their
    32|mycorrhizal partners through [[symbiotic-fungi-mycorrhizal-carbon-trading]].
    33|In exchange for nutrients and water, plants send sugars and lipids down into
    34|the soil through their roots, where [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-networks]] absorb
    35|them and use them to build extensive hyphal networks. A significant portion of
    36|this carbon is incorporated into fungal biomass and subsequently transformed
    37|into stable soil organic matter that resists microbial decomposition for
    38|decades.
    39|
    40|One of the most important carbon-sequestering compounds produced by fungi is
    41|[[glomalin-and-soil-carbon]], a glycoprotein exuded by arbuscular mycorrhizal
    42|fungi. Glomalin acts as a biological glue that binds soil particles together
    43|into stable aggregates, simultaneously improving soil structure and protecting
    44|the carbon contained within those aggregates from microbial attack. Research
    45|has shown that glomalin can persist in soil for 7 to 42 years, making it one
    46|of the most stable forms of soil organic carbon. The
    47|[[symbiotic-fungi-ectomycorrhizal-network-carbon-trading]] pathway in forest
    48|ecosystems operates similarly, with ectomycorrhizal fungi receiving carbon
    49|from trees and converting it into resistant organic compounds.
    50|
    51|The integration of fungi with [[biochar-and-fungi]] creates an additional
    52|carbon sequestration pathway. When biochar is inoculated with mycorrhizal
    53|fungi, the fungal hyphae colonize the biochar's porous structure, creating a
    54|stable habitat that protects both the fungal carbon and the biochar carbon
    55|from degradation. This synergistic relationship enhances the long-term carbon
    56|storage capacity of biochar-amended soils while simultaneously improving plant
    57|nutrient availability through the fungal network.
    58|
    59|Saprotrophic fungi also contribute to carbon sequestration, though in a more
    60|complex way. While decomposition by white-rot and brown-rot fungi releases
    61|carbon dioxide in the short term, it also converts a portion of the carbon
    62|into recalcitrant humic substances that resist further breakdown. The
    63|[[underground-fungal-networks]] created by these decomposers physically
    64|protect soil carbon by entombing organic particles within aggregations that
    65|are inaccessible to decomposing organisms. Dead fungal biomass itself contains
    66|chitin and other resistant compounds that contribute to long-term soil carbon
    67|pools.
    68|
    69|Forest ecosystems demonstrate the greatest fungal carbon sequestration
    70|potential. In mature forests, the [[wood-wide-web]] of mycorrhizal connections
    71|funnels enormous quantities of carbon from canopy trees into deep soil layers,
    72|where it becomes stabilized by mineral associations. Protecting and restoring
    73|these fungal networks through reduced tillage, cover cropping, and minimizing
    74|soil disturbance is one of the most effective climate mitigation strategies
    75|available to land managers.
    76|
    77|Agricultural practices that enhance fungal carbon sequestration include
no-till farming, cover cropping, reduced synthetic fertilizer inputs, and the
application of fungal composts and inoculants. These practices maintain living
root networks that continuously feed mycorrhizal partners, avoid the physical
destruction of hyphal networks through tillage, and promote the accumulation
of stable soil organic matter. Research comparing conventional tillage with
no-till systems consistently shows significantly higher soil carbon stocks in
no-till soils, with mycorrhizal fungi playing a central role in this
difference. The integration of perennial crops and agroforestry systems further
enhances fungal carbon sequestration by providing continuous root networks
that sustain mycorrhizal populations year-round.

The role of fungi in the global nitrogen cycle also indirectly affects carbon
sequestration. Mycorrhizal fungi help plants access organic nitrogen sources
that would otherwise remain unavailable, reducing the need for synthetic
nitrogen fertilizers that stimulate microbial decomposition of existing soil
carbon. When synthetic nitrogen is applied, it often accelerates the
decomposition of stable soil organic matter through a priming effect, actually
releasing more carbon than the plant biomass added. By facilitating more
efficient nitrogen cycling through biological pathways, fungal networks help
maintain the soil carbon that would otherwise be lost to stimulated
decomposition.
