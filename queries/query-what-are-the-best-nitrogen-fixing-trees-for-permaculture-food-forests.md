# What Are the Best Nitrogen-Fixing Trees for Permaculture Food Forests?

Nitrogen-fixing trees are the engine of food forest fertility. They supply free nitrogen to companion plants, build soil organic matter, provide mulch, attract pollinators, and often yield food themselves. The "best" tree depends on your climate zone, soil, and design goals.

---

## Top Species by Climate Zone

### Tropical (USDA 10–12, no frost)

| Species | Growth | N Fixation | Key Uses |
|---------|--------|-----------|----------|
| **Leucaena** (*L. leucocephala*) | Very fast | Very high (100–500 kg/ha/yr) | Fodder, mulch, alley cropping |
| **Gliricidia** (*G. sepium*) | Fast | High (50–200 kg/ha/yr) | Living fence, shade for cacao/coffee |
| **Calliandra** (*C. calothyrsus*) | Fast | Moderate (40–100 kg/ha/yr) | Fodder, slope stabilization, bee forage |
| **Sesbania** (*S. grandiflora*) | Very fast | High (60–150 kg/ha/yr) | Edible flowers/leaves, wet sites |

**Top pick**: Leucaena for maximum nitrogen; Gliricidia for shade-loving understories.

### Subtropical (USDA 9–10, light frost)

| Species | Growth | N Fixation | Key Uses |
|---------|--------|-----------|----------|
| **Tagasaste** (*C. proliferus*) | Fast | High | Fodder, windbreak, bee forage |
| **Pigeon pea** (*Cajanus cajan*) | Fast | Moderate | Edible peas, green manure, hedge |
| **Leucaena** | Very fast | Very high | Mulch, fodder (frost-protect) |

**Top pick**: Tagasaste — productive and frost-tolerant once established.

### Mediterranean (USDA 9–11)

| Species | Growth | N Fixation | Key Uses |
|---------|--------|-----------|----------|
| **Carob** (*C. siliqua*) | Slow | Moderate (15–40 kg/ha/yr) | Edible pods, shade, drought-proof |
| **Black locust** (if cold enough) | Fast | High | Timber, fence posts, honey |

**Top pick**: Carob for food; Black locust on the cooler edge of the zone.

### Temperate (USDA 4–8)

| Species | Growth | N Fixation | Key Uses |
|---------|--------|-----------|----------|
| **Black locust** (*R. pseudoacacia*) | Very fast | High (30–150 kg/ha/yr) | Timber, fence posts, honey |
| **Honey locust** (*G. triacanthos*) | Fast | Moderate (10–30 kg/ha/yr) | Edible pods, shade canopy |
| **Siberian pea shrub** (*C. arborescens*) | Moderate | Moderate (20–80 kg/ha/yr) | Windbreak, edible seeds |
| **Sea buckthorn** (*H. rhamnoides*)¹ | Moderate | Moderate (15–60 kg/ha/yr) | Superfood berries, erosion control |

¹ Actinorhizal (*Frankia* symbiosis, not a legume)

**Top pick**: Black locust (canopy); Siberian pea shrub (shrub layer); Sea buckthorn (marginal sites).

### Cold Continental (USDA 2–4)

| Species | Key Uses |
|---------|----------|
| **Siberian pea shrub** | Edible seeds, windbreak, extreme cold |
| **Sea buckthorn** | Berries, erosion control |
| **Russian olive**¹ | Windbreak, edible fruit |

¹ Check local invasiveness regulations before planting.

### Arid (Under 400 mm rainfall)

| Species | Key Uses |
|---------|----------|
| **Mesquite** (*P. glandulosa*) | Edible pods, drought-survival, fertile islands |
| **Acacia nilotica** | Fodder pods, firewood, gum |
| **Carob** | Mediterranean arid zones |

**Top pick**: Mesquite (Americas); Acacia (Africa/Asia); Carob (Mediterranean).

## Food Forest Integration

### Layer Placement

```
★ Upper Canopy (15+ m):  Black locust, Honey locust, Acacia, Leucaena
★ Sub-Canopy (5–15 m):   Carob, Mesquite, Tagasaste
★ Shrub Layer (1–5 m):   Siberian pea shrub, Sea buckthorn, Autumn olive, Pigeon pea
★ Ground Cover:          Clover, Vetch, Alfalfa, Lupine
```

### Design Rules of Thumb

1. **20–30% N-fixing species** — enough for fertility without displacing food plants
2. **Mix across layers** — canopy trees, shrub species, and ground covers for multi-level cycling
3. **Chop and drop** — prune regularly and drop foliage as mulch around food plants
4. **Proximity matters** — plant food trees within 3–5 m of N-fixers for root-zone benefit
5. **Avoid N overload** — excess nitrogen promotes pest-susceptible lush growth; balance with dynamic accumulators
6. **Succession planning** — use fast N-fixers as nurse trees for slow-establishing species; coppice or remove once canopy matures

---

## Common Mistakes

1. **Not inoculating** — tropical species (Leucaena) often fix zero nitrogen without specific rhizobial inoculant
2. **Ignoring invasiveness** — black locust, autumn olive, and mesquite can escape cultivation; check local lists
3. **Too close to structures** — aggressive roots; keep 5+ m from foundations and pipes
4. **Single-species reliance** — mix 3–5 N-fixers across layers for resilience
5. **Never pruning** — unpruned N-fixers shade food plants and hoard nitrogen in their own biomass
6. **Wrong climate match** — a carob in Minnesota or black locust in Phoenix will fail

## Quick Decision Key

```
START: What is your USDA hardiness zone?
│
├── Zones 2–4 → Siberian pea shrub + Sea buckthorn
├── Zones 4–7 → Black locust (timber) / Honey locust (pods) / Sea buckthorn (berries)
├── Zones 7–9 → Carob + Black locust (dry) / Black locust + Honey locust (humid)
├── Zones 9–11 → Tagasaste + Pigeon pea (frost) / Leucaena + Gliricidia (no frost)
└── Arid (<400mm) → Mesquite (Americas) / Acacia (Africa-Asia) / Carob (Mediterranean)
```
