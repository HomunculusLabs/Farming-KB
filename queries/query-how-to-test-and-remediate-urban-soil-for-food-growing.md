# How to Test and Remediate Urban Soil for Food Growing
## Why Urban Soil Testing Matters

Urban soils often carry contamination from decades or centuries of human activity — lead paint, leaded gasoline, industrial emissions, coal ash, demolition debris, and chemical spills. The most common urban soil contaminant is **lead (Pb)**, which was pervasive in exterior house paint (before 1978 in the US) and gasoline (before 1996). Other contaminants found in urban soils include arsenic (from treated lumber and historical pesticides), cadmium, chromium, petroleum hydrocarbons, PCBs, and asbestos.

Contaminated soil poses [[health-risks-of-hallucinogenic-mushrooms]] primarily through **direct ingestion** (children eating dirt), **dust inhalation**, and **plant uptake** into edible tissues. The risk pathway for most adults growing food is relatively low, but the stakes are highest for children, pregnant women, and communities growing a significant portion of their diet in urban soils.

**Bottom line: test before you grow.** [[soil-testing-and-fertility-planning]] is cheap ($15–$60 per sample) relative to the cost of remediation or the health consequences of growing food in contaminated soil.

---
## Step 1: Assess the Site History

Before collecting soil samples, investigate the land's history to estimate contamination risk:

- **Check historical maps and records** — Sanborn fire insurance maps, city planning archives, and environmental agency databases can reveal past industrial, commercial, or residential uses
- **Look for visual clues** — stained soil, unusual debris (glass, slag, ash), building foundations, underground storage tanks, proximity to busy roads or industrial sites
- **Age of buildings** — pre-1978 buildings likely used lead paint; pre-1990s structures may have asbestos
- **Proximity to sources** — within 10 meters of a painted building foundation, within 50 meters of a major road, within 100 meters of industrial sites all increase lead risk
- **Talk to long-term residents** — neighbors may remember past uses, dumps, or incidents

---
## Step 2: Collect and Submit Soil Samples

### Sampling Protocol

1. **Use clean equipment** — stainless steel trowel or soil probe, not galvanized (which can contaminate zinc results). Wear nitrile gloves.
2. **Collect composite samples** — take 10–15 subsamples from across the growing area (depth 0–15 cm for surface contaminants like lead, 15–30 cm if you plan to dig deeper). Mix subsamples in a clean plastic bucket.
3. **Divide into zones** — sample separately for areas with different histories (e.g., near a building vs. center of the lot vs. near the road)
4. **Air-dry the sample** — spread on clean newspaper, avoid heating
5. **Submit 1–2 cups** to a certified laboratory in a zip-lock bag

### What to Test For

**Essential tests for urban food-growing soils:**
| Parameter | Why It Matters | Typical Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Lead (Pb) | Most common urban contaminant; neurotoxin | Included in basic test |
| Arsenic (As) | Carcinogen; from treated wood, pesticides | $5–$15 extra |
| Cadmium (Cd) | Accumulates in leafy greens; kidney toxin | $5–$15 extra |
| Soil pH | Affects contaminant bioavailability | Included |
| Organic matter % | Affects contaminant binding | Included |
| Soil texture (sand/silt/clay) | Affects drainage and contaminant mobility | $5–$10 |
**Optional tests depending on site history:**
- Petroleum hydrocarbons (if near gas stations, mechanics, or industrial sites)
- PCBs (if near transformers or industrial facilities)
- Chromium, nickel, zinc (if near metalworking or plating shops)
- PAHs (polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons — if near railroads, coal ash deposits, or fire sites)

### Where to Send Samples

- **University extension soil testing labs** — affordable, reliable, often provide urban agriculture-specific guidance (e.g., UMass, Cornell, University of Minnesota)
- **Private environmental labs** — more comprehensive contaminant panels, higher cost
- **Local government programs** — some cities offer free or subsidized soil testing for community gardens and urban farms

## Step 3: Interpret Results

### Lead (Pb) Guidelines

The EPA's current residential soil screening level is **400 ppm (mg/kg)** for bare soil in play areas. However, for food-growing soils, most urban agriculture programs use stricter thresholds:
| Lead Concentration | Risk Level | Recommended Action |
|---|---|---|
| **< 80 ppm** | Low | Generally safe for all food growing with good hygiene |
| **80–200 ppm** | Low–Moderate | Safe for fruiting crops (tomatoes, peppers, beans); limit root and leafy crops; add compost and maintain high pH (6.5–7.0) |
| **200–400 ppm** | Moderate | [[raised-beds-vs-in-ground-vs-containers]] with imported soil for root/leafy crops; fruiting crops may be acceptable with amendments |
| **400–1,200 ppm** | High | Raised beds with [[landscape-fabric-alternatives]] barrier for all edible crops; wear gloves, wash produce thoroughly |
| **> 1,200 ppm** | Very High | Do not grow food directly in native soil; use raised beds with thick barrier or consider container gardening; consult remediation specialist |

### Key Concept: Bioavailability

Total contaminant concentration matters, but **bioavailability** — how much the contaminant can actually be absorbed by plants or the human body — is equally important. Bioavailability is influenced by:

- **Soil pH:** Acidic soils (pH < 6.0) increase lead bioavailability. Maintaining pH 6.5–7.5 significantly reduces plant uptake and human absorption
- **Organic matter:** High organic matter binds contaminants, reducing bioavailability. Compost additions of 5–10% by volume can substantially reduce uptake
- **Phosphorus:** Phosphate amendments (rock phosphate, triple superphosphate) can chemically bind lead into insoluble pyromorphite minerals, permanently reducing bioavailability
- **Soil texture:** Clay and organic-rich soils bind contaminants more tightly than sandy soils

## Step 4: Remediation Strategies

### Strategy 1: Phosphorus Amendment (Lead Immobilization)

Adding phosphate is the most evidence-based in-situ remediation for lead-contaminated soil. Phosphate reacts with lead to form lead-phosphate minerals (pyromorphites) that are extremely insoluble and biologically unavailable.

- Apply **triple superphosphate** at 0.1–1% by weight (roughly 5–50 g per kg of soil) based on lead concentration
- Alternatively, use **fish bone meal** or **bone char** as an organic phosphate source (apply at 1–5% by volume)
- Mix into top 15 cm of soil
- Maintain soil pH at 6.5–7.0 with lime if needed
- Allow 3–6 months for reactions to complete before planting
- Re-test soil after treatment to confirm lead bioavailability reduction

**Limitations:** Phosphorus amendment does not remove lead — it immobilizes it. Disturbing the soil later can re-expose lead. Excess phosphorus can cause nutrient imbalances and water pollution. This strategy is best for moderately contaminated soils (200–800 ppm lead).

### Strategy 2: Compost and Organic Matter Addition

Adding high-quality compost is the single most versatile remediation approach:

- Apply **5–10 cm of finished compost** and mix into top 15–20 cm annually
- Compost dilutes contaminant concentration, increases cation exchange capacity, improves [[soil-structure-and-aggregate-ecology]], and boosts microbial activity
- Microorganisms in compost can transform contaminants into less bioavailable forms
- Earthworms attracted to compost-rich soil further improve soil health and contaminant binding
- This is an ongoing, cumulative strategy — benefits increase with repeated applications

### Strategy 3: Raised Beds with Imported Soil

For soils with moderate to high contamination (>200 ppm lead), raised beds with clean imported soil are the most reliable approach:

- Build beds **30–60 cm tall** using untreated wood, concrete blocks, or galvanized metal
- Line the bottom with **landscape fabric** or geotextile to prevent root penetration into contaminated native soil
- Fill with **tested, certified clean soil** or a mix of topsoil and compost
- For extra safety, add a **5 cm gravel layer** above the fabric before adding soil — this prevents wicking of contaminants upward
- Re-test imported soil before filling beds

**Cost estimate:** A 3 m × 1 m × 30 cm raised bed requires approximately 0.9 m³ (1.2 cubic yards) of soil mix, costing $30–$100 depending on source and quality.
