# What Is Wattle and Daub and How Do I Build With It?

## Quick Answer

Wattle and daub is an ancient wall-building technique where a woven wooden lattice (the **wattle**) is coated with a sticky earthen mixture (the **daub**) made from clay soil, sand, straw, and sometimes animal dung. The result is a solid, breathable, insulating wall panel. It's one of the oldest building methods on Earth — used for over 6,000 years — and it's still practical today for sheds, chicken coops, greenhouses, and even homes when combined with a timber frame.

## What Materials Do I Need?

### For the Wattle (the wooden lattice):

- **Flexible branches** — hazel, willow, or bamboo work best. You need:
  - **Stakes** (uprights): 60–90 cm long, 2–4 cm thick
  - **Withies** (weavers): Long, flexible rods, 1–2 cm thick
- Cut green (fresh) so they're bendable — they'll stiffen as they dry

### For the Daub (the earth mix):

- **Clay subsoil** — dig below the topsoil. Test it: roll a ball and drop it from waist height. If it holds together, you have enough clay.
- **Sand** — sharp/angular builder's sand, not beach sand
- **Chopped straw** — cut to 5–10 cm lengths
- **Water**
- **Optional**: Cow or horse dung (adds fiber and workability), hydrated lime (improves weather resistance)

### Basic Daub Recipe (by volume):

- 4 parts clay subsoil
- 2 parts sand
- 1 part chopped straw
- ½ part animal dung (optional but recommended)
- Water until it feels like thick bread dough

## How Do I Build a Wattle and Daub Panel?

### Step 1: Build or Prepare the Frame

Wattle and daub is **infill only** — it cannot support weight on its own. You need a timber frame (posts and beams), or at minimum two horizontal rails (top and bottom) to support the panel.

### Step 2: Install the Wattle

1. Drive stakes vertically into the sill (bottom rail) at 15–20 cm intervals
2. Weave withies horizontally in and out of the stakes, starting from the bottom
3. Alternate each row — if the first row goes behind the first stake, the second goes in front
4. Pack the weaving tightly as you go
5. Leave about 1 cm gap between the wattle and the frame edges (daub shrinks as it dries)

### Step 3: Mix the Daub

1. Spread a tarp on the ground
2. Pile clay subsoil, sand, straw, and dung on the tarp
3. Mix by folding the tarp back and forth, or by foot-trampling (wear rubber boots)
4. Add water gradually until the mix holds together without being soupy
5. Let it sit 24 hours before using — this fully hydrates the clay

### Step 4: Apply the Daub (Three Coats)

**First coat (scratch coat):**
- Press handfuls of daub firmly into both sides of the wattle, filling all gaps
- Use your hands or a wooden paddle to push it in
- Aim for 4–6 cm thickness on each side
- Score horizontal lines in the surface with a stick while still wet
- Let dry 3–7 days

**Second coat (brown coat):**
- Apply 2–3 cm of smoother daub over the first coat
- This levels the wall surface
- Let dry 3–7 days

**Third coat (finish coat):**
- Apply 5–10 mm of fine, smooth plaster
- This can include lime for weatherproofing or fine sand for polish
- For exterior walls, a lime-based finish is strongly recommended

### Step 5: Protect and Cure

- Shade walls from direct sun during drying to prevent cracking
- Mist lightly in hot, dry weather
- Keep roof overhangs at least 45 cm to protect from rain
- Full cure takes 4–8 weeks

## How Long Does It Last?

A well-built wattle-and-daub wall with proper foundation, roof overhang, and exterior lime render can last **100–500+ years**. Many 500-year-old timber-framed buildings in Europe still have their original wattle-and-daub panels. The key is:

- **Keep it dry at the base** (stone plinth, good drainage)
- **Keep it dry on the outside** (roof overhang, lime render)
- **Keep the wattle fully covered** (no exposed wood = no rot)

## What Can I Build With It?

Wattle and daub works for:

- **Garden walls and fences** — beautiful, natural boundaries
- **Chicken coops, goat shelters, and animal housing** — breathable, well-insulated
- **Greenhouse infill panels** — adds thermal mass to passive solar greenhouses
- **Sheds and workshops** — low-cost, attractive outbuildings
- **Home walls** — as infill in a timber frame structure
- **Outdoor kitchens and pizza oven surrounds**

It is **not suitable** for:
- Below-grade walls (basements)
- Foundation walls (use stone, concrete, or rammed earth)
- Free-standing walls without a frame
- Structures in extremely wet climates without excellent protection

## How Much Does It Cost?

- **Materials**: $5–20 per m² of wall (daub ingredients only)
- **Timber frame**: Additional cost ($50–150/m² depending on design)
- **Labor**: 6–12 hours per m² for a beginner
- **Total**: One of the cheapest wall systems available if you harvest your own wattles and dig your own clay

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

1. **No foundation protection** — never let daub touch the ground. Use a stone or concrete plinth at least 15 cm above grade.
2. **Daub too wet** — causes sagging, cracking, and weak walls. Aim for dough consistency, not batter.
3. **Not enough fiber** — leads to severe cracking. Use generous straw (15–20% by volume).
4. **Skipping the drying time between coats** — traps moisture and causes the wall to fail.
5. **Using topsoil instead of subsoil** — topsoil contains organic matter that decomposes, creating voids and weakening the wall.
6. **Inadequate roof overhang** — rain is the #1 enemy. Minimum 45 cm overhang on all sides.
7. **No exterior finish** — bare daub erodes in rain. Always apply a lime wash or lime render to exterior surfaces.

## Is Wattle and Daub Right for My Project?

**Yes, if:**
- You have or can build a timber frame
- You want low-cost, low-carbon walls
- You have access to clay soil and flexible branches
- You enjoy hands-on, tactile building work
- You're in a mild to dry climate

**Consider cob instead if:**
- You don't want to build a full timber frame
- You want load-bearing earth walls
- You prefer sculptural, curved architecture
- You need maximum thermal mass

**Consider light clay straw instead if:**
- You need better insulation than earth provides
- You want a lighter-weight wall system
- You have abundant straw but limited clay
