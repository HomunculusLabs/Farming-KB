# How can ducks help with pest control and fertility in a garden?

## Short Answer

Ducks help a garden by eating slugs, snails, insects, fallen fruit, weeds, and aquatic plants while returning manure to the system.
They are especially useful where moisture, ponds, orchards, rice paddies, or slug pressure make chickens less suitable.
Good duck integration depends on timing, fencing, water design, and protection from crops they might trample or nibble.
Pages such as permaculture-duck-raising, [[permaculture-duck-pond-systems]] show that ducks work best as a designed element rather than as loose birds everywhere.
The goal is to let ducks cycle pests and fertility through the landscape without turning delicate beds into mud.

## Detailed Explanation

### Ducks as pest managers

Ducks are famous for eating [[query-how-to-get-rid-of-slugs-and-snails-organically]].
They also forage for beetles, larvae, worms, fallen fruit, and many soft-bodied insects.
The pest-control angle connects directly to holzer-slug-snail-management.
Unlike chickens, ducks scratch less aggressively, so they can be gentler around established perennial systems.
They still trample seedlings and compact wet soil if access is unmanaged.
The best strategy is timed access, where ducks enter after harvest, before planting, or under mature plantings.
This turns pest control into a planned pulse rather than constant disturbance.

### Ducks in orchards

Orchards are one of the best garden contexts for ducks.
Trees are tall enough to avoid most duck damage, while dropped fruit attracts insects and disease organisms that ducks can clean up.
The page [[duck-rice-paddy]] describes a classic pattern where ducks move through rice fields eating pests and stirring water.
The more integrated mollison-designers-duck-pond-integrated-systems and [[permaculture-duck-pond-systems]] emphasizes that water must be easy to refresh or route.
A pond that is hard to clean soon becomes a management problem rather than a benefit.

### Manure and fertility cycling

Duck manure is wetter than chicken manure and is often spread through water and bedding.
This makes ducks useful for distributing fertility across paddocks, orchards, and pond edges.
The livestock overview in [[mollison-duck-and-waterfowl-integration]] shows how waterfowl can connect aquatic and terrestrial production.
Bedding from night housing can go to compost rather than directly to tender crops.
Manure should be managed with the same food-safety caution used for any livestock manure.
Avoid fresh manure contact with harvestable [[solomon-leafy-greens-growing]] and low uncommon specialty fruit crops for permaculture.

### Protecting beds from damage

Ducks are not automatically safe in every garden bed.
They can flatten seedlings, muddy paths, uproot small transplants, and chew tender greens.
Fencing, temporary netting, and mobile pens are the main control tools.
The general husbandry page [[comparison-chicken-keeping-vs-duck-keeping]] helps explain why duck damage differs from chicken scratching.
Use ducks around mature perennials, robust [[gaias-garden-cover-crops-soil-building]], pathways, orchards, and post-harvest beds.
Keep them out of newly seeded beds until plants are established.

### Breed and stocking decisions

Different duck breeds have different foraging, egg-laying, flight, and body-size traits.
Light breeds can be active foragers and may do less trampling than very heavy ducks.
Heavier breeds may be calmer but can damage wet ground faster.
The page [[holzer-poultry-keeping-ducks-geese-and-chickens]] places ducks among other poultry options.
Stocking rate should be based on forage, water, shelter, predator pressure, and how often birds rotate.
Too many ducks in too small a space quickly turns pest control into manure overload.
