# How can ducks help with pest control and fertility in a garden?

## Short Answer

Ducks help a garden by eating slugs, snails, insects, fallen fruit, weeds, and aquatic plants while returning manure to the system.
They are especially useful where moisture, ponds, orchards, rice paddies, or slug pressure make chickens less suitable.
Good duck integration depends on timing, fencing, water design, and protection from crops they might trample or nibble.
Pages such as [[duck-keeping]], [[permaculture-duck-raising]], [[permaculture-duck-orchard-integration]], and [[permaculture-duck-pond-systems]] show that ducks work best as a designed element rather than as loose birds everywhere.
The goal is to let ducks cycle pests and fertility through the landscape without turning delicate beds into mud.

## Detailed Explanation

### Ducks as pest managers

Ducks are famous for eating slugs and snails.
They also forage for beetles, larvae, worms, fallen fruit, and many soft-bodied insects.
The pest-control angle connects directly to [[slugs-and-snails-organic-control]] and [[holzer-slug-snail-management]].
Unlike chickens, ducks scratch less aggressively, so they can be gentler around established perennial systems.
They still trample seedlings and compact wet soil if access is unmanaged.
The best strategy is timed access, where ducks enter after harvest, before planting, or under mature plantings.
This turns pest control into a planned pulse rather than constant disturbance.

### Ducks in orchards

Orchards are one of the best garden contexts for ducks.
Trees are tall enough to avoid most duck damage, while dropped fruit attracts insects and disease organisms that ducks can clean up.
The page [[permaculture-duck-orchard-integration]] focuses on this relationship between tree crops and mobile waterfowl.
Ducks can reduce pest life cycles by consuming larvae and damaged fruit under the canopy.
They also deposit manure where tree roots and soil organisms can eventually use it.
Rotational access prevents overmanuring around favorite shade and water spots.
Young trees still need guards because ducks may dabble around roots in wet soil.

### Ducks in rice and wet systems

Ducks fit naturally into wetland and paddy systems.
The page [[duck-rice-paddy]] describes a classic pattern where ducks move through rice fields eating pests and stirring water.
The more integrated [[fukuoka-duck-rice-fish-polyculture]] links ducks with rice, fish, weeds, and nutrient cycling.
Ducks can suppress weeds by grazing tender growth and disturbing germinating seedlings.
They also add manure directly to water, where it enters the pond or paddy nutrient cycle.
This can be productive, but stocking density must be controlled to avoid fouling the water.
Wet systems need design discipline because fertility can become pollution when too concentrated.

### Duck ponds as nutrient engines

A duck pond is not just a bathing area.
It is a nutrient collection point, a microclimate feature, and a biological reactor.
The design pages [[mollison-designers-duck-pond-integrated-systems]] and [[permaculture-designers-manual-duck-pond-systems]] frame ponds as part of a larger flow of fertility.
Dirty duck water can be directed to trees, compost, reed beds, or mulch basins.
This turns a cleaning chore into irrigation and fertilization.
The practical page [[permaculture-duck-pond-systems]] emphasizes that water must be easy to refresh or route.
A pond that is hard to clean soon becomes a management problem rather than a benefit.

### Manure and fertility cycling

Duck manure is wetter than chicken manure and is often spread through water and bedding.
This makes ducks useful for distributing fertility across paddocks, orchards, and pond edges.
The livestock overview in [[mollison-poultry-duck-integration]] treats birds as mobile nutrient cyclers.
The broader system page [[mollison-duck-and-waterfowl-integration]] shows how waterfowl can connect aquatic and terrestrial production.
Bedding from night housing can go to compost rather than directly to tender crops.
Manure should be managed with the same food-safety caution used for any livestock manure.
Avoid fresh manure contact with harvestable leafy greens and low fruit crops.

### Protecting beds from damage

Ducks are not automatically safe in every garden bed.
They can flatten seedlings, muddy paths, uproot small transplants, and chew tender greens.
Fencing, temporary netting, and mobile pens are the main control tools.
The general husbandry page [[duck-keeping-guide-detailed]] is useful for thinking about housing, water, and daily care.
The comparison [[comparison-chicken-keeping-vs-duck-keeping]] helps explain why duck damage differs from chicken scratching.
Use ducks around mature perennials, robust cover crops, pathways, orchards, and post-harvest beds.
Keep them out of newly seeded beds until plants are established.

### Breed and stocking decisions

Different duck breeds have different foraging, egg-laying, flight, and body-size traits.
Light breeds can be active foragers and may do less trampling than very heavy ducks.
Heavier breeds may be calmer but can damage wet ground faster.
The page [[faires-duck-and-goose-keeping]] gives a small-farm view of duck and goose management.
The broader [[holzer-poultry-keeping-ducks-geese-and-chickens]] places ducks among other poultry options.
Stocking rate should be based on forage, water, shelter, predator pressure, and how often birds rotate.
Too many ducks in too small a space quickly turns pest control into manure overload.

## Key Points

- Ducks are strongest where moisture, slugs, fallen fruit, or pond systems already exist.
- Use [[permaculture-duck-orchard-integration]] to place ducks under mature trees rather than in fragile beds.
- Use [[duck-rice-paddy]] and [[fukuoka-duck-rice-fish-polyculture]] for wetland or paddy patterns.
- Design water with [[permaculture-duck-pond-systems]] so dirty water becomes fertility instead of waste.
- Combine duck pest work with [[slugs-and-snails-organic-control]] for slug-heavy gardens.
- Manage manure carefully and compost bedding when food-safety risk is high.
- Compare birds with [[comparison-chicken-keeping-vs-duck-keeping]] before choosing livestock.
- Treat ducks as a rotating design element, not as permanent access to every bed.
