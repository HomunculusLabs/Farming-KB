# Asian Psilocybin Mushroom Guide

Regional distribution guide for psilocybin-containing mushrooms across Asia, covering species, habitats, seasonal patterns, and cultural use. Based on [[jochen-gartz]]"s global survey and [[john-allen]]'s Southeast Asian research. See also [[psilocybin-mushroom-chemistry]] for the worldwide overview and [[mushroom-species-guide]] for species profiles.

## Southeast Asia

Southeast Asia is one of the world's richest regions for psilocybin mushroom biodiversity, with a tropical climate supporting year-round fruiting in many areas.

### Thailand

- **Psilocybe cubensis:** The most common and well-known species. Found on bovine dung in pastures throughout the country. Season: May-October (rainy season). Known locally from mushroom tourism on Koh Samui and Koh Pha-ngan.
- **Panaeolus cyanescens:** Found on bovine dung. Widely distributed in tropical lowlands. Very potent — one of the strongest Panaeolus species.
- **Psilocybe samuiensis:** First described from Ko Samui by Guzman, Bandala and Allen (1992). Small, bluing species found in rice paddies and pastures. Named after the island where it was first collected.
- **Psilocybe subaeruginascens:** Found in Thailand and Japan. Grows in grassy areas and on disturbed soil.

**Mushroom Tourism:** Koh Samui and Koh Pha-ngan have been destinations for "mushroom tourism" since at least the 1980s. Restaurants served psilocybin mushroom dishes (typically P. cubensis omelettes or shakes) to foreign visitors. The practice became widespread enough to appear in guidebooks. Local names include "hed keequai" (mushroom that appears after buffalo defecates).

### Indonesia (Bali, Java, Sumatra)

- **Panaeolus cyanescens:** The dominant psychoactive species in Bali. Found on cattle dung in rice paddies and pastures. Mushroom restaurants serving P. cyanescens omelettes operated openly from the 1970s through the 2000s.
- **Psilocybe cubensis:** Present but less commonly documented than P. cyanescens in Bali.
- **Copelandia cyanescens:** Synonym for Panaeolus cyanescens; some older references use this name.

**Bali mushroom culture:** Mushroom use in Bali was intertwined with the broader tourist economy. Unlike Mexico, where mushroom use has deep indigenous roots, Balinese mushroom tourism was primarily a Western import. Local Balinese people traditionally did not use psychoactive mushrooms before tourism introduced them. This makes Bali a unique case study in how psychoactive mushroom knowledge transfers between cultures.

### Cambodia

- **Psilocybe cubensis:** Found in pastures, particularly around Siem Reap and the Angkor Wat region. Grows on water buffalo dung during rainy season.
- **Panaeolus cyanescens:** Present in tropical lowland pastures.
- Reports from travelers document mushrooms fruiting abundantly on temple grounds and in rural pastures.

### Vietnam

- **Psilocybe cubensis:** Documented from southern Vietnam in pastures and rice paddies.
- **Panaeolus cyanescens:** Present in lowland tropical areas.
- Less systematically surveyed than Thailand or Indonesia; likely underreported.

### Laos

- **Psilocybe cubensis:** Present in northern Laos, particularly around Luang Prabang and Vang Vieng. Grows on cattle and water buffalo dung.
- **Panaeolus cyanescens:** Documented from lowland areas.
- Mushroom shakes sold openly in some backpacker areas (Vang Vieng) until crackdowns.

### Philippines

- **Psilocybe cubensis:** Widely distributed on cattle dung throughout the archipelago.
- **Panaeolus cyanescens:** Present in lowland pastures.
- **Psilocybe subcaerulipes:** Reported from the Philippines (more commonly associated with Japan).

### Myanmar (Burma)

- **Psilocybe cubensis:** Likely present but poorly documented due to limited mycological survey work.
- **Panaeolus cyanescens:** Expected in tropical lowland areas.

## Japan

Japan has a surprisingly diverse psychoactive mushroom flora given its temperate climate:

- **Psilocybe subcaerulipes:** Small woodland species found on decomposing wood and leaf litter. Known from Honshu, Shikoku, and Kyushu. One of Japan"s most common psychoactive species.
- **Psilocybe argentipes:** Found on forest floor in deciduous and coniferous forests. Known from Honshu. Produces moderate bluing reaction.
- **Psilocybe venenata:** Formerly classified as Stropharia venenata Imai. Found in grassy areas and fields. Known from Honshu.
- **Psilocybe subaeruginascens:** Found in grassy areas and on disturbed ground. Known from southern Japan (Kyushu, Ryukyu Islands).
- **Panaeolus subbalteatus:** Found on horse and cattle dung. Widespread.
- **Gymnopilus spectabilis:** Large, orange species growing on dead hardwood. Known from Japan (called "oh-waraitake" — "big laughing mushroom"). Contains psilocybin and bis-noryangonin.
- **Pluteus salicinus:** Wood-inhabiting species found on dead alder and willow. Known from Japan.

**Japanese mushroom law:** In 2002, Japan amended the Narcotics and Psychotropics Control Law to specifically ban psilocybin and psilocybin-containing mushrooms. Previously, fresh mushrooms were in a legal gray area (only the purified alkaloids were banned). The law change was triggered by reports of mushrooms being sold openly at music festivals and in shops.

## South Asia

### India

- **Psilocybe cubensis:** Reported from southern India (Kerala, Karnataka, Goa) growing on bovine dung during monsoon season (June-September).
- **Panaeolus cyanescens:** Documented from tropical lowland areas.
- **Psilocybe subtropicalis:** Reported from India; grows in subtropical forest environments.
- Very little systematic mycological survey work has been done on psychoactive species in India. The rich tradition of Soma worship in Vedic texts has led some scholars (notably [[r-gordon-wasson]]) to speculate about possible mushroom use in ancient India, though this remains debated.

### Nepal

- **Psilocybe cubensis:** Reported from the Terai (lowland tropical region) and Kathmandu Valley during monsoon.
- **Psilocybe nepalensis:** Species described from Nepal; grows in montane and subalpine zones.
- Poorly documented due to limited mycological infrastructure.

### Sri Lanka

- **Psilocybe cubensis:** Present in tropical lowlands, particularly on cattle dung.
- **Panaeolus cyanescens:** Expected but poorly documented.

## Central Asia

### Siberia and the Russian Far East

- **Amanita muscaria:** While not a psilocybin-containing species, A. muscaria (fly agaric) is the most culturally significant psychoactive mushroom in northern Asia. Used by Siberian peoples (Chukchee, Koryak, Kamchadal, Itelmen) for centuries in shamanic rituals.
- **Gymnopilus spectabilis:** Reported from Siberian forests.
- The cold continental climate of Central Asia limits psilocybin mushroom diversity, but Amanita muscaria is abundant in birch and pine forests.

## East Asia

### China

- Very limited documentation of psychoactive mushrooms due to restrictive drug laws and limited mycological survey work focused on this topic.
- **Psilocybe cubensis:** Likely present in southern tropical regions (Yunnan, Guangxi, Hainan).
- **Gymnopilus spectabilis:** Large Asian distribution including China; grows on dead hardwood.
- China's diverse fungal flora (estimated 150,000+ species, only ~10% described) likely contains many undocumented psychoactive species.

### Taiwan

- **Psilocybe cubensis:** Documented from lowland tropical areas.
- **Psilocybe subcaerulipes:** Likely present given proximity to Japan.
- Under-documented due to legal restrictions.

### South Korea

- **Psilocybe cubensis:** Possibly present in southern subtropical areas.
- **Gymnopilus spectabilis:** Expected given presence in Japan and China.
- Very limited documentation.

## Substrate and Habitat Patterns

Across Asia, psychoactive mushrooms follow predictable substrate patterns:

| Substrate | Species | Climate Zone |
|---|---|---|
| Bovine dung (cattle/water buffalo) | P. cubensis, P. samuiensis | Tropical lowlands |
| Equine dung | P. subbalteatus, Panaeolus cyanescens | Tropical/subtropical |
| Decomposing wood | P. subcaerulipes, Pluteus salicinus | Temperate forests |
| Forest floor litter | P. argentipes, P. venenata | Temperate forests |
| Dead hardwood (logs/stumps) | Gymnopilus spectabilis | Temperate |
| Disturbed soil/grassland | P. subaeruginascens | Subtropical/tropical |
