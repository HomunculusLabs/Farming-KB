# What Is the Wood Wide Web and How Do Mycorrhizal Networks Work?

## Short Answer
The "Wood Wide Web" is the popular term for the underground networks formed by
[[mycorrhizal-fungi]] that connect the roots of trees and plants across entire
forest ecosystems. These [[mycorrhizal-networks]] allow plants to exchange
nutrients, water, and chemical signals through shared fungal hyphae that
function as a biological internet beneath the soil surface, fundamentally
shaping how terrestrial ecosystems function at every scale.

## What Are Mycorrhizal Fungi?

Mycorrhizal fungi are symbiotic organisms that form intimate associations with
the root systems of approximately 90% of all plant species. There are two
primary types that dominate forest ecosystems: [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]]
(AMF), which penetrate root cells directly, and [[ectomycorrhizal-fungi]],
which form a sheath around root tips without entering cells. Both types extend
vast networks of hyphae into the surrounding soil, dramatically increasing a
plant's effective root surface area by up to 1,000 times compared to roots
operating alone without fungal partnerships.

The fungal partner receives carbohydrates (primarily glucose) produced by the
host plant through photosynthesis. In return, the fungus provides the plant
with essential nutrients and water that its own roots could not efficiently
access alone. This mutualistic relationship has evolved over 400 million years
and is considered one of the most important and ancient symbioses on Earth,
predating the evolution of roots themselves in terrestrial plants.

## How the Network Functions

The underground network operates through [[common-mycorrhizal-networks]] that
form when a single fungal individual connects to multiple plants simultaneously.
A single ectomycorrhizal network can link hundreds of trees across dozens of
acres. Through these connections, plants can share resources in ways that were
once thought impossible in competitive ecological frameworks. The network
creates what ecologists call a "common mycelial network" where resources flow
between individuals based on source-sink dynamics determined by each plant's
needs and its capacity to produce photosynthate.

[[mycorrhizal-network-communication]] occurs through several mechanisms. Plants
release chemical signals into the fungal network that neighboring plants can
detect and respond to. For example, when a tree is attacked by herbivores or
pathogens, it can send warning signals through the network that prompt nearby
plants to activate their own chemical defenses preemptively. This was first
demonstrated by Suzanne Simard in Douglas fir and ponderosa pine forests, where
injured trees were observed transmitting stress signals to neighboring
individuals through shared mycorrhizal connections.

## Nutrient and Carbon Exchange

One of the most significant functions of the Wood Wide Web is the redistribution
of resources between plants of different species and ages.
[[mycorrhizal-carbon-cycling-soil-sequestration]] plays a critical role in
global carbon dynamics. Trees transfer significant amounts of photosynthetically
fixed carbon through mycorrhizal networks to seedlings growing in shaded
understory conditions, effectively subsidizing the growth of younger trees
that cannot yet support themselves through full-sun photosynthesis.

[[nitrogen-transfer-through-common-mycorrhizal-networks]] is equally important.
Leguminous plants that fix atmospheric nitrogen can share this vital nutrient
with neighboring non-leguminous plants through shared fungal connections. This
nitrogen sharing has been measured in forest ecosystems using isotopic tracing,
revealing that nitrogen moves preferentially toward seedlings and stressed
individuals, suggesting a level of cooperative behavior within forest
communities that challenges traditional competitive models of ecology.

## Ecological Significance

The Wood Wide Web has profound implications for how we understand forest
ecology and manage forest ecosystems. Research has shown that "mother trees"
(large, well-established individuals) serve as hubs in the network, connecting
to and nurturing surrounding seedlings of the same species. These hub trees
can recognize their own kin and preferentially send them more resources than
they send to unrelated neighbors, suggesting a form of kin selection operating
through fungal networks that was previously unknown in plant biology.

## Key Points
- Mycorrhizal networks connect the majority of land plants underground through
  shared fungal hyphae, forming what scientists call the Wood Wide Web
- [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi]] penetrate root cells while
  [[ectomycorrhizal-fungi]] form external sheaths around root tips
- Plants can communicate stress signals and share nutrients through
  [[common-mycorrhizal-networks]], enabling cooperative behavior
- [[mycorrhizal-carbon-cycling-soil-sequestration]] is critical for global
  carbon budgets and forest regeneration dynamics
- [[nitrogen-transfer-through-common-mycorrhizal-networks]] allows nitrogen
  fixed by legumes to benefit neighboring non-leguminous plants
