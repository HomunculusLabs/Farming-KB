## What Are Mycorrhizal Fungi?

Mycorrhizal fungi are symbiotic organisms that form intimate associations with
the root systems of approximately 90% of all plant species. There are two
primary types that dominate forest ecosystems: [[ectomycorrhizal-fungi]],
which form a sheath around root tips without entering cells. Both types extend
vast networks of hyphae into the surrounding soil, dramatically increasing a
plant's effective root surface area by up to 1,000 times compared to roots
operating alone without fungal partnerships.

The fungal partner receives carbohydrates (primarily glucose) produced by the
host plant through photosynthesis. In return, the fungus provides the plant
with essential nutrients and water that its own roots could not efficiently
access alone. This mutualistic relationship has evolved over 400 million years
and is considered one of the most important and ancient symbioses on Earth,
predating the evolution of roots themselves in terrestrial plants.

## Nutrient and Carbon Exchange

One of the most significant functions of the Wood Wide Web is the redistribution
of resources between plants of different species and ages.

Leguminous plants that fix atmospheric nitrogen can share this vital nutrient
with neighboring non-leguminous plants through shared fungal connections. This
nitrogen sharing has been measured in forest ecosystems using isotopic tracing,
revealing that nitrogen moves preferentially toward seedlings and stressed
individuals, suggesting a level of cooperative behavior within forest
communities that challenges traditional competitive models of ecology.

## Ecological Significance

The Wood Wide Web has profound implications for how we understand forest
ecology and manage forest ecosystems. Research has shown that "mother trees"
(large, well-established individuals) serve as hubs in the network, connecting
to and nurturing surrounding seedlings of the same species. These hub trees
can recognize their own kin and preferentially send them more resources than
they send to unrelated neighbors, suggesting a form of kin selection operating
through fungal networks that was previously unknown in plant biology.
