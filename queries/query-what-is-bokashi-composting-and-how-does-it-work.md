# What Is Bokashi Composting and How Does It Work?

## Short Answer
Bokashi composting is an anaerobic fermentation method originating from Japan that
uses Effective Microorganisms (EM) to pickle food scraps, including meat and
dairy. The fermented material is then buried in soil where soil microbes complete
decomposition into humus within weeks, making it one of the fastest ways to
recycle kitchen waste into garden fertility.

## Detailed Explanation

Unlike traditional aerobic composting, [[bokashi-composting]] relies on anaerobic
lactic acid bacteria and yeast to ferment organic matter rather than decomposing
it. The process takes place in a sealed bucket with a drainage spigot, where
layers of food waste are inoculated with [[bokashi]] bran that has been
pre-inoculated with [[effective-microorganisms-em]]. Within two weeks, the waste
is pickled and preserved rather than rotting, which means it does not produce
the foul odors, fruit flies, or methane emissions associated with conventional
composting methods.

The science behind bokashi is rooted in the same principles that produce
sauerkraut, kimchi, and other lacto-fermented foods. The Effective Microorganisms
culture, developed by Japanese microbiologist Teruo Higa in the 1980s, contains
strains of Lactobacillus bacteria, yeast (Saccharomyces), and phototrophic
bacteria that suppress putrefying organisms. As these microbes multiply, they
produce lactic acid, acetic acid, and alcohol, lowering the pH to around 3.5 to
4.0. This acidic environment inhibits pathogenic bacteria like Salmonella and E.
coli while stabilizing nutrients, preventing nitrogen loss through volatilization
that commonly occurs in hot composting where temperatures drive off ammonia.

The [[bokashi-compost-method]] is remarkably simple and requires minimal space.
You need two specialized buckets with tight-fitting lids and drainage spigots at
the bottom. Food scraps are added daily, each layer sprinkled with a handful of
bokashi bran (roughly one to two tablespoons per inch of waste). The bucket is
pressed down firmly to exclude air, sealed, and set aside in a warm location.
Every few days, a nutrient-rich liquid called bokashi leachate drains from the
spigot. This amber-colored liquid can be diluted 1:100 with water and used as a
soil drench, or further diluted at 1:500 as a foliar feed, though it should be
used promptly as it is highly acidic at roughly pH 3.5.

One of bokashi's greatest advantages is that it accepts all food waste, including
meat, dairy, bones, cooked foods, and oily items that would cause pathogen
concerns or odor problems in a regular compost pile. This makes it especially
valuable for urban households and apartments where space for a large compost
system is unavailable. The [[comparison-bokashi-vs-vermicomposting]] analysis
shows that bokashi handles a wider range of inputs than worm bins, which
struggle with meat, dairy, and citrus. A typical household can process all of
its food waste in a standard two-bucket bokashi system.

After the two-week fermentation period, the bokashi pre-compost has a
distinctive sweet-sour pickle smell and the original food items are largely
intact but structurally softened. This material is not yet finished compost and
should not be placed directly on top of soil where it might attract animals. It
must be buried in soil at least six to eight inches deep, where soil microbes
complete the decomposition process within two to four weeks depending on
temperature and soil biology. Trenching between garden rows, burying in raised
beds between crops, or adding to a conventional compost pile are all effective
completion methods. Once buried, the material transforms into rich, dark humus
with excellent soil structure.

The [[comparison-vermicomposting-vs-hot-composting]] page notes that bokashi
bridges the gap between these two established methods. It is faster than
vermicomposting, requires less space and management than hot composting, and
retains more nutrients because the anaerobic process prevents nitrogen off-
gassing. The trade-off is that bokashi requires purchasing inoculant bran,
though the [[bokashi]] page explains how to make your own bran using EM-1
culture, molasses, and wheat bran, which dramatically reduces ongoing costs.

For gardeners integrating multiple composting methods, bokashi pre-compost makes
excellent worm food. The acidic fermented material is neutralized by passing
through an earthworm's gut, and worms process it rapidly into nutrient-dense
vermicast. The [[hot-composting-vs-vermicompost-vs-bokashi]] comparison details
how these three methods can be combined in sequence for maximum efficiency and
zero waste. The [[teaming-with-microbes-compost-biology]] research shows that
bokashi-treated material supports a different but complementary microbial
community compared to traditionally composted material.

## Key Points
- Bokashi uses anaerobic fermentation with Effective Microorganisms to pickle
  all types of food waste including meat, dairy, and bones
- The process takes only two weeks in a sealed bucket and produces no foul
  odors, fruit flies, or methane greenhouse gas emissions
- Bokashi leachate drained from the bucket is a concentrated liquid fertilizer
  when diluted, rich in organic acids and soluble nutrients
- Fermented bokashi must be buried in soil for two to four weeks to complete
  decomposition into plant-available humus
- Bokashi is ideal for small spaces, apartments, and households that generate
  diverse food waste that conventional composting cannot handle
