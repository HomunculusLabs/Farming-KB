# Best Goat Breeds for Small Homesteads and Permaculture Systems

A practical FAQ for selecting goat breeds suited to small-scale, sustainable homesteading
and permaculture integration.

---

## Q: What are the best dairy goat breeds for a small homestead?

- **Nigerian Dwarf** — Compact (50–75 lbs), high butterfat milk (6–10%), excellent for
  small lots and urban homesteads. A single doe can produce 1–2 quarts/day, plenty for
  a family's drinking and cheese needs. They breed year-round, unlike seasonal breeds.

- **Nubian** — Large breed with high butterfat (4–5%), known for friendly personalities.
  Very vocal (be prepared for loud calls). Great for cheese-making due to rich milk.
  They tolerate heat well but need protection from cold drafts.

- **LaMancha** — Easy-going temperament, reliable producers (~2–3 gallons/day in peak),
  and distinctive "gopher" or "elf" ears. One of the calmest dairy breeds — excellent
  for families. They perform well on varied forage and are efficient browse converters.

- **Alpine / Saanen** — High-volume producers (2–3+ gallons/day), reliable, and widely
  available. Alpines offer a good balance of volume and components; Saanens are the
  "Holsteins of the goat world" — pure white and prolific.

- **Oberhasli** — Hardy, docile, and moderate producers (~1–2 gallons/day). Beautiful
  bay coloring with black markings. Quiet disposition makes them good neighbors.
  Originally from Switzerland, they are cold-hardy and ideal for northern climates.

## Q: What are the best meat goat breeds?

- **Boer** — Fast-growing, heavily muscled, and the most popular meat breed in North
  America. Boer kids can reach 60–80 lbs by 90 days. Less parasite-resistant than
  alternatives; prone to foot rot in wet conditions.

- **Kiko** — Originally from New Zealand, exceptionally parasite-resistant, hardy, and
  low-maintenance. The go-to breed for permaculture brush management — they thrive on
  browse and pasture alone with minimal inputs.

- **Spanish** — Feral-origin, extremely hardy, heat tolerant, and low-input. Excellent
  brush clearers with superb survival instincts. A conservation priority representing
  a landrace adapted to the American Southeast over centuries.

- **Myotonic (Fainting Goat)** — Good meat-to-bone ratio, easy to contain (due to
  myotonia), and docile. Heritage breed worth conserving. They don't actually lose
  consciousness — muscles just stiffen when startled. Excellent on small acreage.

## Q: What about dual-purpose breeds?

- **Kinders** — A Nubian × Pygmy cross producing rich milk (~3.5–4.5% butterfat) and a
  good carcass. Medium-sized, excellent mothers. A true American breed developed in
  the Pacific Northwest; often productive for 8–10 years.

- **Pygmy** — Very small (50–75 lbs), used for both meat and modest milk production.
  Hardy, friendly, and ideal where space is very limited. Originally from West Africa.
  Pygmy milk is lower volume than Nigerian Dwarf but richer in butterfat.

- **Savannah** — Hardy, parasite-resistant meat breed from South Africa. Good mothers
  and efficient browse converters. Less common in the US but gaining popularity.
  Distinct white coat with black skin provides natural sun protection.

## Q: What breed considerations matter for permaculture integration?

- **Browse preference and brush-clearing ability** — Goats are browsers, not grazers.
  Spanish, Kiko, and Nigerian Dwarfs are aggressive browsers excellent for invasive
  species control (multiflora rose, kudzu, bittersweet, autumn olive).

- **Temperament and trainability** — LaManchas, Oberhasli, and Nigerian Dwarfs tend
  to be docile and easier to handle for rotational grazing systems. Trainable goats
  can be led with a collar and will return to shelter when called.

- **Climate adaptability** — Nubians struggle in extreme cold; Boers need shelter
  from heat. Kikos and Spanish goats tolerate heat and humidity well. Match breed to
  your USDA hardiness zone for best results.

- **Land requirements** — Plan 3–5 goats per acre on good browse/pasture, fewer on
  poor land. Rotate frequently to prevent parasite buildup. Overstocking is the #1
  cause of parasite problems in small herds.

- **Parasite resistance** — Kiko, Spanish, and Myotonic breeds have strong natural
  resistance, critical for sustainable systems minimizing chemical dewormers. Use
  FAMANCHA scoring to monitor individual animals rather than blanket deworming.

## Q: How many goats do I need to start?

- **Minimum herd size** — Goats are herd animals; never keep fewer than two. A lone
  goat will be stressed, unhealthy, and noisy. Budget for at least two from the start.

- **Space per goat** — Minimum 200 sq ft of outdoor space per goat, plus shelter.
  On pasture, stock at 3–5 per acre. Goats need dry, draft-free shelter but not
  heated barns. Good ventilation matters more than insulation.

- **Recommended starting configurations:**
  - *Dairy for home use:* 2–3 does (ideally in milk or bred), no buck (use AI or
    hire stud service). Avoids keeping a noisy, smelly buck year-round.
  - *Meat on brush:* 3–4 does + 1 buck of a hardy breed (Kiko or Spanish).
  - *Dual-purpose:* 2 Kinder does + 1 Kinder buck for a self-sustaining herd.

## Q: Where should I buy goats and what should I look for?

- **Testing for CAE, CL, and Johne's** — Insist on negative tests for CAE (Caprine
  Arthritis Encephalitis), CL (Caseous Lymphadenitis), and Johne's Disease. These
  are chronic, incurable, and can devastate a herd. Get written test results.

- **Conformation basics** — Look for strong feet and legs, a wide chest floor, sound
  udder attachment (for dairy), and overall good condition. Watch the goat walk;
  limping can indicate hoof or joint problems. Check that the bite is correct.

- **Age considerations** — Started does (1–3 years) are best for beginners. First-
  fresheners are often priced well. Avoid very young kids unless experienced with
  every-2-4-hour bottle-feeding.

- **Registered vs. grade animals** — Registered animals (ADGA, ABGA) provide traceable
  lineage and breeding value. Grade animals can be excellent producers at lower cost.
  For production-focused homesteads, grade does from tested herds are a smart buy.
