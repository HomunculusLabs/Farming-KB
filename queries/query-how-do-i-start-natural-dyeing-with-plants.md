# How Do I Start Natural Dyeing with Plants?

## Short Answer

Natural dyeing with plants involves extracting color pigments from
plant material and bonding them to fabric or fiber using a mordant.
You can start with common dye plants like indigo, marigold, onion
skins, and black walnut, using basic equipment most kitchens already
have.
A mordant (usually alum) is essential for making most plant colors
permanent and wash-fast on protein fibers like wool and silk, or
cellulose fibers like cotton and linen.

## Understanding Fibers

Not all fibers take dye equally well. Protein fibers (wool, silk,
alpaca, mohair) accept natural dyes readily and produce the richest,
most saturated colors with minimal preparation. Cellulose fibers
(cotton, linen, hemp, bamboo) are more stubborn and typically require
a two-step mordanting process with tannin followed by alum for good
results. The [[fiber-plants-permaculture]] page covers growing many of
these fiber crops in a homestead setting.

## Essential Mordants and Modifiers

A mordant is a metal salt or plant compound that creates a chemical
bridge between the fiber and the dye molecule, fixing the color
permanently. Alum (potassium aluminum sulfate) is the safest and most
versatile mordant for beginners. Iron (ferrous sulfate) darkens and
"saddens" colors, turning yellows into olive greens and pinks into
purples. Copper sulfate shifts yellows toward green and can brighten
some reds. Tannins from oak galls, sumac, or black tea serve as
pre-mordants for cellulose fibers. The
[[permaculture-designers-manual-tanbark-and-tannins]] page describes
traditional tannin sources from trees.

## Beginner-Friendly Dye Plants

Several plants produce strong, reliable colors with minimal
experience. Marigold (Tagetes erecta) yields bright yellows and golds.
Onion skins (both yellow and red varieties) produce warm golds, oranges,
and deep rusts. Black walnut hulls give rich browns without any
mordant. Avocado pits and skins produce surprisingly vivid pinks and
peach tones on protein fibers. Coreopsis (tickseed) flowers yield
oranges and reds. The [[permaculture-designers-manual-natural-dyes]]
page lists many traditional dye species suitable for permaculture
gardens.

## The Basic Dyeing Process

Start by scouring your fiber to remove oils, dirt, and manufacturing
finishes. Wool needs hot soapy water; cotton benefits from a boiling
wash with soda ash. Mordant your fiber by simmering it in a solution
of alum (15% weight of fiber for wool, 15-20% for cotton) for one
hour, then let it cool in the bath. Prepare your dye bath by chopping
plant material, covering it with water, and simmering for 30-60
minutes to extract pigment. Strain out the plant material, add your
pre-mordanted fiber, and simmer gently for 45-90 minutes. Let the
fiber cool in the dye bath for the deepest color. Rinse in cool water
until the water runs clear, then dry away from direct sunlight.

## Growing Your Own Dye Garden

A dedicated dye garden can supply a full palette of colors year-round.
[[mollison-designers-wool-processing-natural-dyeing]] describes
traditional integrated approaches. Coreopsis, marigold, dyer's
chamomile, and weld produce yellows. Madder root yields reds and
oranges after two to three years of growth. Woad and Japanese indigo
produce blues through different extraction methods. St. John's wort
gives reds and golds. Many of these plants double as ornamentals,
medicinals, or companion plants in a vegetable garden.

## Understanding Fast vs Fugitive Dyes

Not all natural dyes are equally permanent. "Fast" dyes bond firmly to
fiber and resist fading from washing and sunlight, while "fugitive"
dyes are beautiful initially but fade quickly. Indigo is among the
most lightfast and wash-fast natural dyes in existence. Walnut hulls
are also highly permanent even without a mordant. Beet juice and
berry juices, by contrast, are notoriously fugitive — they produce
stunning pinks and purples but fade within weeks in sunlight.
Understanding which dyes are fast helps you choose plants that produce
lasting results for garments and textiles you intend to wear and wash
regularly.

## Safety Considerations

Some mordants and dye processes require precautions. Alum is food-safe
and the least hazardous option. Copper and chrome mordants are toxic
and should be handled with gloves in well-ventilated spaces. Always
use dedicated dye pots, never pots used for food preparation. Wear
gloves when handling mordants and hot dye baths. Work in a
well-ventilated area. Dispose of mordant-containing wastewater
responsibly — copper and chrome should never go down the drain or
into waterways.

## Key Points

- Protein fibers dye more easily than cellulose fibers.
- Alum is the safest all-purpose mordant for beginners.
- Marigold, onion skins, and black walnut are reliable beginner dye
  plants.
- Always scour and mordant fiber before dyeing for wash-fast results.
- Iron modifies colors by darkening them, creating greens from yellows.
- A small dye garden can supply a full color palette within one growing
  season.
- Use dedicated equipment, never food preparation pots, for dyeing.
- Start a dye journal to record plant species, mordants, temperatures,
  and timing for reproducible results.
