     9|# What Is the Difference Between Psilocybin and Psilocin?
    10|
    11|## Short Answer
    12|Psilocybin and psilocin are two closely related psychedelic compounds found in
    13|magic mushrooms. Psilocybin is a stable prodrug that the body converts into
    14|psilocin, which is the active compound responsible for the psychedelic effects.
    15|
    16|## Detailed Explanation
    17|[[psilocybin]] and [[psilocin]] are both indole alkaloids belonging to the
    18|tryptamine class of compounds, and they are the primary psychoactive
    19|constituents found in psilocybin-containing mushrooms. While they are
    20|chemically similar, they differ in their molecular structure, stability, and
    21|how they interact with the human body. Understanding the distinction between
    22|these two compounds is essential for anyone interested in the chemistry,
    23|pharmacology, or effects of psychedelic mushrooms.
    24|
    25|Psilocybin (4-phosphoryloxy-N,N-dimethyltryptamine) contains a phosphate group
    26|attached to its molecular structure, making it more stable and water-soluble
    27|than psilocin. This phosphate group acts as a protective mechanism in the
    28|mushroom tissue, allowing the compound to persist without degrading rapidly.
    29|When dried mushrooms are stored properly, [[psilocybin]] remains relatively
    30|stable over time, which is why dried specimens retain their potency for months
    31|or even years when kept in cool, dark conditions.
    32|
    33|Psilocin (4-hydroxy-N,N-dimethyltryptamine) lacks the phosphate group and is
    34|the dephosphorylated, pharmacologically active form of the molecule. It is
    35|this compound that crosses the blood-brain barrier and binds primarily to
    36|serotonin 5-HT2A receptors, producing the characteristic visual, cognitive,
    37|and emotional effects of the psychedelic experience. Because it lacks the
    38|stabilizing phosphate group, [[psilocin]] is significantly less stable and
    39|degrades more quickly when exposed to heat, light, and oxygen.
    40|
    41|When a person ingests psilocybin-containing mushrooms, the body does not
    42|absorb psilocybin directly in its active form. Instead, enzymes in the gut and
    43|liver, primarily alkaline phosphatase, cleave the phosphate group from
    44|[[psilocybin]] to produce [[psilocin]]. This conversion process is why
    45|psilocybin is considered a prodrug. The onset of effects typically occurs 20
    46|to 60 minutes after ingestion, corresponding to the time needed for this
    47|metabolic conversion. Some psilocin may also be present directly in fresh
    48|mushrooms, but it exists in much smaller quantities compared to psilocybin.
    49|
    50|The biosynthesis of these compounds in fungal tissue follows a pathway that
    51|begins with the amino acid [[tryptophan]]. The enzyme [[tryptophan-decarboxylase]]
    52|converts tryptophan into tryptamine, which then undergoes hydroxylation and
    53|methylation steps through the [[psilocybin-biosynthesis-pathway]] to produce
    54|psilocybin. Along the way, the intermediate compound [[baeocystin]] is also
    55|produced, which is a demethylated analog of psilocybin found in varying
    56|concentrations across different mushroom species. The complete biochemistry of
    57|this pathway is detailed in the [[tihkal-psilocin-psilocybin-chemistry]]
    58|reference.
    59|
    60|Both compounds are classified as Schedule I substances in many jurisdictions,
    61|though their legal status is evolving rapidly as clinical research demonstrates
    62|therapeutic potential. When analyzing mushroom potency, laboratories typically
    63|measure both psilocybin and psilocin content, as well as [[baeocystin]] and
    64|norbaeocystin, to get a complete picture of a given specimen's psychoactive
    65|profile. The relative proportions of these compounds can vary significantly
    66|between species, growing conditions, and developmental stages of the mushroom.
    67|
    68|The pharmacokinetics of these compounds differ in ways that matter for both
recreational and therapeutic users. When psilocybin is administered orally, it
undergoes first-pass metabolism in the liver, where the phosphate group is
rapidly cleaved. Peak plasma concentrations of psilocin are typically reached
within 1.5 to 2 hours after ingestion, and the half-life of psilocin is
approximately 2 to 3 hours. The subjective effects generally last 4 to 6
hours, corresponding to the time required for psilocin to be metabolized and
eliminated. Understanding this metabolic pathway is important for clinical
researchers studying dose-response relationships and therapeutic protocols.

Legal and regulatory considerations also differ subtly between the two
compounds. In most jurisdictions, both psilocybin and psilocin are scheduled
substances, but testing methods typically detect psilocin rather than psilocybin
in biological samples because psilocybin is rapidly converted. The stability
differences between the two compounds also affect analytical testing of mushroom
specimens. Fresh mushrooms contain more psilocin relative to psilocybin than
dried specimens, as some psilocin degrades during the drying process. This is
why potency comparisons between fresh and dried material must account for the
different chemical profiles and moisture content.

## Key Points
    69|- Psilocybin is a stable prodrug with a phosphate group; psilocin is the
    70|  active metabolite without it
    71|- The body converts [[psilocybin]] to [[psilocin]] via alkaline phosphatase
    72|  enzymes in the gut and liver
    73|- Psilocin binds to serotonin 5-HT2A receptors to produce psychedelic effects
    74|- Psilocybin is more stable for storage; psilocin degrades quickly with heat
    75|  and light
    76|- Both compounds are biosynthesized from [[tryptophan]] through the
    77|  [[psilocybin-biosynthesis-pathway]]
