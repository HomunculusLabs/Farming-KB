# What Are the 12 Permaculture Principles?

## Short Answer

The 12 permaculture principles, developed by David Holmgren, are design guidelines for creating sustainable human settlements and agricultural systems that work with nature rather than against it. They build upon three core ethics: earth care, people care, and fair share, and provide a framework for decisions about land use, energy, water, food production, and community design.

## The Three Ethics

The [[permaculture-ethics]] establish the moral framework for all design decisions.

1. **Earth Care** - actively improving ecosystem health rather than degrading it
2. **People Care** - ensuring human needs for food, shelter, community, and meaningful work
3. **Fair Share** - distributing resources equitably and setting consumption limits

Every design decision should be evaluated against these three ethics.

## Principle 1: Observe and Interact

Spend time understanding the natural patterns of your site before making changes.

This connects to the broader [[permaculture-design-process]], which emphasizes thorough site analysis.

Good observation includes tracking sun angles, mapping water flow, noting prevailing winds, cataloging existing vegetation, and understanding soil types. The goal is to design with nature rather than imposing solutions without understanding context.

## Principle 2: Catch and Store Energy

Harvest and hold onto resources like solar energy, water, and biomass through rainwater harvesting, passive solar design, food preservation, building soil organic matter, and growing perennial food crops. The [[permaculture-zone-and-sector-planning]] framework helps organize these strategies spatially.

## Principle 3: Obtain a Yield

Sustainable systems must produce tangible, useful outputs. Design for redundancy of yields, diversify output types (food, medicine, fiber, fuel), and ensure surplus can be shared or preserved.

## Principle 4: Apply Self-Regulation and Accept Feedback

Monitor your systems and adjust based on results. Keep records of yields and observations, conduct regular site assessments, and be willing to abandon approaches that do not work.

## Principle 5: Use and Value Renewable Resources

Prioritize biological and renewable inputs over fossil-fuel-dependent ones: compost over synthetic fertilizer, biological pest control over chemical pesticides, solar and wind energy over grid power.

## Principle 6: Produce No Waste

Reframe "waste" as a resource in the wrong place. Compost food scraps, use greywater for irrigation, design closed-loop nutrient cycles, and repurpose materials in construction.

## Principle 7: Design from Patterns to Details

Establish broad patterns (water flow, access paths, canopy placement) before filling in details like individual plant selections. The [[permaculture-pattern-language]] provides vocabulary for recognizing natural patterns like branching, spirals, and waves.

## Principle 8: Integrate Rather Than Segregate

Elements in beneficial relationship produce more than the sum of their parts. Examples include chickens in orchards, aquaculture paired with irrigation, composting toilets paired with food production, and multi-functional infrastructure.

## Principle 9: Use Small and Slow Solutions

Start with small-scale experiments before committing to large interventions.

Benefits of small and slow:
- Lower risk if something fails
- More observation and learning opportunity
- Easier to adjust course
- Builds skills and confidence incrementally
- Often more energy efficient

## Principle 10: Use and Value Diversity

Diverse systems are more resilient, productive, and adaptable.

Diversity takes many forms:
- Polycultures over monocultures
- Multiple income streams
- Genetic diversity in seeds and livestock
- Mixed age structures in plantings
- Diverse habitat types for wildlife

## Principle 11: Use Edges and Value the Marginal

The interface between two ecosystems is often the most productive zone.

Edge examples:
- Pond edges (wetland to dryland transition)
- Forest edges (canopy to meadow transition)
- Hedgerows (field to field transition)
- Shorelines (water to land transition)

The [[permaculture-design-methodology]] incorporates edge analysis as a key tool.

## Principle 12: Creatively Use and Respond to Change

Change is inevitable; good design anticipates and works with it.

Applications:
- Climate adaptation planning
- Succession planning in food forests
- Flexible and adaptive design approaches
- Building resilience into systems
- Embracing disturbance as an opportunity

## Key Points

- The 12 principles are grounded in [[permaculture-ethics]] of earth care, people care, and fair share
- "Observe and Interact" is the first principle and the foundation of [[permaculture-design-process]]
- [[permaculture-zone-and-sector-planning]] puts principles into spatial practice
- The principles form an interconnected system where each reinforces the others
- [[permaculture-pattern-language]] provides tools for working with natural patterns
