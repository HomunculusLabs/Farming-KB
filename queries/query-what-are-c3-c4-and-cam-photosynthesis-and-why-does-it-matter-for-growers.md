## What Are C3, C4, and CAM Photosynthesis, and Why Does It Matter for Growers?

## What is photosynthesis?

Photosynthesis is the process by which plants convert light energy, water, and
[[carbon-dioxide-retention-jar-lid-mushroom-cultivation]] (CO₂) into sugars. All plants do this, but they use different
biochemical strategies to capture and process CO₂. The three main pathways are C3,
C4, and CAM—named after the number of carbon atoms in the first stable compound
produced during CO₂ fixation.

## What is C3 photosynthesis?

C3 is the most common and ancestral pathway, used by about 85% of plant species.
The enzyme RuBisCO directly captures CO₂ from the air and fixes it into a 3-carbon
compound (3-phosphoglycerate). C3 plants include most vegetables (tomato, pepper,
lettuce, beans), grains (wheat, rice, oats), [[fungal-endophytes-grass-crop-systems]]
like corn or sorghum, it's C4. If it's a succulent, cactus, pineapple, or
epiphytic orchid, it's CAM. When in doubt, look up the species—most plant databases
include photosynthetic pathway information.

## Why should a grower care about this?

Understanding your plant's photosynthetic pathway explains a lot about its behavior
and optimal growing conditions:

**Temperature sensitivity**: Your C3 tomatoes will struggle and may drop flowers
above 90°F (32°C) due to photorespiration, while your C4 corn will thrive in that
same heat. This explains why certain crops have defined [[solomon-climate-zones-and-growing-seasons]].

**Water needs**: C3 plants need consistent soil moisture because their stomata
must stay open during the day. CAM plants can go weeks without water. C4 plants
fall in between—more water-efficient than C3 but not as drought-tolerant as CAM.

**CO₂ enrichment**: C3 plants respond dramatically to elevated CO₂ (20–40% yield
increases in greenhouse studies) because RuBisCO is CO₂-limited. C4 and CAM plants
show little to no response because they already concentrate CO₂ internally. If
you're investing in CO₂ supplementation for a greenhouse, C3 crops like tomatoes
and lettuce give the best return.

**Nitrogen requirements**: C4 plants typically need more nitrogen per unit leaf
area because they maintain two sets of photosynthetic enzymes. This is why corn
is a heavy nitrogen feeder compared to wheat.

No plant switches between C3 and C4, though rare **C3–C4 intermediates** (like
*Flaveria* species) exhibit traits of both pathways and may represent evolutionary
transitional forms.

## Is it possible to engineer C4 photosynthesis into C3 crops?

This is one of the most ambitious goals in plant biotechnology. The C4 Rice Project
(IRRI) has worked for over two decades to introduce C4 photosynthesis into rice.
The challenge is that C4 requires not just new enzymes but also Kranz anatomy—
bundle sheath cells arranged around veins with coordinated gene expression in two
cell types. Progress has been made in understanding the genetic basis, but a fully
functional C4 rice has not yet been achieved.

If successful, projected benefits include 30–50% yield increases in tropical
regions, reduced water requirements, and lower [[jadam-natural-nitrogen-fertilizer]] needs. Similar
efforts are underway for wheat and soybean.

## Which common garden plants use each pathway?

**C3**: Tomato, pepper, eggplant, lettuce, spinach, beans, peas, carrots, onions,
potatoes, strawberries, apples, peaches, roses, most herbs (basil, cilantro,
parsley), most lawn grasses (Kentucky bluegrass, fescue), deciduous trees.

**C4**: Corn, sorghum, sugarcane, millet, amaranth. Common C4 weeds: crabgrass,
pigweed (Amaranthus), barnyard grass, johnsongrass, bermudagrass, foxtail.
