# How to Start Seed Saving

## Short Answer
Seed saving is the practice of harvesting, drying, and storing seeds from your garden plants to grow in future seasons. Beginners should start with easy self-pollinating species like tomatoes, beans, peas, and lettuce. The basic process involves selecting the best plants, allowing seeds to fully mature, properly drying them, and storing them in cool, dark, and dry conditions.

## Key Points
- Save seeds only from open-pollinated and heirloom varieties, not hybrids
- Start with easy self-pollinating crops: tomatoes, beans, peas, peppers, and lettuce
- Always select seeds from your healthiest, most productive plants
- Process dry-seeded crops by letting pods dry fully on the plant
- Ferment wet-seeded crops like tomatoes for three to five days
- Store dried seeds in glass jars with desiccant in a cool, dark place
- Label every container with variety, harvest year, and selection notes
- Test germination every one to two years to track seed viability

## Detailed Explanation

### Why Save Your Own Seeds?
Seed saving is one of the oldest human technologies and is experiencing a
resurgence as gardeners seek greater self-sufficiency and variety preservation.
When you save seeds from your own garden year after year, the resulting plants
gradually adapt to your specific climate, soil, and growing practices.
They often become more vigorous and disease-resistant than commercially produced
seeds.
The [[solomon-seed-saving-fundamentals]] guide explains that seed saving also
saves money, preserves genetic diversity, and gives gardeners access to varieties
that may not be available commercially.
This is especially valuable for gardeners practicing
[[query-what-are-the-benefits-of-no-till-gardening]], since locally adapted seeds
complement regenerative soil management.

### Open-Pollinated vs. Hybrid Varieties
The first and most important concept to understand is the difference between
open-pollinated and hybrid varieties. Open-pollinated (OP) varieties are stable
genetic lines that produce seeds true to the parent plant, meaning the offspring
will closely resemble the parents.
Heirloom varieties are a subset of open-pollinated types with a history of being
passed down through generations.
Hybrid (F1) varieties are crosses between two distinct parent lines, and their
seeds will not grow true. Seeds saved from hybrid plants produce offspring with
unpredictable traits.
The [[solomon-hybrid-vs-open-pollinated]] guide strongly recommends that seed
savers focus exclusively on open-pollinated and heirloom varieties.
The [[comparison-seed-saving-vs-hybrid-seeds]] page provides a detailed comparison
of the trade-offs between saving your own seeds and purchasing commercial hybrids.

| Seed Type | Grows True? | Best For | Beginner Friendly? |
|-----------|-------------|----------|-------------------|
| Open-Pollinated | Yes | Seed saving, heirlooms | Yes |
| Heirloom | Yes | Preservation, flavor | Yes |
| Hybrid (F1) | No | Commercial production | No |

### Choosing the Right Crops
Plant breeding behavior determines which species are suitable for beginners.
Self-pollinating species (selfers) have flowers that fertilize themselves before
opening, so cross-pollination between different varieties is rare.
Tomatoes, peppers, lettuce, peas, beans, and eggplant are all self-pollinating
and are ideal first seed-saving crops.
Cross-pollinating species (outcrossers) require pollen from a different plant of
the same species, meaning they can cross with other varieties growing nearby.
Squash, corn, beets, carrots, and brassicas are outcrossers and require isolation
distances to maintain seed purity.
The [[solomon-seed-saving-guide]] provides isolation distance requirements and
hand-pollination techniques for both categories.

#### Beginner-Friendly Self-Pollinating Crops
1. **Tomatoes** - Allow fruits to fully ripen, then ferment and rinse seeds
2. **Beans** - Let pods dry on the plant, shell, and store
3. **Peas** - Similar to beans; dry pods on the vine
4. **Peppers** - Scrape seeds from fully ripe fruit, air dry
5. **Lettuce** - Wait for seed heads to form and dry, then thresh

### Selecting the Best Plants
Selection is the art and science of choosing which plants to save seed from.
The fundamental principle is to save seed from the best specimens, not just the
ones that happened to survive.
Select for traits like disease resistance, flavor, yield, earliness, and
adaptation to your conditions. For tomatoes, save from the first ripe fruits of
the healthiest, most productive plants.
For lettuce, save from plants that bolt latest in hot weather. For beans and
peas, save from the most vigorous plants with the highest pod count.
The [[solomon-seed-saving-vegetable-breeding]] resource explains how consistent
selection over multiple seasons can develop locally adapted strains.
These strains often outperform commercial varieties in your specific garden
conditions. Healthy soil is the foundation of strong seed stock, so consider
[[query-how-to-build-healthy-garden-soil]] as a parallel practice.

### Seed Processing Methods
Seed processing varies by species. Dry-seeded crops like beans, peas, lettuce,
and radish produce seeds in dry pods or seed heads.
Allow the pods to fully dry on the plant, harvest, and thresh to separate seeds
from chaff. Wet-seeded crops like tomatoes, cucumbers, and squash have seeds
embedded in fleshy fruit.
For tomatoes, squeeze the seeds and gel into a jar, add water, and let ferment
for three to five days. The gel and pulp will float to the top while viable
seeds sink to the bottom.
Pour off the floating material, rinse the seeds, and spread them on a screen or
paper towel to dry.

**Dry-seeded vs. Wet-seeded Processing:**
- **Dry-seeded** (beans, peas, lettuce, radish):
  - Allow pods to dry completely on the plant
  - Harvest entire seed heads or pods
  - Thresh by crushing pods gently
  - Winnow to separate seeds from chaff
- **Wet-seeded** (tomatoes, cucumbers, squash):
  - Scoop seeds and pulp into a container
  - Ferment for 3-5 days (tomatoes) or rinse immediately
  - Rinse seeds through a fine mesh strainer
  - Spread on a screen to dry at room temperature

### Proper Seed Storage
Proper storage is the final critical step. Seeds store best in cool (32 to 50
degrees Fahrenheit), dark, and dry (below 8 percent moisture content) conditions.
Glass jars with tight-fitting lids work well, especially when silica gel
desiccant packets are added to absorb residual moisture.
For long-term storage, seeds can be kept in a freezer, which extends viability
for many species to ten years or more.
Label every jar with the variety name, harvest year, and any selection notes
about the parent plants.
The [[solomon-seed-storage-for-maximum-viability]] guide emphasizes that seeds
must be completely dry before storage to prevent mold and maintain germination rates.
Regular germination testing every one to two years helps track viability and
identify when seeds need to be replanted to refresh your stock.
