## What Plants Can I Grow Near Black Walnut Trees?

## Short Answer

Black walnut trees ([[juglans-nigra]]) produce juglone, a natural herbicide that stunts or kills many common garden vegetables and ornamentals.
The toxic zone extends from the tree's drip line outward through the root system, which can reach well beyond the canopy edge.
Despite this challenge, dozens of useful plants tolerate juglone well, including [[pawpaw]], [[jerusalem-artichoke]], and several berry bushes.

## Why Black Walnut Is Toxic to Other Plants

Juglone (5-hydroxy-1,4-naphthoquinone) is produced by all parts of the black walnut tree — roots, leaves, nut hulls, and bark.
This chemical phenomenon is called [[allelopathy-and-chemical-warfare-in-plants]], and black walnut is one of the most potent allelopathic species in North America.
The compound inhibits respiration in susceptible plants, causing wilting, yellowing, and eventual death.
Symptoms often appear within days of root contact with contaminated soil.
Juglone persists in soil even after a tree is removed, lingering for years in decomposing roots.

### Understanding the Toxic Zone

The highest juglone concentrations occur within the tree's drip line, where roots are densest.
However, black walnut roots can extend 50 to 80 feet beyond the canopy, making the actual danger zone much larger.
Soil type matters: well-drained soils flush juglone faster, while heavy clay retains it longer.
The [[black-walnut-juglans-allelopathy]] pattern follows root density — the closer to the trunk, the more toxic the soil.

### Juglone-Sensitive Plants to Avoid

Tomatoes, potatoes, peppers, and eggplant (Solanaceae family) are extremely sensitive to juglone.
Other highly susceptible plants include cabbage, broccoli, Brussels sprouts, and most brassicas.
Blackberries, blueberries, and azaleas also suffer severe damage near black walnuts.
Asparagus, rhubarb, and peonies are notably intolerant and will decline rapidly.
Even apple trees struggle near black walnuts due to root-zone juglone exposure.

## Juglone-Tolerant Fruit Trees and Shrubs

Several fruit-bearing trees and shrubs thrive despite juglone exposure.
[[pawpaw]] (Asimina triloba) is perhaps the ideal companion — it naturally grows as an understory tree beneath black walnut in eastern forests.
[[elderberry-cultivation-and-variety-guide|elderberry]] (Sambucus canadensis) tolerates juglone well and produces abundant fruit for jams, wine, and medicine.
[[currant-and-gooseberry-cultivation|Currants and gooseberries]] (Ribes species) perform reliably near black walnuts.
[[haskap-honeyberry-cultivation-and-variety-guide|Honeyberry]] (Lonicera caerulea) and [[aronia-melanocarpa|aronia]] are also juglone-tolerant superfruit options.
[[kiwifruit-cultivation-and-variety-guide|Kiwifruit]] vines can be trained to climb walnut trees without suffering damage.

## Juglone-Tolerant Vegetables and Root Crops

[[jerusalem-artichoke]] (Helianthus tuberosus) is a standout — it produces prolifically near black walnuts and can even help break up compacted soil.
Squash, melons, and cucumbers generally tolerate juglone without problems.
Beans, corn, and onions are moderately tolerant and usually productive in the root zone.
Carrots, parsnips, and beets often grow well despite juglone presence in the soil.
Cherry tomatoes show more tolerance than standard slicing tomato varieties.

## Juglone-Tolerant Herbs and Flowers

Many culinary herbs thrive near black walnuts, making them excellent [[companion-planting-and-polycultures|companion planting]] choices.
Mint, lemon balm, and other members of the Lamiaceae family are highly tolerant.
[[echinacea-cultivation-and-medicinal-uses|Echinacea]] (coneflower) grows vigorously in juglone-contaminated soil.
Yarrow, black-eyed Susans, and [[monarda-didyma|bee balm]] are reliable juglone-tolerant perennials.
Zinnias, marigolds, and nasturtiums provide colorful, juglone-resistant annual options.

## Designing a Black Walnut Guild

In [[permaculture-plant-guilds|permaculture guild]] design, black walnut can serve as the canopy layer of a productive polyculture.
The key is layering juglone-tolerant species at every vertical niche — canopy, understory, shrub, herbaceous, ground cover, and root zone.
[[pawpaw]] fills the understory niche, while [[elderberry-cultivation-and-variety-guide|elderberry]] occupies the shrub layer.
[[jerusalem-artichoke]] works as both a root crop and a tall herbaceous layer plant.
Nitrogen fixers like [[nitrogen-fixing-trees-and-shrubs-for-permaculture|black locust]] (Robinia pseudoacacia) also tolerate juglone and improve soil fertility.

### What About Other Walnut Species?

English walnut ([[juglans-regia]]) produces much less juglone than black walnut and is safer to garden beneath.
Butternut ([[juglans-cinerea]]) falls somewhere between black and English walnut in juglone production.
Hickories (Carya species) are close relatives but generally produce negligible juglone.
If you have a choice, English walnut is the more garden-friendly option for [[nut-tree-cultivation-for-permaculture-food-forests|nut tree cultivation]].

### What About Growing Mushrooms Under Black Walnut?

Interestingly, many mushroom species fruit happily on black walnut wood and in its root zone.
Oyster mushrooms and [[white-mulberry-permaculture-mollison|wine cap]] (Stropharia rugosoannulata) can be cultivated on walnut wood chips.
The lignin-rich walnut wood is an excellent substrate for wood-decomposing fungi.
This makes black walnut a dual-purpose tree: nuts for food and wood for mushroom cultivation.

## Practical Tips for Gardening Near Black Walnuts

If you cannot remove the tree, raised beds with a fabric barrier and imported soil can create safe pockets for sensitive crops.
Raking and removing fallen leaves and nut hulls each autumn reduces juglone buildup in the topsoil.
Adding organic matter and keeping soil well-drained accelerates juglone breakdown by soil microorganisms.
Test soil drainage in the [[soil-ph-and-nutrient-availability|affected area]] — poorly drained spots concentrate juglone.
Compost and [[cover-crop-quick-reference|cover crops]] that tolerate juglone can help remediate contaminated soil over time.

### What About Removing a Black Walnut Tree?

If you decide to remove a black walnut, juglone persists in the soil for years as roots decompose.
Grinding the stump and major roots speeds decomposition but does not eliminate juglone immediately.
Planting juglone-tolerant species for two to three seasons after removal is the safest strategy.
Some gardeners plant [[dynamic-accumulator-quick-reference|dynamic accumulators]] like comfrey to help pull residual juglone from the soil.
The decaying wood can also be inoculated with mushroom spawn, turning a problem into a productive resource.

## Key Points

- Black walnut ([[juglans-nigra]]) produces juglone, a potent allelopathic compound that kills many common garden plants.
- The toxic zone extends well beyond the drip line through an extensive root system.
- Tolerant fruits include [[pawpaw]], [[elderberry-cultivation-and-variety-guide|elderberry]], [[currant-and-gooseberry-cultivation|currants]], and [[aronia-melanocarpa|aronia]].
- Tolerant vegetables include [[jerusalem-artichoke]], squash, beans, corn, and carrots.
- Herbs like [[echinacea-cultivation-and-medicinal-uses|echinacea]], mint, and [[monarda-didyma|bee balm]] thrive despite juglone.
- Design a [[permaculture-plant-guilds|juglone-tolerant guild]] by stacking tolerant species at every layer.
- Solanaceae (tomatoes, peppers, potatoes) and most brassicas are highly sensitive and should be kept far away.
- Raised beds with imported soil can create safe growing zones within the root zone.
- After tree removal, juglone persists for years — plant tolerant species during the transition period.
