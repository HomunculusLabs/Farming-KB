# How to Make a DIY Still Air Box for Mushroom Cultivation

## Short Answer
A still air box (SAB) is a simple enclosed workspace that blocks airflow to create a
contaminant-free environment for sterile mushroom cultivation tasks like agar work, spore
germination, and grain inoculation. You can build an effective SAB from a large clear
plastic tote box with two arm holes cut into it. Despite its simplicity, a properly used
SAB can achieve contamination rates comparable to expensive laminar flow hoods.

## Detailed Explanation

The still air box is the most accessible piece of sterile equipment available to
mushroom cultivators, and for many home growers, it is the only piece of specialized
equipment needed to produce clean cultures. The principle is straightforward: by
enclosing your workspace and eliminating air currents, you prevent airborne contaminants
like mold spores and bacteria from settling on your sterile media. While a flow hood
provides positive pressure with filtered air, a SAB relies on the simple fact that still
air does not carry contaminants. When combined with proper technique, the results can be
excellent. For growers considering their full lab setup, the SAB is often the starting
point discussed in [[query-how-to-set-up-a-mushroom-grow-lab-or-clean-room]].

### Choosing the Right Container

The ideal SAB container is a large, clear plastic storage tote, typically 50 to 116
quarts in volume. The key requirements are that it is transparent so you can see your
work, large enough to comfortably fit your hands, tools, and media inside, and tall
enough that your arms can move without touching the walls. Polycarbonate or polypropylene
totes work well because they are durable and can withstand wiping down with 70 percent
isopropyl alcohol. Avoid thin, flimsy containers that flex when you press against them,
as this movement creates air currents that defeat the purpose. The lid should fit snugly
to maintain the still air environment.

### Cutting Arm Holes

Arm holes should be positioned on opposite sides of the tote, roughly 6 to 8 inches in
diameter, and placed at a height that is comfortable for you to work through. Most
cultivators place them slightly below center so that the upper portion of the box serves
as a protective canopy above the workspace. Use a sharp utility knife or a hole saw to
cut clean circles. Smooth the edges with sandpaper or a lighter to remove any rough
plastic that could snag sleeves or scratch your arms. Some growers attach rubber grommets
or latex sleeves around the arm holes, but this is not strictly necessary and can
actually introduce additional surfaces where contaminants can hide.

### Preparing the SAB for Use

Before each use, wipe down the entire interior of the SAB with 70 percent isopropyl
alcohol. Place all of your tools, media, and cultures inside the box before starting
your work. This includes your inoculation loop or syringe, agar plates, grain jars,
alcohol lamp, and any other items you will need. Once everything is inside, close the
lid and let the air settle for 10 to 15 minutes. This settling period is critical: it
allows any dust and spores that were stirred up when you placed items inside to drift to
the bottom of the box, leaving the working zone in the upper portion relatively clean.

### Proper Technique Inside the SAB

Technique matters more than the box itself. Always work slowly and deliberately inside
the SAB. Avoid talking, coughing, or making sudden movements that create air currents.
Keep your movements within the center of the box and away from the walls and lid, as
these surfaces are more likely to harbor contaminants. Flame-sterilize your tools
inside the box using an alcohol lamp or butane torch, passing them through the flame
until they glow red before each use. Never open a sterile container unless it is inside
the SAB with the lid closed. These practices are foundational to preventing
contamination, as outlined in [[contaminant-identification-and-prevention]].

### Common SAB Tasks

The SAB is suitable for virtually all sterile cultivation tasks. Agar work, including
transferring mycelium between plates and inoculating agar from spores, is one of the
most common uses. See [[query-how-to-make-agar-plates-for-mushroom-cultivation]] for
details on preparing the plates you will work with inside the SAB. Grain-to-grain
transfers and [[agar-to-grain-wedge-transfer-technique]] are also routinely performed in
a SAB. Additionally, [[mushroom-liquid-culture-techniques]] such as creating and
transferring liquid culture syringes can be done effectively in a still air box.

### SAB vs. Flow Hood

While a laminar flow hood is the gold standard for sterile cultivation work, a SAB is
more than adequate for most home growers. The main advantage of a SAB is cost: a basic
setup can be built for under 20 dollars, whereas a flow hood costs several hundred to
over a thousand dollars. The main disadvantage is comfort and speed. Working through arm
holes is awkward, and tasks take longer than they would on an open bench with a flow
hood. However, with practice, contamination rates of 1 to 5 percent are achievable in a
SAB, which is acceptable for most hobby-scale cultivation.

### Troubleshooting Contamination

If you are experiencing high contamination rates while using a SAB, the problem is almost
always technique rather than the box itself. Common mistakes include opening the lid
before the air has settled, reaching over open containers instead of moving them, wearing
contaminated clothing, and working in a drafty room. Close windows and turn off fans and
HVAC vents before starting work. Consider taping plastic sheeting over doorways to
minimize drafts in your workspace. For a comprehensive guide to identifying and
preventing contamination issues, refer to [[query-how-to-prevent-and-troubleshoot-mushroom-contamination]].

Proper [[jar-preparation-and-sterilization]] is also essential. Even perfect SAB
technique will not save you if your grain jars were not properly sterilized before
inoculation. Always pressure-cook grain jars at 15 PSI for 90 to 120 minutes to ensure
complete sterilization. Allow jars to cool fully inside the pressure cooker or in the SAB
with the lid on before opening them for inoculation.

## Key Points
- A still air box is a large clear plastic tote with two arm holes that provides a
  still-air environment for sterile mushroom cultivation work
- Choose a 50 to 116 quart clear polycarbonate tote, cut 6 to 8 inch arm holes on
  opposite sides, and smooth the edges
- Wipe interior with 70 percent isopropyl alcohol, place all tools inside, close the
  lid, and wait 10 to 15 minutes for air to settle before working
- Work slowly, keep movements in the center of the box, flame-sterilize tools inside,
  and never open sterile containers outside the SAB as per
  [[contaminant-identification-and-prevention]]
- A well-used SAB can achieve 1 to 5 percent contamination rates, making it a viable
  alternative to expensive laminar flow hoods for home cultivators
