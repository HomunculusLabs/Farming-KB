## How Do I Use Biological Control to Manage Pests Organically?

## Short Answer

Biological control (biocontrol) uses living organisms like predators, parasites, and pathogens to suppress pest populations.
It is the cornerstone of organic pest management and works with natural ecosystems instead of fighting them.
Unlike chemical pesticides, biocontrol agents reproduce and spread on their own, providing long-term protection.
The three main approaches are conservation (supporting existing beneficials), augmentation (releasing additional agents), and classical (introducing new natural enemies).

## What Is Biological Control?

Biological control harnesses natural relationships between organisms to keep pest populations below damaging levels.
[[beneficial-insects-and-biological-pest-control|Beneficial insects]] are the most visible biocontrol agents, but the field extends far beyond insects.
Microorganisms like [[trichoderma|Trichoderma fungi]] and [[bacillus-thuringiensis|Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt)]] attack pests at the microscopic level.
The concept has been practiced for centuries but was formalized in the late 1800s with the vedalia beetle introduction to control cottony cushion scale in California.

Modern biocontrol integrates seamlessly with [[integrated-pest-management|Integrated Pest Management (IPM)]] frameworks.
It reduces reliance on synthetic chemicals while maintaining effective pest suppression.
Biocontrol is especially important in organic systems where chemical options are limited.
The approach works best when the garden or farm has high biodiversity and minimal pesticide use.

## Conservation Biocontrol: Supporting Existing Beneficials

Conservation biocontrol is the easiest and most cost-effective approach for home gardeners.
It involves modifying the habitat to support populations of natural enemies already present in the landscape.
Planting [[insectary-plants-guide|insectary plants]] provides nectar, pollen, and shelter for beneficial insects throughout the season.
Flowers in the Umbelliferae (dill, fennel, coriander) and Asteraceae (yarrow, daisies) families are particularly attractive to [[beneficial-insects-pollinators|pollinators and predators]].

Avoiding broad-spectrum pesticides is essential because these kill beneficials along with pests.
Even organic sprays like pyrethrin can harm beneficial insects if applied carelessly.
Providing water sources, ground cover, and overwintering habitat helps maintain year-round beneficial populations.
[[coleman-beneficial-insects-and-ecological-pest-control|Ecological pest control]] emphasizes creating complete habitat rather than just adding individual species.
Companion planting with pest-repelling species complements conservation biocontrol by reducing pest pressure.

## Augmentation Biocontrol: Releasing Beneficial Organisms

Augmentation involves purchasing and releasing natural enemies to boost their numbers.
This is useful when existing beneficials are insufficient or when dealing with a sudden pest outbreak.
[[comparison-predatory-mites-vs-ladybugs-biocontrol|Predatory mites and ladybugs]] are among the most commonly purchased biocontrol agents.
[[comparison-parasitoid-wasps-vs-chemical-pesticides|Parasitoid wasps]] are highly effective against aphids, whiteflies, and caterpillars.

Timing is critical for successful releases — beneficials must be present when the pest population is still low.
Releasing too late means the pest has already caused significant damage and reproduces faster than the beneficials can control.
Follow release rates carefully and choose species matched to your specific pest problem.
[[cervantes-biological-pest-control-predators|Biological pest control for indoor gardens]] requires special consideration for enclosed environments.
Repeated releases may be necessary throughout the growing season for persistent pest problems.

## Microbial Biocontrol: Bacteria, Fungi, and Beyond

Microbial biocontrol agents are microscopic organisms that attack specific pests or diseases.
[[bacillus-thuringiensis|Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt)]] is the most widely used microbial insecticide, producing toxins lethal to caterpillars and mosquito larvae.
[[beauveria-bassiana|Beauveria bassiana]] is an [[entomopathogenic-fungi|entomopathogenic fungus]] that infects and kills insects by growing through their bodies.
[[comparison-beauveria-bassiana-vs-metarhizium-anisopliae|Metarhizium anisopliae]] is another entomopathogenic fungus effective against soil-dwelling pests like grubs and termites.

[[comparison-trichoderma-vs-bacillus-vs-pseudomonas-biocontrol|Trichoderma vs Bacillus vs Pseudomonas]] compares the major biocontrol microbes used against plant diseases.
[[beneficial-fungi-trichoderma-soil-health|Trichoderma]] species are particularly valuable because they both suppress pathogens and promote plant growth.
[[aact-trichoderma-biocontrol-ingham|Compost tea enriched with Trichoderma]] provides an easy application method for soil and foliar disease prevention.
[[entomopathogenic-fungi-and-biological-pest-control|Entomopathogenic fungi]] offer broad-spectrum insect control with minimal impact on beneficials.

## Trap Cropping and Push-Pull Strategies

Trap cropping uses plants that are more attractive to pests than the main crop, drawing pests away from valuable plants.
[[comparison-trap-cropping-vs-beneficial-insects|Trap cropping vs beneficial insects]] shows how these approaches complement each other in an integrated system.
[[comparison-insectary-plants-vs-trap-cropping|Insectary plants vs trap cropping]] compares two popular biocontrol habitat strategies.
The push-pull strategy combines a repellent intercrop (push) with an attractive trap crop (pull) to manage pests systematically.
[[comparison-companion-planting-reference-vs-trap-cropping|Companion planting and trap cropping]] work together to create a multi-layered pest management approach.

## What About Biocontrol in Cannabis Growing?

Cannabis cultivation has embraced biocontrol because of strict residue limits and the premium placed on clean flowers.
[[beneficial-insects-cannabis|Beneficial insects for cannabis]] include predatory mites for spider mites, rove beetles for fungus gnats, and parasitoid wasps for aphids.
[[cervantes-bacillus-thuringiensis-bt-control|Bt for cannabis pest control]] targets caterpillars and fungus gnat larvae in both indoor and outdoor grows.
[[cervantes-trichoderma-beneficial-fungi|Trichoderma for cannabis]] provides disease suppression and root zone protection.
[[diatomaceous-earth-cannabis|Diatomaceous earth for cannabis]] offers physical pest control as a complement to living biocontrol agents.

## What About Biocontrol for Plant Diseases?

Biocontrol is not limited to insect pests — it is equally important for disease management.
[[comparison-trichoderma-vs-entomopathogenic-fungi|Trichoderma vs entomopathogenic fungi]] illustrates how different microbial agents target different problems.
[[biodiversity-of-fungi-plant-health-pathogens-biocontrol|Fungal biodiversity and plant health]] explores how diverse fungal communities suppress pathogens naturally.
Endophytic fungi and mycorrhizae contribute to plant immunity through induced systemic resistance.
[[ectomycorrhizal-biocontrol-root-pathogens|Mycorrhizal biocontrol]] protects roots from soil-borne pathogens by forming physical and chemical barriers.

## Key Points

- Biological control uses predators, parasites, and pathogens to manage pests without synthetic chemicals.
- Conservation biocontrol supports existing [[beneficial-insects-guide|beneficial insects]] through habitat creation.
- Augmentation involves releasing purchased natural enemies like [[comparison-predatory-mites-vs-ladybugs-biocontrol|predatory mites and ladybugs]].
- Microbial agents include [[bacillus-thuringiensis|Bt]], [[beauveria-bassiana|Beauveria]], and [[beneficial-fungi-trichoderma-soil-health|Trichoderma]].
- Trap cropping and [[comparison-trap-cropping-vs-companion-planting|companion planting]] enhance biocontrol by managing pest movement.
- Biocontrol works best as part of an [[integrated-pest-management|IPM]] strategy rather than a standalone solution.
- Avoiding broad-spectrum sprays preserves beneficial populations already present in your garden.
- [[comparison-neem-oil-vs-bacillus-thuringiensis|Neem oil vs Bt]] shows how organic sprays and microbial agents can be combined.
- Timing is critical — release beneficials early before pest populations explode.
- [[entomopathogenic-fungi-biocontrol-mycopesticides|Mycopesticides]] represent the cutting edge of targeted biological pest control.

## What About Combining Biocontrol with Organic Sprays?

Biocontrol agents can be combined with organic pesticides for a layered defense strategy.
The key is choosing sprays that are selective and applying them at times when beneficials are less active.
[[comparison-neem-oil-vs-diatomaceous-earth|Neem oil vs diatomaceous earth]] compares two popular organic options that have different impacts on beneficial organisms.
[[diatomaceous-earth-and-natural-pest-management|Diatomaceous earth]] is relatively safe for beneficials when applied to soil rather than foliar surfaces.
Spinosad is effective against caterpillars but toxic to bees — apply only in the evening when bees are not foraging.
Always read labels carefully and observe the impact on beneficial populations after any spray application.
