## What Exactly Is Glomalin?

Glomalin belongs to a family of glycoproteins called glomalin-related soil proteins (GRSP).

These proteins are exuded through the hyphal walls of [[hamilton-composting-and-organic-matter-management]] held together by binding agents.

Glomalin is one of the most important of these binding agents in soils dominated by AMF associations.

As fungal hyphae grow through soil, they deposit glomalin along their length, coating mineral particles.

When hyphae die and decompose, the glomalin persists and continues to hold soil particles together.

This process creates water-stable aggregates that resist erosion and improve soil structure.

## Factors That Increase Glomalin Production

Several management practices and environmental conditions favor higher glomalin concentrations in soil.

**Minimal or no-till farming** preserves mycorrhizal hyphal networks, allowing continuous glomalin deposition.

**Perennial crops and permanent pastures** maintain unbroken mycorrhizal associations year-round.

**Cover cropping** provides living root networks that sustain AMF populations between cash crops.

**Reduced synthetic fertilizer use**, especially high-phosphorus fertilizers, avoids suppressing AMF colonization.

**Diverse crop rotations** support a wider range of AMF species and potentially greater glomalin production.

**[[fungal-glomalin-and-soil-carbon|relationship between glomalin and soil carbon]] varies by ecosystem type.

Grasslands and forests typically accumulate more glomalin than annual croplands.

Restoring degraded cropland to perennial vegetation can rebuild glomalin stocks over a period of 5 to 15 years.
