# How Do I Identify and Fix Magnesium Deficiency in Cannabis?

## Short Answer
Magnesium deficiency in cannabis causes interveinal chlorosis (yellowing between leaf
veins while veins remain green) that typically starts on the lower, older leaves and
progresses upward. It is one of the most common cannabis nutrient problems, especially
in hydroponic systems and coco coir where magnesium is not naturally abundant. Treatment
involves applying a magnesium source such as Epsom salt (magnesium sulfate), cal-mag
supplements, or dolomite lime, and correcting any underlying pH imbalance that may be
preventing magnesium uptake.

## Detailed Explanation

Magnesium is a mobile macronutrient that plays an essential role in cannabis plant
health as the central atom in the chlorophyll molecule, making it directly responsible
for the plant's ability to perform photosynthesis. Without adequate magnesium, plants
cannot efficiently convert light energy into chemical energy, and growth stalls
noticeably. The [[cannabis-nutrient-deficiency-guide]] provides visual references for
identifying magnesium deficiency and distinguishing it from other nutrient problems
that produce similar symptoms.

The classic symptom pattern of magnesium deficiency is distinctive once you learn to
recognize it. It begins as interveinal chlorosis on the lower, older leaves, meaning
the tissue between the veins turns yellow while the veins themselves remain dark green.
As the deficiency progresses, the yellowing areas may develop rust-colored spots or
necrotic (dead brown) patches on the leaf margins and tips, and the symptoms spread
upward to younger leaves as the plant continues to mobilize its limited magnesium
supply. The [[cervantes-macronutrient-deficiency-toxicity]] page from Jorge Cervantes'
work provides detailed descriptions and photographs of progressive magnesium deficiency
symptoms at each stage.

Because magnesium is a mobile nutrient, the plant relocates it from older leaves to
support new growth when supplies are insufficient. This is why deficiency symptoms
always appear on the lower canopy first. The [[cervantes-nutrient-mobility-deficiency-diagnosis]] page explains this mobility principle in depth and provides a diagnostic
framework that uses leaf position to narrow down which nutrient is deficient. If you
see interveinal chlorosis on new growth rather than old growth, you are more likely
dealing with an iron deficiency or manganese deficiency rather than magnesium.

Several factors make cannabis plants particularly susceptible to magnesium deficiency.
Coco coir, one of the most popular growing mediums, naturally contains very little
magnesium and can actually bind magnesium from nutrient solutions, making deficiency
almost inevitable without supplementation. The [[cervantes-sinsemilla-cultivation]]
page emphasizes the importance of cal-mag supplementation in all coco grows. Hydroponic
systems with reverse osmosis water also lack the magnesium naturally present in tap
water, so growers using RO must supplement accordingly. Even soil growers can encounter
magnesium deficiency if their soil pH is too low (below 6.0) because acidic conditions
reduce magnesium availability.

Fixing magnesium deficiency requires both immediate correction and addressing the
underlying cause. The fastest treatment for established deficiency is a foliar spray
of Epsom salt solution (1 to 2 teaspoons of magnesium sulfate per gallon of water)
applied to the undersides of affected leaves, which bypasses the root uptake pathway
and delivers magnesium directly where it is needed. For systemic correction, adding
a cal-mag supplement to your regular feeding provides both calcium and magnesium in
balanced ratios. The [[cannabis-nutrient-recipes]] page includes specific supplement
recipes for treating magnesium deficiency at different growth stages.

pH correction is essential for lasting results. Even if you add sufficient magnesium,
the plant cannot absorb it if the root zone pH is out of range. In soil, magnesium is
optimally available between pH 6.0 and 7.0. In hydroponic and coco systems, the ideal
range is 5.5 to 6.5. The [[cannabis-nutrient-lockout-and-ph]] page explains how pH
affects nutrient availability and provides step-by-step pH adjustment protocols.
Testing your runoff water can reveal whether pH is the root cause of the problem.

Prevention is more effective than treatment. In coco coir grows, adding a cal-mag
supplement to every watering from the beginning virtually eliminates magnesium
deficiency. In soil grows, incorporating dolomite lime (which contains both calcium
carbonate and magnesium carbonate) into your soil mix provides a slow-release source
of magnesium that buffers pH simultaneously. The [[micronutrient-deficiency-symptoms]]
page covers the full range of micronutrient deficiencies and their prevention
strategies, noting that magnesium sits on the boundary between macro and micronutrient
importance and is often overlooked in basic nutrient programs.
