# How to Grow Amaranth for Grain and Greens

## Short Answer

Amaranth is a warm-season annual that produces both nutritious edible leaves and protein-rich grain heads.

It thrives in full sun with well-drained soil and tolerates heat and drought far better than most garden crops.

The plant is related to [[chenopodium-quinoa|quinoa]] and shares its reputation as a complete-protein pseudocereal.

You can harvest tender young leaves throughout the [[savory-growing-season-and-non-growing-season-management]] and collect grain from mature seed heads in fall.

Amaranth is one of the easiest grain crops to grow on a home scale, requiring minimal inputs once established.

## What Makes Amaranth Special

Amaranth has been cultivated for over 8,000 years and was a staple food of the Aztecs and other Mesoamerican civilizations.

The genus includes roughly 70 species, but only a handful are widely grown for food today.

Unlike true cereal grains like wheat and corn, amaranth is a pseudocereal, meaning it produces a seed that is used like a grain but comes from a broadleaf plant.

Its seeds contain roughly 13-14% protein with a complete amino acid profile, including lysine, which is typically low in other grains.

The leaves are also highly nutritious, packing more iron, calcium, and vitamin C than spinach when eaten raw or lightly cooked.

Amaranth is naturally drought-tolerant once established, making it an excellent choice for dryland gardens and water-conscious growers.

## Choosing the Right Variety

Different amaranth species are bred or selected for either [[fukuoka-no-till-grain-production-method]], leaf production, or ornamental use.

[[amaranthus-cruentus|Amaranthus cruentus]] is the most versatile species, producing both edible leaves and substantial grain heads.

It is widely grown in Mexico, [[guzman-allen-gartz-neurotropic-fungi-central-america-caribbean]], and parts of Africa where it is known as huauzontle or bledo.

[[amaranthus-hypochondriacus|Amaranthus hypochondriacus]] is the premier grain-producing species with the largest seed heads.

This species was a sacred crop of the Aztecs and produces yields comparable to quinoa in favorable conditions.

[[amaranthus-caudatus|Amaranthus caudatus]] is commonly called love-lies-bleeding for its dramatic drooping flower tassels.

While often grown ornamentally, it also produces edible grain and is popular in South Asian cuisine.

[[amaranthus-tricolor|Amaranthus tricolor]] is primarily grown for its spectacular edible leaves that come in red, yellow, and green.

This species is popular in Asian stir-fries and salads and performs best when harvested young and tender.

[[amaranthus-spinosus|Amaranthus spinosus]] is a wild species sometimes foraged for its nutritious leaves, though it has spiny stems.

It is less commonly cultivated but worth knowing for wildcrafters and permaculture foragers.

## Planting and Soil Requirements

Amaranth is a warm-season crop that should be direct-seeded after all danger of frost has passed.

Soil temperatures should be at least 65 degrees Fahrenheit (18 degrees Celsius) for reliable germination.

The plants are not particularly fussy about soil type and will grow in sandy, loamy, or even clay soils as long as drainage is adequate.

They perform best in moderately fertile soil with a pH between 6.0 and 7.5.

Seeds are very small and should be sown only about 1/4 inch deep, then kept consistently moist until germination.

Germination typically occurs within 7-14 days when conditions are warm enough.

## Growing and Maintenance

Once established, amaranth requires surprisingly little care compared to other garden crops.

The deep taproot system makes the plant remarkably drought-tolerant, though consistent watering improves leaf quality and grain yield.

Mulching around the base of plants helps conserve soil moisture and suppress weeds during early growth.

As the plants grow tall, some grain varieties can reach 6-8 feet and may need staking in windy locations.

For leaf-harvest varieties, you can begin cutting outer leaves about 30-40 days after planting.

Continuous harvesting of leaves actually encourages bushier growth and extends the productive life of the plant.

Pests are generally not a major problem with amaranth, though aphids and flea beetles may occasionally feed on young leaves.

[[cervantes-neem-oil-organic-pest-control]] control methods like insecticidal soap or neem oil are effective if pest pressure becomes significant.

## Harvesting Grain vs Greens

The harvesting method depends entirely on whether you are growing for leaves, grain, or both.

For leaf harvest, pick individual leaves from the lower portion of the plant once they reach 4-6 inches long.

Avoid removing more than one-third of the leaves at once to avoid stressing the plant.

Leaves become tougher and more bitter as the plant matures and begins flowering, so focus on young growth.

For grain harvest, wait until the flower heads have turned from bright green to golden or deep red-brown.

A simple test is to gently shake the seed head over your palm; if mature seeds fall easily, it is ready to harvest.

Cut the entire seed head and hang it upside down in a dry, well-ventilated area to finish drying.

Once fully dry, thresh the seeds by rubbing the seed heads between your hands over a bucket or tarp.

Winnow the chaff by pouring the seeds back and forth between two containers in a light breeze.

Properly dried amaranth grain can be stored in airtight containers for up to a year or more.

## Saving Seeds for Next Season

Amaranth is an open-pollinated crop, which means saved seeds will breed true to the parent variety.

Store saved seeds in a cool, dry place in paper envelopes or glass jars with desiccant packets.

Cross-pollination can occur between different amaranth species grown nearby, so isolate varieties if purity matters to you.
