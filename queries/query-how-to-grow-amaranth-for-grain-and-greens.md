## How to Grow Amaranth for Grain and Greens

## Short Answer

Amaranth is a warm-season annual that produces both nutritious edible leaves and protein-rich grain heads.

It thrives in full sun with well-drained soil and tolerates heat and drought far better than most garden crops.

The plant is related to [[savory-growing-season-and-non-growing-season-management]] and collect grain from mature seed heads in fall.

Amaranth is one of the easiest grain crops to grow on a home scale, requiring minimal inputs once established.

## What Makes Amaranth Special

Amaranth has been cultivated for over 8,000 years and was a staple food of the Aztecs and other Mesoamerican civilizations.

The genus includes roughly 70 species, but only a handful are widely grown for food today.

Unlike true cereal grains like wheat and corn, amaranth is a pseudocereal, meaning it produces a seed that is used like a grain but comes from a broadleaf plant.

Its seeds contain roughly 13-14% protein with a complete amino acid profile, including lysine, which is typically low in other grains.

The leaves are also highly nutritious, packing more iron, calcium, and vitamin C than spinach when eaten raw or lightly cooked.

Amaranth is naturally drought-tolerant once established, making it an excellent choice for dryland gardens and water-conscious growers.

## Harvesting Grain vs Greens

The harvesting method depends entirely on whether you are growing for leaves, grain, or both.

For leaf harvest, pick individual leaves from the lower portion of the plant once they reach 4-6 inches long.

Avoid removing more than one-third of the leaves at once to avoid stressing the plant.

Leaves become tougher and more bitter as the plant matures and begins flowering, so focus on young growth.

For grain harvest, wait until the flower heads have turned from bright green to golden or deep red-brown.

A simple test is to gently shake the seed head over your palm; if mature seeds fall easily, it is ready to harvest.

Cut the entire seed head and hang it upside down in a dry, well-ventilated area to finish drying.

Once fully dry, thresh the seeds by rubbing the seed heads between your hands over a bucket or tarp.

Winnow the chaff by pouring the seeds back and forth between two containers in a light breeze.

Properly dried amaranth grain can be stored in airtight containers for up to a year or more.

## Saving Seeds for Next Season

Amaranth is an open-pollinated crop, which means saved seeds will breed true to the parent variety.

Store saved seeds in a cool, dry place in paper envelopes or glass jars with desiccant packets.

Cross-pollination can occur between different amaranth species grown nearby, so isolate varieties if purity matters to you.
