## What Is Mycofiltration and How Do Mushrooms Filter Water?

## Short Answer

Mycofiltration is a bioremediation technique that uses living fungal mycelium to filter contaminants from water.
The mycelial mat acts as a biological membrane that traps particles, absorbs heavy metals, and breaks down organic pollutants through enzymatic action.
Pioneered by mycologist Paul Stamets, this approach harnesses the natural filtering capacity of fungi to clean agricultural runoff, greywater, and even industrial waste streams.
Unlike mechanical filtration systems, mycofiltration operates passively and continues improving as the fungal network matures and expands.
The concept builds on the broader field of [[mycofiltration-overview|mycofiltration research]] that has expanded significantly since the early 2000s.

## How Does Mycofiltration Work?

The process relies on the extraordinary surface area and enzymatic power of [[mycelium]].
As water passes through a substrate colonized by fungi, the mycelial network physically traps suspended particles like a microscopic sieve.
Simultaneously, the fungi secrete extracellular enzymes that decompose complex organic molecules including pesticides, petroleum hydrocarbons, and bacterial pathogens.
The dense web of hyphal strands also provides habitat for beneficial bacteria that contribute additional breakdown capacity.
This combination of physical, chemical, and biological filtration makes mycofiltration remarkably effective for a low-tech system.

## Which Mushroom Species Work Best for Mycofiltration?

Not all fungi are equally suited to water filtration applications.
[[oyster-mushroom|Oyster mushrooms]] (Pleurotus species) are among the most widely used because they grow rapidly and produce powerful lignin-degrading enzymes.
[[king-stropharia|King Stropharia]] (Stropharia rugoso-annulata) is another favorite, especially for outdoor installations treating farm runoff and greywater.
This species forms massive mycelial mats in wood chip beds and tolerates a wide range of environmental conditions.
Turkey tail (Trametes versicolor) and other polypore fungi excel at breaking down persistent organic pollutants due to their robust enzyme systems.
The choice of species depends on climate, substrate availability, and the specific contaminants present in the water.

## Building a Mycofiltration System

### Substrate Selection and Preparation

The substrate serves as both food for the fungi and the physical medium through which water flows.
Wood chips, straw, and spent mushroom substrate are the most common choices for mycofiltration installations.
[[mycofiltration-membrane-construction|Membrane construction]] typically involves layering inoculated substrate between geotextile fabrics to contain the mycelium.
The substrate must be properly pasteurized or sterilized before inoculation to prevent contamination by competing organisms.
[[mushroom-cultivation-fundamentals|Mushroom cultivation fundamentals]] apply here — clean technique and proper spawn rates ensure rapid colonization.

### Installation Designs

Mycofiltration systems range from simple burlap sack installations to engineered watershed-scale treatment systems.
[[tabletop-mycofiltration-unit-construction|Tabletop units]] are a great starting point for small-scale experiments and educational demonstrations.
At the farm scale, [[mycofiltration-of-agricultural-runoff|agricultural runoff]] can be treated by installing mycelial buffer strips along drainage channels.
[[mycofiltration-silt-barrier-design|Silt barrier designs]] combine erosion control with biological filtration by placing myceliated straw bales in drainage swales.
[[mycofiltration-farm-livestock-buffers|Livestock buffer systems]] use fenced mycelial zones to intercept nutrient-rich runoff before it reaches waterways.

## What Contaminants Can Mycofiltration Remove?

Mycofiltration is effective against a surprisingly broad spectrum of water pollutants.
[[mycofiltration-of-pathogens|Pathogen removal]] occurs through direct predation by fungi on bacteria like E. coli and physical trapping of microbial cells.
Heavy metals such as lead, cadmium, and mercury are adsorbed onto the mycelial cell walls through a process called biosorption.
[[mycofiltration-of-chemical-runoff|Chemical runoff]] including pesticides, herbicides, and petroleum products can be enzymatically degraded by white-rot fungi.
[[mycofiltration-of-water-contaminants|Water contaminants]] of all types — biological, chemical, and physical — are addressed through multiple complementary mechanisms.
Nutrient pollution from nitrogen and phosphorus is reduced as the fungi assimilate these elements for growth.

## Advantages and Limitations

Mycofiltration offers several compelling advantages over conventional water treatment approaches.
The systems are inexpensive to build, using locally available materials like wood chips and agricultural waste.
They require no electricity or chemical inputs once established, making them ideal for off-grid and rural applications.
However, mycofiltration has limitations that must be acknowledged for realistic planning.
Flow rates are slower than mechanical systems, and very high contaminant loads can overwhelm the fungal network.
Cold climates slow fungal metabolism significantly, reducing treatment efficiency during winter months.
Regular maintenance includes replenishing the substrate as it decomposes and monitoring for contamination.

## What About Mycofiltration at the Watershed Scale?

[[mycofiltration-watershed-protection|Watershed-scale applications]] represent the most ambitious use of fungal water filtration.
These installations involve strategically placing myceliated substrate at key points throughout a drainage basin.
[[mycofiltration-water-treatment|Water treatment]] at this scale can address non-point source pollution that is difficult to control with conventional methods.
Paul Stamets demonstrated this concept in his [[stamets-mushroom-mycofiltration-mycelium-as-filtration-membrane|seminal work on mycelium as filtration membranes]].
The approach integrates naturally with other ecological restoration strategies like riparian buffer plantings and constructed wetlands.
[[constructed-wetlands-and-ecological-wastewater-treatment|Constructed wetlands]] and mycofiltration systems can be combined for synergistic water purification.
Costs remain low relative to engineered solutions, but successful implementation requires careful site assessment and ongoing monitoring.

## What About Combining Mycofiltration with Other Water Treatment Methods?

Mycofiltration works best as part of an integrated water treatment strategy rather than a standalone solution.
Combining fungal filters with [[constructed-wetlands-and-ecological-wastewater-treatment|constructed wetlands]] creates a multi-stage purification train.
The wetland plants handle nutrient uptake while the mycelial layers target pathogens and organic pollutants.
[[fungal-bioremediation-mechanisms|Fungal bioremediation mechanisms]] complement bacterial processes by breaking down compounds that bacteria alone cannot degrade.
Upstream sediment traps and swales pre-filter large debris before water reaches the sensitive mycelial membrane.
This layered approach extends the life of the mycofiltration system and improves overall treatment reliability.

## What About Maintenance and System Longevity?

A mycofiltration system is a living infrastructure that requires ongoing attention to remain effective.
The substrate gradually decomposes as the fungi consume it, losing both volume and filtering capacity over time.
Most wood-chip-based systems need substrate replenishment every one to three years depending on flow volume and climate.
Monitoring is straightforward — visual inspection of mycelial coverage and periodic water quality testing at inlet and outlet points.
If the mycelium becomes patchy or contaminated with mold, removing the affected section and adding fresh inoculated substrate restores function.
Winter preparation in cold climates involves insulating the system with thick mulch or a protective cover to prevent freeze damage.
The fungi themselves produce antifungal and antibacterial compounds that help maintain system health against invaders.

## Key Points

- Mycofiltration uses living [[mycelium]] as a biological filter to remove contaminants from water.
- The technique combines physical trapping, enzymatic degradation, and biosorption in one system.
- [[oyster-mushroom|Oyster mushrooms]] and [[king-stropharia|King Stropharia]] are the most commonly used species.
- Systems range from tabletop experiments to [[mycofiltration-watershed-protection|watershed-scale installations]].
- Effective against pathogens, heavy metals, petroleum hydrocarbons, pesticides, and nutrient pollution.
- [[bioremediation-using-fungi|Fungal bioremediation]] encompasses mycofiltration plus soil-based remediation techniques.
- Low cost and passive operation make mycofiltration suitable for rural and off-grid applications.
- Cold temperatures and high contaminant loads can limit performance and require supplemental approaches.
- Integration with [[spent-mushroom-substrate|spent mushroom substrate]] recycling creates circular waste-to-resource systems.
