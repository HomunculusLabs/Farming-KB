# How to Grow and Use Lemongrass for Natural Pest Control

Lemongrass (Cymbopogon citratus) is a tropical aromatic grass widely valued for its pest-repellent properties. This FAQ covers the science behind its effectiveness, how to grow it strategically, and practical methods for using it as part of a natural pest management approach.

---

## What pests does lemongrass repel and what is the science behind it?

Lemongrass repels or deters a range of common garden and household pests, including **mosquitoes, ticks, fleas, ants, flies, and aphids**. The effectiveness comes from its essential oil, which is rich in several bioactive compounds:

- **Citral** (a mixture of geranial and neral): The dominant compound in lemongrass oil (typically 65-85% of total composition), citral has demonstrated significant repellent and insecticidal activity against mosquitoes and other flying insects in multiple laboratory and field studies. It disrupts the olfactory receptors that insects use to locate hosts.

- **Citronellal**: Present in smaller amounts (typically 1-5%), this compound is a well-documented mosquito repellent and is the primary active ingredient in commercial citronella products. It masks the carbon dioxide and lactic acid signals that attract biting insects.

- **Geraniol**: A monoterpene alcohol that contributes to the characteristic citrus aroma, geraniol is EPA-registered as a biopesticide and has shown repellent efficacy against ticks, fleas, and flies in peer-reviewed research.

The combined effect of these compounds creates a volatile barrier that interferes with insect host-seeking behavior. However, repellency is dose-dependent and temporary -- the aroma must be present in sufficient concentration to maintain effectiveness, which means lemongrass works best in confined or well-planted areas rather than as a standalone solution for large open spaces.

## How should I grow lemongrass specifically for pest control purposes?

For maximum pest-repellent effect, consider these strategic growing practices:

**Placement and density.** Plant lemongrass in clumps of 3-5 stalks spaced 12-18 inches apart along pathways, near seating areas, around patios, and along the perimeter of vegetable beds. The scent plume from a dense clump extends roughly 3-6 feet downwind. Wind direction matters -- position plantings upwind of areas you want to protect.

**Companion planting combinations.** Lemongrass pairs effectively with other aromatic herbs to create a multi-layered repellent barrier. Good combinations include:
- **Lemongrass + marigolds + basil**: Effective against a broad spectrum of garden pests while attracting pollinators.
- **Lemongrass + rosemary + mint**: Strong flying insect deterrent for outdoor living spaces.
- **Lemongrass + garlic + chives**: Useful border planting around vegetable beds to deter aphids and leafhoppers.

**Growing conditions.** Lemongrass is a perennial in USDA zones 9-11 and an annual elsewhere. It requires full sun (6-8 hours minimum), well-draining soil, and consistent moisture. In colder climates, grow in containers (minimum 12-inch pots) and overwinter indoors near a sunny window. Plants grown in containers can be moved to strategic positions as pest pressure shifts through the season.

**Harvest for potency.** The essential oil concentration peaks just before flowering and is highest in the lower leaf sheaths. Regular harvesting of outer stalks actually stimulates new growth and maintains oil production at higher levels in remaining foliage.

## How do I make lemongrass pest-repellent sprays and extracts at home?

Several homemade preparations can capture lemongrass's repellent properties:

**Lemongrass infusion spray.** Chop 2-3 fresh stalks (roughly 1 cup of chopped material) and steep in 2 cups of just-boiled water for 30-60 minutes. Strain, cool, and add 1 teaspoon of mild liquid soap (as a surfactant to help the solution adhere to plant surfaces). Transfer to a spray bottle and apply to plant foliage, doorways, and window frames. Reapply every 2-3 days or after rain.

**Lemongrass oil infusion.** Pack a glass jar with chopped fresh lemongrass leaves, cover with a carrier oil (coconut, olive, or jojoba oil), and let steep in a warm sunny spot for 2-4 weeks, shaking daily. Strain and use the infused oil as a skin repellent (dilute 1:4 with additional carrier oil before direct application) or add a few tablespoons to a spray bottle with water and soap.

**Dried lemongrass sachets.** Dry harvested stalks by hanging them in a warm, well-ventilated area out of direct sun. Once fully dry, crush the leaves and place in small muslin bags. Position these in closets, pantries, and near entry points to deter ants and flies. Replace the contents every 4-6 weeks as the volatile oils dissipate.

All homemade preparations are most effective when used fresh. Label and date your products, and discard any that develop an off-smell or cloudiness.

## How does lemongrass essential oil compare to the fresh plant for pest control?

Essential oil is far more concentrated than fresh plant material. One ounce of lemongrass essential oil represents roughly 5-10 pounds of fresh plant material. This concentration advantage translates to several practical differences:

- **Potency and duration.** Essential oil formulations maintain effective repellent concentrations for 2-4 hours on skin and 1-2 days on surfaces. Fresh plant material in the garden provides a lower but continuous release of volatile compounds for as long as the plant is healthy.

- **Versatility.** Essential oil can be formulated into sprays, lotions, candles, and diffuser blends. Fresh lemongrass is limited to growing in place, steeping into infusions, or processing into dried products.

- **Cost and sustainability.** For home gardeners with established lemongrass plants, using fresh or dried material is essentially free. Purchased essential oil is effective but represents an ongoing expense, typically $8-15 per ounce for therapeutic grade.

- **Consistency.** Commercial essential oil has a standardized citral content (typically 75-85%). Home-grown plant potency varies with growing conditions, harvest timing, and variety.

For a home garden IPM program, the best approach is to grow the plants for ambient protection and keep a bottle of essential oil on hand for targeted applications when pest pressure spikes.

## What pests does lemongrass NOT repel and what are its limitations?

Understanding lemongrass's limitations prevents overreliance and unrealistic expectations:

- **Does NOT repel**: Japanese beetles, squash bugs, tomato hornworms, cabbage loopers, slugs, snails, spider mites, or most caterpillar species. These pests are not deterred by the aromatic compounds in lemongrass and require different management approaches.

- **Limited effectiveness outdoors**: In open, windy areas, the volatile compounds disperse too quickly to create a meaningful repellent zone. Lemongrass works best in sheltered gardens, patios with windbreaks, and enclosed or semi-enclosed spaces.

- **Not a knockdown agent**: Lemongrass repels rather than kills. It does not provide residual control once the scent dissipates. For active infestations, other interventions are needed.

- **Duration limitations**: Even the best lemongrass-based preparations require frequent reapplication. Burned citronella candles, for example, lose effectiveness beyond a 3-6 foot radius and only while actively burning.

- **Seasonal constraints**: In temperate climates, lemongrass dies back with frost, leaving gaps in protection during the very months when indoor pest pressure often increases.

Lemongrass is a tool in the pest management toolkit, not a silver bullet. Pair it with other methods for reliable results.

## Is lemongrass safe around pets and beneficial insects?

**Pets.** Lemongrass is generally safe for dogs and cats in its whole-plant form in the garden. However, concentrated essential oil poses risks:
- Cats lack the liver enzyme (glucuronyl transferase) needed to efficiently metabolize many essential oils, including lemongrass. Ingestion of concentrated oil can cause drooling, vomiting, lethargy, and in severe cases, liver damage.
- Dogs are less sensitive but can still experience gastrointestinal upset from ingesting essential oil. The ASPCA lists Cymbopogon citratus as non-toxic to dogs and horses but notes that essential oil concentrations are a separate concern.

For households with cats, use the whole plant in the garden rather than diffusing essential oil indoors, and store all oil-based preparations securely out of reach. If using sprays on surfaces accessible to pets, use the water-based infusion rather than oil-based products.

**Beneficial insects.** Lemongrass does not appear to significantly repel most pollinators. Bees, butterflies, and hoverflies are attracted to nectar sources regardless of nearby aromatic plants. However, broad-spectrum spraying of any repellent -- including lemongrass-based ones -- can coat pollinator bodies and interfere with foraging. Apply sprays in the early morning or late evening when pollinators are less active, and avoid spraying directly on flowering plants.

Ladybugs, lacewings, and parasitic wasps are generally unaffected by lemongrass aroma, making it compatible with biological control programs.

## How do I integrate lemongrass into a broader Integrated Pest Management (IPM) strategy?

IPM combines multiple pest management methods for sustainable, long-term control. Here is how lemongrass fits into that framework:

**Cultural controls (Tier 1 -- Foundation).** Lemongrass serves as a cultural control when used as [[companion-planting|companion planting]] and garden design. It contributes to pest prevention by creating aromatic barriers that reduce initial pest colonization. This is its strongest role in IPM -- preventing problems rather than solving them.

**Mechanical and physical controls (Tier 2).** Combine lemongrass plantings with row covers for vulnerable crops, sticky traps for monitoring and catching flying insects, and physical barriers like copper tape or diatomaceous earth for slugs and crawling pests that lemongrass does not affect.

**Biological controls (Tier 3).** Maintain habitat for beneficial insects (insectary plants, water sources, bare ground for ground-nesting bees) alongside your lemongrass. The two approaches are complementary since lemongrass does not deter most predatory insects.

**Biorational and least-toxic chemical controls (Tier 4).** Use lemongrass-based sprays and essential oil applications as a first-line biorational treatment before reaching for stronger products. Neem oil, insecticidal soap, and Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt) address the pests that lemongrass cannot.

**Conventional controls (Tier 5).** As a last resort, targeted use of synthetic pesticides for severe infestations. Lemongrass provides no protection here, but having maintained other IPM tiers means conventional interventions should be rare and limited in scope.

**Monitoring and record-keeping.** Track pest observations weekly in a garden journal. Note which areas have lemongrass plantings and whether pest pressure differs from unprotected zones. This data helps you refine placement density and companion combinations over successive growing seasons.
