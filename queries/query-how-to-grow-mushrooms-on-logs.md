# How to Grow Mushrooms on Logs?

## Short Answer
Growing mushrooms on logs involves inoculating freshly cut hardwood logs with mushroom
spawn (either plug spawn or sawdust spawn), sealing the inoculation sites with wax, and
waiting 6 to 18 months for the mycelium to fully colonize the wood before fruiting
begins. Shiitake and oyster mushrooms are the most popular species for log cultivation,
and a single inoculated log can produce mushrooms for 3 to 7 years with proper management.

## Detailed Explanation

Log cultivation is one of the oldest and most reliable methods for growing gourmet and
medicinal mushrooms. It mimics the natural lifecycle of wood-decomposing fungi and
produces high-quality mushrooms with minimal equipment and ongoing cost. The
[[mushroom-log-cultivation]] page provides a comprehensive overview of the entire
process, and the [[log-cultivation-methods]] page compares different approaches including
traditional log inoculation, wedge techniques, and totem methods.

Selecting the right wood species is critical for success. Most cultivated mushrooms
prefer hardwood logs, with oak, maple, beech, birch, and alder being the most commonly
recommended species. Softwoods like pine and cedar contain resins and compounds that
inhibit fungal growth and are generally unsuitable. The [[mushroom-log-stump-cultivation-tree-species-guide]] page provides a detailed reference matching mushroom
species to preferred log types, noting that shiitake thrives on oak while oyster
mushrooms are more flexible and will grow on a wider range of hardwoods including
poplar and aspen.

Timing your log harvest matters significantly. Logs should be cut during the dormant
season (late fall through early spring) when the trees' sugar content is highest and
moisture content is ideal for fungal colonization. Logs should be inoculated within
2 to 6 weeks of cutting before the natural fungal competitors have a chance to
establish. The [[shiitake-cultivation-guide]] page covers the specific timing
considerations for shiitake, which is the most popular species for log cultivation.
The [[cotter-log-stump-cultivation]] page from Tradd Cotter's work offers additional
perspectives on log selection and preparation.

Inoculation involves drilling holes into the log and inserting spawn. Plug spawn
(small wooden dowels colonized with mycelium) is the easiest method for beginners
because it can be tapped into the holes with a hammer. Sawdust spawn is more
economical and colonizes faster but requires a specialized inoculation tool to pack
the sawdust into the drilled holes. After inoculation, all holes and any fresh cut
surfaces should be sealed with food-grade wax (cheese wax or beeswax) to prevent
the spawn from drying out and to protect against contamination. The
[[stamets-outdoor-natural-culture-methods]] page provides Paul Stamets' detailed
protocol for outdoor log cultivation.

After inoculation, logs enter a colonization phase (also called the spawn run or
incubation period) that lasts anywhere from 6 months to 2 years depending on the
mushroom species, log size, and environmental conditions. During this phase, logs
should be kept in a shaded, moist location where they receive indirect sunlight and
rainfall or regular watering. Stacking logs in a crib or lean-to arrangement promotes
air circulation while maintaining humidity. The [[shiitake-cultivation-log-method]]
page describes optimal stacking arrangements and environmental conditions for the
spawn run period.

Fruiting is triggered by environmental cues that mimic natural conditions. For
shiitake, the most reliable trigger is soaking the colonized log in cold water for
24 hours, which simulates a heavy rainfall and shocks the mycelium into producing
mushrooms. The [[shiitake-log-vs-sawdust-cultivation]] page compares the log method
to indoor sawdust cultivation, noting that log-grown shiitake generally have better
flavor, longer shelf life, and higher market value despite lower overall yields.
The [[oyster-mushroom-short-log-cultivation]] page covers the slightly different
fruiting requirements for oyster mushrooms, which respond more to humidity changes
and temperature drops than to soaking.

## Key Points

- Use freshly cut hardwood logs (oak, maple, beech) harvested during dormant season
  [[mushroom-log-stump-cultivation-tree-species-guide]]
- Inoculate within 2 to 6 weeks of cutting using plug spawn for beginners or sawdust
  spawn for efficiency [[log-cultivation-methods]]
- Seal all inoculation sites with food-grade wax to prevent drying and contamination
  [[stamets-outdoor-natural-culture-methods]]
- Colonization takes 6 to 18 months in a shaded, moist location with good air
  circulation [[shiitake-cultivation-log-method]]
- Force fruiting by soaking logs in cold water for 24 hours to simulate rainfall
  [[shiitake-cultivation-guide]]
- A single inoculated log can produce mushrooms for 3 to 7 years with proper care
  [[shiitake-log-vs-sawdust-cultivation]]

Beyond shiitake and oyster mushrooms, several other species perform well on logs.
Lion's mane (Hericium erinaceus) fruits on oak and beech logs and produces distinctive
cascading white spines that are prized both for culinary use and their neuroprotective
compounds. Wine cap (Stropharia rugoso-annulata) grows well on hardwood logs partially
buried in wood chip beds. The [[wine-cap-mushroom-cultivation]] page covers this
adaptable species, and the [[mushroom-log-stump-cultivation-tree-species-guide]] page
provides species-specific recommendations for matching mushrooms to log types.
