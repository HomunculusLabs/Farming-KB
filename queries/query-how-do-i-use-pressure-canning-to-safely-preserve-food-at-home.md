# How Do I Use Pressure Canning to Safely Preserve Food at Home?

## Short Answer

Pressure canning is the only safe home method for preserving low-acid foods like vegetables, meats, and soups.
Unlike [[water-bath-canning-guide|water bath canning]], which only reaches 212°F, a pressure canner heats food to 240–250°F under steam pressure.
This temperature is required to destroy _Clostridium botulinum_ spores that cause botulism, a potentially fatal form of [[pressure-canning-safety|food poisoning]].
With proper equipment, tested recipes, and careful attention to processing times, pressure canning lets you safely store shelf-stable meals for a year or more.

## What Foods Require Pressure Canning?

Low-acid foods with a pH above 4.6 must be pressure canned because boiling water alone cannot kill botulinum spores.
This category includes all vegetables (except tomatoes when acidified), meats, poultry, seafood, soups, and mixed recipes containing low-acid ingredients.
Tomatoes sit near the pH borderline and can be water-bath canned only if you add acid in the form of lemon juice or citric acid.
Fruits, pickles, jams, and jellies are high-acid and can safely use the [[water-bath-canning-guide|water bath method]] instead.
When a recipe contains both high-acid and low-acid components, the entire jar must be treated as low-acid and pressure canned.
The [[canning-and-preserving|canning and preserving]] overview covers the full spectrum of methods and which foods belong to each category.

## How Does a Pressure Canner Work?

A pressure canner is a heavy pot with a locking lid, a pressure gauge, and a vent pipe that regulates internal steam pressure.
When water boils inside the sealed vessel, steam builds pressure that raises the boiling point above 212°F.
At 10 pounds per square inch of pressure, the internal temperature reaches approximately 240°F at sea level.
At 15 psi, it reaches roughly 250°F, which provides an even greater safety margin for dense or large-volume jars.
Most modern pressure canners use either a dial gauge that shows exact pressure or a weighted gauge that rocks gently at the target pressure.
The [[pressure-canning-food-preservation|pressure canning food preservation]] guide explains gauge types and calibration in detail.
It is critical to distinguish a pressure canner from a pressure cooker — not all pressure cookers are rated for safe canning.

## What Equipment Do You Need?

A dedicated pressure canner is the single most important piece of equipment.
Recommended models include the Presto 16-quart (dial gauge), the All American 921 (weighted gauge, no gasket), and the Mirro 22-quart.
You also need Mason-type jars with two-piece lids (flat lid plus screw band), a jar lifter, a canning funnel, and a headspace measuring tool.
Clean kitchen towels, a timer, and a reliable pressure gauge are essential for safe operation.
The [[mason-jar-preserving-guide|Mason jar preserving guide]] covers jar selection, lid types, and reuse guidelines.
Always inspect jars for nicks or cracks before each use, as damaged jars can break under pressure.
Replacement gauges and gaskets should be checked annually — many county extension offices offer free gauge testing.

## Step-by-Step Pressure Canning Process

First, prepare your recipe using a scientifically tested source such as the USDA Complete Guide to Home Canning or a university extension publication.
Fill hot, clean jars with food, leaving the headspace specified in the recipe (typically 1 inch for low-acid foods).
Remove air bubbles by running a nonmetallic spatula around the inside of each jar, then wipe the rim clean.
Place the lid and screw band on each jar, finger-tight only — overtightening prevents proper venting.
Add 2 to 3 inches of hot water to the canner, place jars on the rack, and lock the lid.
Vent steam from the canner for 10 minutes before closing the petcock or placing the weight on the vent pipe.
Once the gauge reaches the target pressure, start the timer and maintain steady pressure for the full processing time.
When the time is complete, turn off the heat and let the canner depressurize naturally — never force-cool it.
After pressure reaches zero, wait 10 minutes, then carefully open the lid away from your face.
Remove jars with a lifter, place them on a towel, and let them cool undisturbed for 12 to 24 hours.

## What About Altitude Adjustments?

Altitude affects pressure canning because water boils at lower temperatures as elevation increases.
At higher elevations, you must increase the canner pressure to achieve the same internal temperature as at sea level.
For dial gauge canners, increase pressure by 1 psi for each 2,000 feet above sea level.
For weighted gauge canners, switch to the 15 psi weight if you are above 1,000 feet elevation.
Processing times remain the same regardless of altitude — only the pressure setting changes.
The [[comparison-pressure-canning-vs-water-bath-canning|pressure canning vs. water bath canning comparison]] provides a full altitude adjustment table.
Failing to adjust for altitude is one of the most common and dangerous mistakes in home canning.

## What About Food Safety and Botulism?

Botulism is caused by _Clostridium botulinum_ spores that thrive in moist, low-acid, anaerobic environments — exactly the conditions inside a sealed jar.
Pressure canning eliminates this risk by heating food to temperatures that destroy the spores themselves.
Never taste food from a jar with a bulging lid, a broken seal, or an off odor, as these may indicate botulism.
The [[pressure-canning-safety|pressure canning safety]] page covers warning signs, disposal procedures, and emergency response.
Following tested recipes exactly — with no modifications to ingredients, jar size, or processing time — is the foundation of safe canning.
Modern canning guidelines have evolved significantly; recipes from before 1990 may not meet current safety standards.

## Key Points

- Pressure canning is the **only** safe method for preserving low-acid foods (vegetables, meats, soups) at home.
- It heats food to 240–250°F under steam pressure, destroying _C. botulinum_ spores that boiling water cannot kill.
- Always use a **tested recipe** from the USDA, a university extension, or a verified canning authority — never improvise processing times.
- Adjust pressure for altitude: add 1 psi per 2,000 feet above sea level for dial gauge canners.
- Inspect equipment annually — gauges drift, gaskets crack, and jars develop nicks that compromise safety.
- Never force-cool a pressure canner; natural depressurization is critical for jar seal integrity and safety.

## What About Choosing the Right Pressure Canner?

Two main types of pressure canners are available for home use: dial gauge and weighted gauge models.
Dial gauge canners (such as the Presto series) display the exact pressure on a dial and require careful monitoring throughout the processing time.
Weighted gauge canners (such as the All American series) rock or jiggle at the target pressure and are easier for beginners to use consistently.
Dial gauges must be tested for accuracy every year — many county extension offices provide this service free of charge.
Weighted gauges never need calibration because they operate on a simple physics principle of weight and steam pressure.
The [[comparison-freezing-vs-canning|freezing vs. canning comparison]] helps you decide whether pressure canning or freezing better suits your storage goals and available space.
For most homesteaders, a 16- to 23-quart canner offers the best balance of capacity, weight, and stove compatibility.

## What About Common Mistakes to Avoid?

The most dangerous mistake is using a pressure cooker that is not rated for canning — these units are smaller and lack the pressure regulation needed for safe processing.
Another common error is failing to vent the canner for the full 10 minutes before building pressure, which traps air pockets that lower the internal temperature.
Overtightening jar lids prevents proper air escape during processing, leading to seal failure or jar breakage.
Modifying tested recipes by adding extra ingredients, changing jar sizes, or shortening processing times can create unsafe conditions.
Using jars from commercial products (such as pasta sauce jars) is unsafe because they are not designed to withstand repeated pressure cycles.
