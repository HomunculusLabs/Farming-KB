# What Is the Difference Between Topping and Fimming Cannabis?

## Short Answer
Topping and FIMing are both high-stress training techniques that cut the apical
dominance of a cannabis plant to encourage lateral branching and a wider canopy.
Topping cleanly removes the entire top growth tip between nodes, creating two
symmetrical colas. FIMing (an acronym for "Fuck, I Missed") involves pinching or
cutting roughly 75% of the top growth tip, which typically produces three to four
new colas from the remaining tissue and is less stressful to the plant than a full
topping cut.

## Detailed Explanation

The core principle behind both [[cannabis-topping-training]] and FIMing is the
disruption of apical dominance, the hormonal mechanism by which the plant's central
stem tip suppresses lateral branch growth below it. By removing or damaging the
apical meristem, the plant redistributes growth hormones (primarily auxins) to the
lower nodes, stimulating dormant branch sites into active growth. This transforms a
tall, single-cola plant into a shorter, bushier specimen with multiple main colas
that are more evenly exposed to light.

Topping is the more deliberate and predictable of the two techniques. To top a
cannabis plant, you locate a node pair on the main stem and make a clean cut
through the stem directly above the node junction, removing the entire upper growth
tip. The two axillary buds at that node site then develop into two new main stems
that grow outward and upward. This technique is best performed during the
[[cannabis-vegetative-growth-stage]] when the plant has developed at least three to
five node pairs, giving it enough structure to recover quickly. Topping can be
repeated multiple times as the plant grows, with each topping event doubling the
number of main colas (one to two to four to eight, and so on).

FIMing is essentially an imprecise or partial topping that was discovered
accidentally, hence the colorful name. Instead of making a clean cut through the
entire stem above a node, FIMing involves cutting roughly 75% of the newest growth
tip, leaving a small portion of the apical meristem intact. Because some apical
tissue remains, the plant's response is less predictable. Rather than producing
exactly two new colas, FIMing often results in three to four (and sometimes more)
new growth tips emerging from the damaged site. The results vary depending on
exactly where and how the cut is made, the genetics of the plant, and environmental
conditions during recovery.

Comparing the two techniques directly, topping is generally considered more
reliable and consistent in its results. You know you will get two new colas from
each topped site, making it easier to plan your canopy architecture. FIMing is less
predictable but potentially more productive per cut, since it can create three or
more new branches from a single intervention. Recovery time is typically shorter
for FIMing since less tissue is removed and the plant retains some of its apical
growth momentum. However, the asymmetrical results can make canopy management and
light distribution more challenging compared to the even symmetry produced by
topping.

Both techniques fall under the broader umbrella of
[[cannabis-training-techniques-comparison]], alongside low-stress training (LST),
super cropping, screen of green (ScrOG), and main-lining. Each technique has its
own risk-reward profile and is suited to different grow styles and experience
levels. High-stress techniques like topping and FIMing cause a temporary growth
pause of several days while the plant recovers, so they should be completed early
enough in the vegetative stage that the plant has time to fill out before flowering
begins. For growers interested in maximizing yield through training,
[[query-which-cannabis-training-techniques-produce-highest-yields]] provides
comparative data across methods.

The relationship between these techniques and
[[cannabis-pruning-and-defoliation-guide]] practices is also worth understanding.
While topping and FIMing alter the plant's structural architecture during
vegetative growth, pruning and defoliation are typically performed during the
transition into flowering or during early flower to improve light penetration and
airflow through the developing canopy. Combining structural training techniques
like topping with strategic defoliation can produce exceptionally even, productive
canopies when executed correctly.

For growers looking to take their training further,
[[query-what-is-main-lining-cannabis-and-how-does-it-work]] is a technique that
builds on topping by creating a deliberate manifold structure where each branch is
tied down to create an even, symmetrical canopy with perfectly distributed colas.
Main-lining typically begins with a topping cut and then uses LST to position the
resulting branches horizontally before allowing them to grow upward. Another
complementary technique,
[[query-what-is-super-cropping-and-when-to-do-it]], involves intentionally crushing
or bending stems to create knuckle-like joints that strengthen the branch
structure and can be combined with topping or FIMing for comprehensive plant
training.

Timing is critical for both techniques. Perform topping or FIMing too early and the
plant may not have enough foliage to photosynthesize efficiently during recovery.
Perform it too late and the plant may not have adequate time to develop new
branches before the flowering phase begins. Most experienced growers recommend
topping at the fourth or fifth node during the third to fourth week of vegetative
growth for photoperiod strains, while autoflowering varieties should be trained
very early (second to third node) due to their fixed vegetative timeline. Always
use clean, sterilized tools to prevent infection at the cut site, and avoid
performing any high-stress training when the plant is already stressed from
nutrient issues, pest problems, or environmental extremes.

## Key Points
- Topping cleanly removes the entire apical tip to produce exactly two new colas
  from the node below
- FIMing removes roughly 75% of the tip, creating three to four new colas but with
  less predictable results
- Both techniques work by disrupting apical dominance to promote lateral branching
  during [[cannabis-vegetative-growth-stage]]
- Topping is more reliable and symmetrical; FIMing is less stressful and
  potentially more productive per cut
- Combine these techniques with LST, ScrOG, or
  [[query-what-is-main-lining-cannabis-and-how-does-it-work]] for maximum canopy
  control
