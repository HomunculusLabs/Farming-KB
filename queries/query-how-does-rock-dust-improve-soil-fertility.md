---# How Does Rock Dust Improve Soil Fertility?

## Short Answer

Rock dust — finely ground volcanic, sedimentary, or igneous rock — slowly releases a broad spectrum of plant-essential minerals through natural weathering processes. It replenishes [[silicon]] (Si)** — Cell wall fortification, [[electron-transport-chain-mitochondrial-respiration]] in respiration
- **Manganese (Mn)** — Enzyme cofactor, nitrogen assimilation
- **Zinc (Zn)** — Auxin production, carbohydrate metabolism
- **Copper (Cu)** — Lignin synthesis, reproductive development
- **Boron (B)** — Cell wall integrity, pollen tube growth, fruit set
- **Cobalt (Co)** — Essential for [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-fungi-biology-symbiosis]] secrete citric, oxalic, and malic acids that chelate and dissolve mineral cations directly from rock surfaces.
- **Microbial metabolism:** Soil bacteria and fungi produce CO₂ (lowering local pH) and organic ligands that accelerate mineral dissolution.
- **Biofilm activity:** Microbial biofilms colonizing rock dust particles concentrate acids at the mineral-water interface.

### Physical Weathering

Freeze-thaw cycles, root expansion, and soil compaction forces fracture rock dust particles over time, increasing surface area and accelerating chemical and biological weathering rates.

## Soil Biology: The Missing Link

Rock dust alone does not feed plants — **soil biology is the intermediary**. Without a healthy soil food web, rock dust sits largely inert for years.

- **Mycorrhizal fungi** extend hyphae into rock dust particles, exuding acids and enzymes that solubilize minerals. They transport these nutrients directly to plant roots in exchange for carbon sugars.
- **Free-living bacteria** (*Pseudomonas*, *Bacillus*) produce siderophores and organic acids that chelate iron, zinc, and manganese from mineral surfaces.
- **Actinomycetes** slowly break down complex silicate structures, contributing to clay formation and humic-mineral complexes.
- **Earthworms and arthropods** physically grind and redistribute rock dust through the soil profile, increasing particle-biology contact.

**Practical takeaway:** Rock dust works best in biologically active soils. Always combine it with compost, compost tea, or mulch to feed the biology that unlocks the minerals.

## Effects on Soil pH and CEC

### pH Effects

| Rock Dust Type | pH Effect | Best For |
|---------------|-----------|----------|
| Basalt dust | Slightly raising (pH 8–9) | Slightly acidic soils |
| Granite dust | Near-neutral to slightly raising | Neutral soils |
| Limestone dust | Strongly raising (calcitic) | Acidic soils needing lime |
| Dolomite dust | Strongly raising (Ca + Mg) | Acidic, Mg-deficient soils |
| Gypsum dust | Neutral to slightly lowering | Alkaline or sodic soils |

**Caution:** Calcareous dusts (limestone, dolomite) on already-alkaline soils (pH >7.5) worsen iron chlorosis and phosphorus lockout. Always test pH before applying.

### Cation Exchange Capacity (CEC)

Rock dust boosts CEC directly (zeolite and volcanic ash have high inherent CEC) and indirectly — weathered particles form secondary clay minerals (allophane, imogolite) over years to decades, providing lasting nutrient-holding capacity.

## Realistic Timelines for Results

- **0–3 months:** Minimal nutrient release. Soil structure may begin improving with very fine dust.
- **3–12 months:** Noticeable microbial activity increases. Some calcium and magnesium become available.
- **1–3 years:** Significant trace mineral release. Improved plant vigor, flavor, and pest resistance. CEC begins rising.
- **3–10 years:** Full mineral spectrum available. Measurable soil test improvements. Clay formation underway.

Rock dust is cumulative. Expect results in growing seasons, not days. It is not a substitute for soluble fertilizers in the short term.

## Application Rates and Methods for Garden Scale

**Rates:** Raised beds (new) — 5–10 lbs per 100 sq ft, mixed into top 4–6" with compost. Raised beds (maintenance) — 2–5 lbs per 100 sq ft top-dressed annually. In-ground gardens — 50–200 lbs per 1,000 sq ft, incorporated with broadfork or tillage. Fruit trees — 5–20 lbs per tree ringed around the drip line. Compost piles — 1–2 cups per cubic foot layered between materials. Container mixes — 1–2 tablespoons per gallon blended at planting.
