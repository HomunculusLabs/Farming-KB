# How Do I Control Tomato Blight?

## Short Answer

Tomato blight is controlled through a combination of resistant varieties,
cultural prevention practices, organic and synthetic fungicides, and
prompt removal of infected plant material. The three most common blight
diseases are early blight (Alternaria solani), late blight (Phytophthora
infestans), and Septoria leaf spot (Septoria lycopersici). Each requires
slightly different management, but overlapping strategies exist.

Prevention is far more effective than treatment once symptoms appear.

## Identifying the Three Blights

The first step is correctly identifying which blight is affecting your
plants, as management differs significantly.

Early blight causes dark brown concentric-ringed lesions on lower
leaves, starting near the soil and moving upward. The spots have a
distinctive "bullseye" pattern. It progresses slowly, yellowing and
drying leaves over weeks.

Late blight is the most aggressive and destructive form. It causes
water-soaked dark lesions that spread rapidly, often accompanied by
white fuzzy growth on leaf undersides during humid conditions. Entire
plants can collapse within days during favorable conditions (cool, wet
weather).

Septoria leaf spot produces small circular spots with dark borders and
tiny black fruiting bodies (pycnidia) visible in the center of each
spot. It typically starts on the lowest leaves and progresses upward.

## Cultural Prevention Methods

Prevention is the most effective strategy against all three blight types.

Proper spacing of 24-36 inches between plants ensures good air
circulation, which reduces leaf wetness duration. Staking or caging
keeps foliage off the ground where pathogens overwinter.

Mulching around the base of plants prevents soil from splashing onto
lower leaves during rain or irrigation. This is particularly important
for early blight and Septoria, whose spores live in the soil.

Avoid overhead watering; use drip irrigation or soaker hoses instead.
If overhead watering is necessary, water early in the morning so leaves
dry quickly.

Remove lower leaves once fruits begin forming on the first cluster.
This improves airflow and removes the oldest leaves most susceptible to
infection.

Rotate crops on a three-year minimum cycle, avoiding planting tomatoes,
potatoes, peppers, or eggplants in the same location. All are members
of the Solanaceae family and share many diseases.

Destroy infected plant material at the end of the season rather than
composting it. Many blight pathogens survive composting temperatures.

## Organic Fungicide Options

Several organic fungicides can help prevent and slow the spread of
blight when applied before infection becomes severe.

Copper-based fungicides (copper hydroxide, copper sulfate) are the
most widely used organic option. They provide broad-spectrum protection
against all three blight types. Apply every 7-10 days, coating both
leaf surfaces. [[cervantes-chemical-fungicides-insecticides]]

Neem oil has antifungal properties and can suppress early blight and
Septoria when applied regularly. It also controls insect pests that may
wound tissue and create entry points for pathogens.
[[cervantes-neem-oil-organic-pest-control]]

Biological fungicides containing Bacillus subtilis (Serenade) or
Trichoderma harzianum work by colonizing leaf surfaces and outcompeting
pathogens. These are most effective as preventives rather than curatives.
[[compost-tea-aact]]

Potassium bicarbonate (similar to baking soda) creates an alkaline
surface hostile to fungal spores. It works best against powdery mildew
but provides some suppression of Septoria and early blight.

## Resistant Varieties

Choosing resistant varieties is the single most impactful long-term
strategy. The [[tomato-growing-guide]] and [[growing-tomatoes-organically]]
resources both emphasize variety selection as the foundation of disease
management.

For late blight resistance, look for varieties with the Ph-2 or Ph-3
genes, such as Mountain Magic, Defiant, and Iron Lady. These are
specifically bred for late blight resistance.

For early blight, tolerance varies widely among varieties. Mountain
Merit, Jasper, and many cherry tomatoes show good natural resistance.

Many modern hybrid varieties combine resistance to multiple diseases.
Check seed catalogs for codes indicating resistance to specific
pathogens (V = Verticillium, F = Fusarium, LB = late blight).

## Emergency Response to Active Infection

When blight is already present, take immediate action to prevent spread.

Remove and destroy all infected leaves immediately. Clean pruners
between cuts with rubbing alcohol to avoid spreading spores.

For late blight specifically, remove entire infected plants if the
disease is detected early. Late blight spreads too rapidly for
containment through leaf removal alone.

Apply copper fungicide to remaining healthy foliage as a protective
barrier. This will not cure existing infections but prevents new ones.

If the outbreak is severe late in the season, consider harvesting all
green tomatoes and ripening them indoors. Green tomatoes ripen
off the vine at room temperature, though flavor will be less developed.

## Companion Planting and Integrated Approaches

Companion planting with basil, marigolds, and borage may provide some
general pest and disease benefits, though scientific evidence for blight
suppression specifically is limited. [[companion-planting]]

An integrated approach combining resistant varieties, cultural
practices, biological controls, and targeted organic fungicides is
far more effective than any single method. [[integrated-pest-management]]
emphasizes this layered strategy, monitoring plants regularly and
intervening at the earliest sign of disease.

## Key Points

- Correct identification of the blight type determines the best
  management approach
- Prevention through spacing, mulching, and drip irrigation is more
  effective than any fungicide
- Copper fungicides provide the best organic protection when applied
  preventatively every 7-10 days
- Late blight requires immediate plant removal; early blight and
  Septoria can be managed through cultural practices
- Resistant varieties are the most cost-effective long-term strategy
- Destroy infected material rather than composting it
- Three-year crop rotation breaks disease cycles in the soil
