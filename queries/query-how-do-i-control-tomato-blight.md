# How Do I Control Tomato Blight?

## Short Answer

Tomato blight is controlled through a combination of resistant varieties,
cultural prevention practices, organic and synthetic fungicides, and
prompt removal of infected plant material. The three most common blight
diseases are early blight (Alternaria solani), late blight (Phytophthora
infestans), and Septoria [[jadam-canker-leaf-spot-pesticide]] (Septoria lycopersici). Each requires
slightly different management, but overlapping strategies exist.

Prevention is far more effective than treatment once symptoms appear.

## Identifying the Three Blights

The first step is correctly identifying which blight is affecting your
plants, as management differs significantly.

Early blight causes dark brown concentric-ringed lesions on lower
leaves, starting near the soil and moving upward. The spots have a
distinctive "bullseye" pattern. It progresses slowly, yellowing and
drying leaves over weeks.

Late blight is the most aggressive and destructive form. It causes
water-soaked dark lesions that spread rapidly, often accompanied by
white fuzzy growth on leaf undersides during humid conditions. Entire
plants can collapse within days during favorable conditions (cool, wet
weather).

Septoria leaf spot produces small circular spots with dark borders and
tiny black fruiting bodies (pycnidia) visible in the center of each
spot. It typically starts on the lowest leaves and progresses upward.

## Cultural Prevention Methods

Prevention is the most effective strategy against all three blight types.

Proper spacing of 24-36 inches between plants ensures good air
circulation, which reduces leaf wetness duration. Staking or caging
keeps foliage off the ground where pathogens overwinter.

Mulching around the base of plants prevents soil from splashing onto
lower leaves during rain or irrigation. This is particularly important
for early blight and Septoria, whose spores live in the soil.

Avoid overhead watering; use [[comparison-drip-irrigation-vs-passive-irrigation]] or soaker hoses instead.
If overhead watering is necessary, water early in the morning so leaves
dry quickly.

Remove lower leaves once fruits begin forming on the first cluster.
This improves airflow and removes the oldest leaves most susceptible to
infection.

potatoes, peppers, or eggplants in the same location. All are members

Destroy infected plant material at the end of the season rather than
composting it. Many blight pathogens survive composting temperatures.

## Organic Fungicide Options

Several organic fungicides can help prevent and slow the spread of
blight when applied before infection becomes severe.

Copper-based fungicides (copper hydroxide, copper sulfate) are the
most widely used organic option. They provide broad-spectrum protection
against all three blight types. Apply every 7-10 days, coating both
leaf surfaces. [[cervantes-neem-oil-organic-pest-control]]

Biological fungicides containing Bacillus subtilis (Serenade) or
Trichoderma harzianum work by colonizing leaf surfaces and outcompeting
pathogens. These are most effective as preventives rather than curatives.
