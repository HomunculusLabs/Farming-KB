# Do beans and peas feed nearby plants with nitrogen?

## Short Answer

Beans and peas can improve soil nitrogen over time, but they usually do not directly feed nearby plants with large amounts of nitrogen while they are alive.
Most nitrogen fixed by a bean or pea plant first goes into that plant's leaves, roots, nodules, pods, and seeds.
Neighboring crops may receive small amounts through root exudates, nodule turnover, and shared soil biology, but the main benefit comes after residues decompose.
If pods or dry seed are harvested and removed, much of the nitrogen leaves the bed with the crop.
The practical answer is explained by [[legume-nitrogen-myth-beans-feed-corn]], [[nitrogen-availability-in-legumes]], and [[legume-nitrogen-dynamics]].

## Detailed Explanation

### The myth in one sentence

The myth says that planting beans beside a hungry crop automatically fertilizes that crop during the same season.
The reality is slower and more biological.
Beans and peas can fix atmospheric nitrogen through nodules, but they use most of that nitrogen for their own growth.
That nitrogen becomes soil fertility mainly when roots, nodules, leaves, stems, and unharvested residues break down.
This is why [[legume-nitrogen-myth-beans-feed-corn]] is a useful caution against oversimplified companion planting claims.
The myth contains a seed of truth, but the timing is usually wrong.

### How beans and peas fix nitrogen

Beans and peas form nodules when compatible rhizobia bacteria infect their roots.
Inside those nodules, bacteria convert atmospheric nitrogen gas into forms the plant can use.
The plant pays for that service with sugars produced by photosynthesis.
The nodule process is covered by [[rhizobia-legume-nodule-formation]].
The bacterial partnership is covered by [[rhizobium-legume-symbiosis]].
The larger fixation mechanism is covered by [[legume-nitrogen-fixation-mechanism]].

### Why the neighbor crop gets little immediately

A living bean plant is not a nitrogen faucet for its neighbor.
The plant controls the flow of fixed nitrogen because that nitrogen is valuable for its own proteins, enzymes, and seeds.
A nearby corn, tomato, squash, or cannabis plant competes for light, water, and minerals in the same soil volume.
It may benefit from the legume's soil cover, microbial stimulation, or later residue decomposition.
It should not be expected to receive a full feeding while the bean is still growing.
That practical distinction is part of [[nitrogen-availability-in-legumes]].

### What happens when the crop is harvested

Harvest changes the nitrogen budget.
Green beans remove some nitrogen in pods, but dry beans and peas can remove a larger share in protein-rich seed.
If the gardener pulls the whole plant and exports vines, roots, and pods, the bed receives much less nitrogen back.
If roots stay in place and vines become mulch or compost, more nitrogen returns to the soil food web.
The crop can therefore be food production, fertility building, or a compromise between the two.
For crop management, see [[growing-beans-peas-legume-guide]] and [[solomon-growing-beans-and-peas]].

### Residues are the main transfer pathway

The most important transfer happens after plant tissues die and decompose.
Roots, nodules, leaves, and stems feed bacteria, fungi, protozoa, nematodes, earthworms, and other decomposers.
Those organisms mineralize organic nitrogen into plant-available forms over time.
The process connects beans and peas to the broader [[nitrogen-cycle-in-soil]].
It also connects them to [[query-what-is-the-soil-food-web-and-why-does-it-matter]].
Leaving residues in place is usually more important than the exact companion-planting geometry.

### When interplanting still helps

Interplanting beans and peas can still be useful even if instant nitrogen feeding is modest.
Legumes can cover soil, shade weeds, add flowers, support beneficial insects, and diversify root patterns.
Pole beans can use vertical space that might otherwise be empty.
Peas can finish early and leave a bed ready for a summer crop.
Mixed plantings can reduce bare soil and improve garden resilience.
Those benefits are real even when the nitrogen story is slower than advertised.

### When interplanting hurts

Beans and peas can also compete with the main crop.
A vigorous pole bean can shade a crop that needs full sun.
Dense pea vines can reduce airflow and increase disease risk in damp weather.
All legumes use water and minerals even while they fix atmospheric nitrogen.
If the main crop is already nutrient-stressed, adding a companion plant can make the stress worse.
Interplanting should be designed for space, light, airflow, and harvest access rather than nitrogen alone.

### Better ways to use beans and peas for fertility

Grow beans and peas as part of a rotation rather than expecting instant sharing.
Leave roots in the soil after harvest to preserve nodules and root channels.
Chop vines and return them as mulch if disease pressure is low.
Compost residues when they need to be moved off the bed.
Follow a legume crop with a hungry feeder after residues begin decomposing.
For intentional fertility, use dedicated [[cover-crops-green-manures]] or [[green-manure-cover-crops]].

### Cover crop peas versus edible peas

Field peas grown as cover crops are managed differently from edible snap peas or shelling peas.
A cover crop pea stand is usually grown for biomass and terminated before or around flowering.
An edible pea crop is grown for pods and often removed from the system through harvest.
The cover crop returns more nitrogen because more of its biomass stays on site.
The edible crop returns less nitrogen but produces food.
Both can be useful, but they should not be credited with the same fertility effect.

### How to evaluate the claim in your garden

Dig plants and look for nodules before assuming fixation happened.
Split nodules and look for pink or reddish interiors as a sign of activity.
Watch whether the following crop grows better after residues decompose.
Compare beds where legume residues stayed with beds where plants were removed.
Avoid adding high soluble nitrogen if the goal is to encourage fixation.
Use [[legume-nitrogen-yields-by-species]] to keep expectations realistic.

## Key Points

- Beans and peas fix nitrogen for themselves first, then feed the soil mainly through residue decomposition.
- [[legume-nitrogen-myth-beans-feed-corn]] explains why the common companion-planting claim is often exaggerated.
- [[legume-nitrogen-fixation-mechanism]] explains how the biological process actually works.
- [[rhizobia-legume-nodule-formation]] shows why nodules are the visible sign to check.
- [[nitrogen-availability-in-legumes]] explains when neighboring or following crops can use the nitrogen.
- Harvesting pods or dry seed exports nitrogen that otherwise could have returned to the bed.
- Leaving roots, nodules, vines, and leaves in place increases the fertility return.
- Interplanting can help with diversity and soil cover, but it can also create light, water, and airflow competition.
- Dedicated cover-crop legumes usually build more fertility than edible bean or pea crops.
