# What Earthworks Should I Use For Water Harvesting?

## Short Answer

Choose water-harvesting earthworks by starting with slope, soil, rainfall pattern, overflow safety, and the consequence of failure.
Small slow-spread-sink features such as swales, basins, and contour paths are usually safer for gardens than large dams or aggressive reshaping.
Big earthworks should follow careful observation, survey, and overflow design because water concentrates energy when it moves downhill.
Use [[permaculture-earthworks]] for the general design frame and [[earthworks-for-water-management]] for the water-specific logic.
The right earthwork is the one that hydrates soil while safely passing storm overflow.

## Detailed Explanation

### Begin With Observation

Do not start by hiring a machine.
Start by watching where water enters, slows, ponds, erodes, and leaves the site.
[[permaculture-earthworks-and-water-harvesting]] emphasizes that earthworks should cooperate with existing water movement rather than fight it.
Walk the land during rain if it is safe.
Mark rills, wet spots, compacted paths, roof runoff, driveway flow, and natural deposition zones.
The best earthwork often appears after several storms reveal the pattern.

### Use Contour To Slow Water

Contour earthworks slow water because every point along the line is at the same elevation.
A swale on contour spreads runoff sideways and gives it time to soak in.
[[pdc-earthworks-and-swales]] covers swales as a standard permaculture water-harvesting tool.
Swales are most appropriate on gentle to moderate slopes with stable soils and safe overflow routes.
They are not appropriate where saturation could destabilize a bank, foundation, or road cut.
Every contour feature needs a level sill spillway or other planned overflow.

### Match The Tool To The Slope

Flat land, gentle slopes, steep slopes, and gullies need different interventions.
Basins and infiltration beds work well on flat or nearly flat ground.
Swales and contour berms work on gentle slopes when overflow can be handled safely.
Terraces may be better where slopes are too steep for broad infiltration lines.
[[comparison-terracing-vs-swale]] helps compare reshaping a slope into steps with holding water on contour.
Steep land requires more caution because stored water can trigger slips or erosion.

### Understand Swales, Dams, And Keyline

Swales store water in soil, while dams store water in open reservoirs.
Keyline design moves water from wetter valleys toward drier ridges using subtle cultivation and layout.
[[comparison-keyline-vs-contour-swale]] is useful when deciding between pattern cultivation and excavated infiltration features.
Dams are powerful but require spillways, compaction, catchment analysis, and legal awareness.
Swales are simpler but still fail if they capture too much water without release.
Keyline is usually less visually dramatic but can be effective over broad pasture or orchard areas.

### Design Safe Overflow First

Overflow is not an accessory; it is the main safety feature.
[[mollison-designers-swale-design-and-water-harvesting-earthworks]] emphasizes sizing and linking earthworks so excess water exits without cutting new gullies.
A spillway should be level, armored when necessary, and directed to a stable receiving area.
Do not send overflow toward buildings, septic systems, roads, or neighboring property.
A series of small overflow events should be harmless.
If a single storm can cause damage, the earthwork is too risky or underdesigned.

### Plant Immediately After Disturbance

Fresh earth is vulnerable to erosion, drying, and weed invasion.
[[mollison-designers-post-earthworks-planting-and-slope-stabilisation]] focuses on stabilizing disturbed ground after construction.
Seed annual cover immediately, mulch exposed soil, and plant deep-rooted perennials as soon as possible.
Fast cover protects the shape of the earthwork while permanent roots develop.
On swale berms, mix grasses, legumes, shrubs, and trees according to the desired system.
The planting plan should be ready before the machine starts.

### Use Earthworks With Roof And Surface Water

Many sites need roof harvesting, greywater, paths, and planting basins as much as large contour works.
[[pdc-water-harvesting-earthworks-design]] treats water harvesting as a connected site system.
Roof runoff can feed tanks first, then overflow to basins or swales.
Paths can be shaped as infiltration features instead of compacted drains.
Garden beds can be placed where earthworks already hydrate the soil profile.
The goal is to catch water repeatedly at different scales rather than rely on one large structure.

### Avoid Common Mistakes

The most common mistake is building an earthwork without knowing where overflow goes.
The second is placing infiltration where water should actually be drained away.
The third is making the feature too large for the catchment and soil.
The fourth is leaving bare earth after construction.
The fifth is assuming a diagram from another climate will work unchanged.
[[mollison-earthworks-planning-and-post-earthworks-planting]] links planning and revegetation because both decide whether the earthwork matures or fails.

### Choose Low-Risk First Projects

For beginners, start with small basins, contour mulch lines, shallow diversion-to-infiltration paths, and garden-scale swales.
These projects teach water behavior without creating major failure consequences.
A backyard grower can often solve drought stress with mulch, roof overflow basins, and small contour beds.
Large ponds, dam walls, road drainage changes, and steep-slope cuts should wait for expert survey and design.
[[earthworks-for-water-management]] is most useful when read as a decision framework rather than a construction recipe.
Good earthworks disappear into a hydrated, planted landscape over time.

## Key Points

- Start with observation, not excavation.
- [[permaculture-earthworks]] gives the broad design principles for reshaping land carefully.
- [[earthworks-for-water-management]] explains how earthworks slow, spread, sink, and safely release water.
