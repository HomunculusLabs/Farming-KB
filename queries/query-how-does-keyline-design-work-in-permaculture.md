# How Does Keyline Design Work in Permaculture?

## Short Answer
Keyline design is a land management technique developed by P.A. Yeomans that
uses the natural topography of a landscape to distribute water evenly across
slopes. It identifies a specific contour line called the keyline, where the
valley floor meets the slope, and uses cultivation and earthworks along this
line to spread rainfall runoff from wet valleys to dry ridges, dramatically
improving hydration and soil health across the entire property.

## Detailed Explanation

Keyline design is one of the most elegant and effective tools in the
permaculture toolkit for managing water on sloping land. Developed by Australian
farmer P.A. Yeomans in the 1950s, the system works with natural landscape
contours rather than against them. The [[keyline-design-water-management]] page
explains the full theory and practice, but the core concept is simple: by
identifying a specific contour line on a hillside and working the soil along it,
you can redirect water flow from concentrated valley channels across the broader
slope, ensuring more even moisture distribution.

The keyline itself is found at the point where a valley or watercourse begins to
flatten out, typically where the steep valley walls meet the more gradual slope
of the ridge. This is the narrowest point of the valley and the place where a
contour line drawn from one side of the valley to the other will have the
greatest effect on water distribution. Once the keyline is identified, all
cultivation, planting, and earthworks follow parallel lines that gradually fall
off the true contour as they extend toward the ridges. This slight downward
slope toward the ridges is what moves water from the wet valleys to the dry
ridges.

The primary tool for implementing keyline design is the Keyline plow, also known
as the Yeomans plow or
[[soil-conditioning-chisel-plow-biological-methods]]. This implement uses narrow shanks to fracture compacted subsoil without inverting the soil profile. Unlike a moldboard plow that turns the soil over, the keyline plow creates deep vertical channels that improve water infiltration, root penetration, and soil aeration while preserving the existing soil structure and microbial communities. The cultivation depth can be adjusted from shallow surface ripping to deep subsoiling at 12 to 20 inches.

Keyline pattern cultivation follows the keyline contour as a reference and then
gradually diverges from true contour as the lines extend up the slopes toward
the ridges. This creates shallow swale-like depressions that capture and spread
rainwater runoff. Over time, the combination of improved infiltration from
subsoiling and the directional spreading of surface water transforms the
hydrology of the landscape. Dry ridges that once supported only sparse
vegetation become productive, while valley bottoms that were prone to
waterlogging drain more effectively.

Integration with other water management techniques amplifies the benefits of
keyline design. [[swale-design-construction]] and
[[comparison-swale-design-vs-contour-swale-irrigation-design]] can be constructed along
keyline patterns to create more permanent water distribution features.
[[mollison-designers-dam-construction-and-water-storage]] positioned at the highest practical point on
the keyline can provide gravity-fed irrigation to the entire property below. The
[[water-harvesting-techniques-comparison]] page
shows how keyline design fits into a broader water management strategy alongside
swales, gabions, and check dams.

The soil health benefits of keyline design extend well beyond water
distribution. By breaking up compacted layers without inverting the soil, the
[[lowenfels-earthworms-soil-food-web]] is preserved and enhanced. Deeper root channels
allow perennial plants to access moisture and nutrients from greater depths. The
[[teaming-with-nutrients-composting-and-nutrient-cycling]] process accelerates as increased
water infiltration supports more active microbial communities. Over time,
keyline-managed land develops deeper topsoil, greater organic matter content,
and improved aggregate stability.

Implementation of keyline design does not require expensive equipment or
earthworks on a small scale. A simple A-frame level or laser level can be used
to find contours. On small properties, a broadfork or hand-powered ripper can
accomplish what the keyline plow does on larger acreage. The
[[water-catchment-permaculture]] principles apply at any scale,
from a backyard food forest to a hundred-acre farm. Start by observing how water
moves across your land during rain events, identify the keypoint in each valley,
and plan your cultivation and planting accordingly.

For tree crops and
[[query-how-to-start-a-food-forest]], keyline design provides the water distribution framework that makes dense tree plantings viable on sloping land without irrigation. Trees planted along keyline patterns benefit from the improved soil moisture and deeper soil structure. The integration of [[companion-planting-with-fungi]]
with keyline-planted trees creates resilient, self-watering polyculture systems
that become more productive and self-sustaining with each passing year as the
soil and tree roots develop together.

- The keyline-design|keyline is found where the valley floor meets the slope
