# What Is the Difference Between a High Tunnel and a Hoop House?

## Short Answer

A high tunnel and a hoop house are essentially the same structure: a passively heated, hoop-shaped frame covered with polyethylene plastic.

The term "high tunnel" is more commonly used in academic and USDA contexts, while "hoop house" is the popular term among gardeners and homesteaders.

In practice, high tunnels tend to be taller and designed for standing-height growing, whereas smaller hoop houses may be low and require bending to enter.

Both structures extend the growing season by trapping solar heat and protecting crops from wind, rain, and frost.

## Are They Actually Different Structures?

No single definition separates the two terms universally.

The USDA Natural Resources Conservation Service (NRCS) defines a high tunnel as a structure with a single-layer plastic covering, no permanent heating or ventilation systems, and crops grown directly in the ground.

By this NRCS definition, a hoop house that meets those criteria is a high tunnel.

However, many growers use "hoop house" for any size of hoop-framed poly structure, including low tunnels that are just a few feet tall.

The distinction is mostly regional and contextual rather than structural.

### When People Say "High Tunnel"

They usually mean a tall, walk-in structure with metal or PVC hoops anchored into the ground.

High tunnels typically stand 8 to 16 feet tall at the peak.

They are large enough to walk and work inside comfortably.

They often have roll-up side walls for ventilation and end doors for access.

Commercial growers and research farms favor the term.

### When People Say "Hoop House"

The term is more informal and can refer to structures of any size.

A small 4-foot-tall structure over a raised bed is often called a hoop house by home gardeners.

It is the catch-all term for any curved-frame structure covered with plastic.

Small-scale and hobby growers tend to use this term more often.

## Structural Features They Share

Both use curved ribs (hoops) made from metal conduit, PVC pipe, or bent wood.

Both are covered with 6-mil polyethylene plastic sheeting, typically UV-resistant.

Both rely on passive solar heating with no artificial heat source.

Both lack permanent concrete foundations and can be moved or disassembled.

Crops are grown directly in native soil rather than in containers or benches.

Neither is considered a permanent building in most zoning codes.

## Key Differences in Practice

| Feature | High Tunnel | Hoop House |
|---------|------------|------------|
| Typical height | 8 to 16 feet | 4 to 10 feet |
| Primary users | Commercial farms, research | Home gardeners, homesteads |
| Cost range | $1,000 to $10,000+ | $50 to $2,000 |
| Frame material | Galvanized steel conduit | PVC pipe or EMT conduit |
| Ventilation | Roll-up sides, end doors | Manual venting or open ends |
| USDA NRCS eligible | Yes (cost-share programs) | Sometimes, if tall enough |
| Growing method | In-ground beds | Beds or containers |

## Growing in a High Tunnel or Hoop House

The growing season can extend by 4 to 8 weeks on each end compared to open-field growing.

Warm-season crops like tomatoes, peppers, and cucumbers thrive in the protected, warm environment.

Cool-season crops like lettuce, spinach, and kale can be grown through winter in mild climates.

Drip irrigation is the preferred watering method since overhead watering increases humidity and disease risk.

[[query-how-do-i-extend-my-growing-season-with-cold-frames-and-season-extension-techniques|Season extension techniques]] like interior row covers can add even more frost protection inside a tunnel.

## Ventilation and Temperature Management

Overheating is the most common problem in tunnels and hoop houses on sunny days.

Temperatures can spike 30 to 40 degrees above ambient on clear winter days.

Roll-up side walls are essential for cross-ventilation in taller structures.

End doors should be opened on warm days even in winter.

Automatic vent openers using wax cylinders are an affordable upgrade for smaller hoop houses.

## Site Selection and Setup

Choose a level site with good drainage to prevent flooding during heavy rain.

Orient the structure east-to-west for maximum light capture in winter.

Position it to take advantage of prevailing winds for natural cross-ventilation.

Locate it near a water source since drip irrigation will be needed.

- Avoid low-lying frost pockets where cold air settles at night.

## Cost and Materials

A small DIY hoop house can be built for under $100 using PVC pipe and greenhouse plastic.

A mid-size 20-by-50-foot high tunnel with steel frames costs between $2,000 and $5,000.

- Commercial-grade high tunnels with automated ventilation can exceed $15,000.

## Key Points

- High tunnels and hoop houses are the same basic structure under different names.
- High tunnels tend to be taller and used commercially, while hoop houses range from small to large.
- Both provide passive solar heating, wind protection, and season extension without artificial climate control.
- Ventilation through roll-up sides and open doors is critical to prevent overheating.
- Crops are grown directly in the ground, distinguishing them from traditional greenhouses.
