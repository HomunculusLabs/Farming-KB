# What Is the Difference Between Aerobic and Anaerobic Composting?

## Short Answer
Aerobic composting uses oxygen-loving microorganisms to break down organic
matter quickly at higher temperatures, producing carbon dioxide, heat, and
finished compost in weeks to months. Anaerobic composting occurs without
oxygen, produces methane and other gases, works more slowly, and yields a
different end product that is wetter and more acidic, as seen in bokashi
fermentation.

## Detailed Explanation
Composting is the biological decomposition of organic matter into a stable,
humus-rich soil amendment, but the process unfolds very differently depending
on whether oxygen is present. Understanding the distinction between
composting methods that rely on aerobic (with oxygen) versus anaerobic
(without oxygen) decomposition is fundamental to choosing the right approach
for your situation. Both pathways occur in nature and can be harnessed by
growers, but they produce different results in terms of speed, odor, nutrient
retention, and end-product quality.

Aerobic composting is the most widely practiced method and involves creating
conditions where oxygen-dependent bacteria, fungi, and actinomycetes can
thrive. As described in [[teaming-with-microbes-compost-biology]], aerobic
decomposition progresses through distinct microbial successional stages,
beginning with mesophilic organisms that raise the temperature through
metabolic activity, followed by thermophilic organisms that sustain
temperatures of 130 to 160 degrees Fahrenheit, killing pathogens and weed
seeds. This heat generation is a hallmark of aerobic composting and a key
advantage over anaerobic methods. The [[thermal-composting-vs-aact]] comparison
shows that traditional hot composting produces finished material in 2 to 12
weeks, depending on management intensity.

Anaerobic composting, in contrast, relies on microorganisms that function in
the absence of oxygen. This process is much slower and produces intermediate
compounds including organic acids, hydrogen sulfide, and methane, which cause
the characteristic foul odors associated with anaerobic conditions. The most
well-managed form of anaerobic composting is [[bokashi-composting]], which
uses effective microorganisms (primarily lactobacillus) in an airtight
container to ferment organic waste through a controlled anaerobic process.
Bokashi preserves more nutrients than aerobic methods because the fermentation
process does not release carbon and nitrogen as gases, but the end product is
a pre-compost that must be buried in soil to finish decomposing.

From a nutrient perspective, anaerobic methods like bokashi retain more
nitrogen because nitrogen is not lost as ammonia gas, which is a significant
issue in hot aerobic composting if carbon-to-nitrogen ratios are not carefully
managed. However, aerobic composting generally produces a more mature,
humus-stable product that is immediately ready for use as a soil amendment.
The [[aact-definitions-and-types-ingham]] framework distinguishes between
traditional aerobic composting, aerated compost teas, and anaerobic fermented
extracts, each serving different purposes in soil management.

Space and management requirements differ significantly between the two
approaches. Aerobic composting requires adequate space for piles or bins,
regular turning to maintain oxygen levels, and sufficient carbon material to
prevent anaerobic pockets from forming. Anaerobic systems like bokashi
require minimal space and can even be used indoors in apartments, since the
sealed fermentation process prevents odor release. For urban growers with
limited space, anaerobic pre-composting followed by soil burial offers a
practical solution that aerobic composting cannot match.

Both methods have their place in a comprehensive soil management strategy.
Many experienced growers combine approaches, using bokashi fermentation for
kitchen scraps and aerobic hot composting for yard waste and larger material.
The [[soil-food-web]] benefits from both types of organic matter input, as
they support different microbial communities. Aerobic compost favors fungi and
actinomycetes, while anaerobic fermentation promotes lactic acid bacteria and
yeasts. A diverse feeding strategy for soil biology ultimately produces the
most resilient and productive growing systems.

Temperature profiles provide a clear distinction between the two methods.
Aerobic composting generates significant heat through microbial metabolism,
with well-managed piles reaching 130 to 160 degrees Fahrenheit in the core.
This thermophilic phase serves an important sanitation function, killing
weed seeds, plant pathogens, and human pathogens within 3 to 7 days at
sufficient temperatures. Anaerobic composting, by contrast, generates little
to no heat and does not achieve pathogen kill through thermal means. While
the acidic environment of bokashi fermentation inhibits many pathogens,
anaerobic pre-compost is not considered sanitized until it has been buried in
soil and allowed to decompose aerobically for several weeks. This means that
materials contaminated with persistent pathogens may not be fully sanitized by
anaerobic methods alone.

Cost and accessibility considerations also differ between the approaches.
Aerobic composting requires only basic materials like a bin or pile area, a
pitchfork for turning, and sufficient organic matter. It can be done at any
scale from apartment balconies to municipal facilities. Anaerobic bokashi
systems require purchasing effective microorganism inoculant (EM or equivalent)
and airtight containers, which represent an ongoing cost. However, bokashi
systems can handle materials that are difficult to compost aerobically,
including meat, dairy, and cooked foods, because the acidic fermentation
environment prevents putrefaction. This broader input tolerance makes
anaerobic systems particularly attractive for urban composters who generate
diverse food waste streams.

## Key Points
- Aerobic composting uses oxygen and heat to decompose organic matter in 2 to
  12 weeks with minimal odor
- Anaerobic composting works without oxygen, is slower, and produces methane
  and organic acids
- [[bokashi-composting]] is a controlled anaerobic fermentation that retains
  more nutrients but requires soil burial to finish
- Aerobic methods produce more mature compost; anaerobic methods preserve
  more nitrogen
- Both approaches support different microbial communities and can be combined
  for diverse [[soil-food-web]] inputs
