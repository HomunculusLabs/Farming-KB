# What Are the Stages of Cannabis Growth?

## Short Answer
Cannabis progresses through four main growth stages: germination (3 to 10 days), seedling (2 to 3 weeks), vegetative (3 to 16 weeks depending on variety), and flowering (8 to 12 weeks).
Each stage has distinct environmental requirements, nutrient demands, and management priorities that directly affect final yield and quality.
Understanding these stages allows growers to anticipate the plant's needs and intervene at the right times with training, feeding, and environmental adjustments.
Photoperiod varieties progress through stages based on light cycle, while autoflowers transition automatically based on age.

## Detailed Explanation
Understanding the cannabis growth cycle is fundamental to successful cultivation regardless of growing method.
Each stage of development requires different light intensity, nutrient ratios, humidity levels, and training techniques.
The [[cannabis-growth-stage-checklist]] provides a comprehensive overview of tasks and targets for each phase, making it an essential reference for both new and experienced growers.
Photoperiod varieties progress through these stages based on light cycle, while autoflowers transition automatically based on age, which simplifies scheduling but limits the grower's ability to control plant size through vegetative period length.

### Germination and Seedling Stages

Germination is the first stage, where the seed absorbs water, cracks its shell, and pushes out a taproot.
This process typically takes 3 to 10 days and requires consistent moisture, darkness, and warmth around 70 to 78 degrees Fahrenheit.
Popular methods include the paper towel technique, direct planting into moist medium, and soaking in water for 12 to 24 hours.
Once the taproot emerges and reaches roughly half an inch in length, the seed is ready to be planted root-down into its growing medium.
Proper germination technique is covered in depth in the [[cannabis-seedling-care-guide]], which also addresses common problems like damping off and shell-stuck seedlings.
Seeds that fail to germinate within 10 days are likely non-viable and should be discarded to prevent mold in the growing medium.
Using seeds from reputable breeders and storing them properly in a cool, dark place maximizes germination success rates.

The seedling stage begins when the first set of true leaves appears and lasts for roughly two to three weeks.
During this phase, the plant is establishing its root system and building its first photosynthetic leaves.
Seedlings are extremely fragile and require gentle handling, moderate humidity of 65 to 80 percent, lower light intensity than mature plants (around 200 to 400 PPFD), and minimal nutrients.
Overfeeding seedlings is one of the most common beginner mistakes, as their nutrient needs are minimal and their young root systems cannot yet handle concentrated nutrient solutions.
The [[green-cannabis-seedling-care]] guide covers optimal seedling conditions including temperature, humidity dome use, and watering frequency.
Seedlings in a [[no-till-cannabis]] living soil system may not need any additional nutrients at all during this stage, as the biology in the soil provides everything the plant requires.

### Vegetative Growth and Training

The vegetative stage is when the plant does the majority of its structural growth, developing the branches, stems, and root mass that will support flower production later.
Photoperiod cannabis remains in vegetative growth under light schedules of 18 hours on and 6 hours off (or 24 hours continuous), while autoflowering varieties transition to flowering after a fixed period of 3 to 5 weeks regardless of light cycle.
The [[cannabis-vegetative-growth-stage]] covers the key management practices including training techniques like topping, low-stress training, and screen of green (SCROG) that shape the plant's architecture for maximum light exposure and yield.
Nitrogen is the primary nutrient demand during this phase, though balanced feeding with adequate calcium and magnesium is important for healthy cellular structure.
Plants in well-built [[cannabis-living-soil-guide]] systems often show more vigorous vegetative growth than those in inert media because the biological nutrient cycling provides a steady supply of all essential elements.

Training during the vegetative stage is one of the most impactful things a grower can do to increase yield.
Topping (cutting the main stem above a node) encourages the plant to develop two main colas instead of one, effectively doubling the flowering sites at the top of the canopy.
Low-stress training (LST) involves gently bending and tying branches to create an even, flat canopy that maximizes light absorption across all bud sites.
Screen of green (SCROG) uses a horizontal net to support bent branches and create an even canopy height, which is particularly effective in indoor grows with limited vertical space.
These techniques should be completed at least two weeks before the planned transition to flowering to allow the plant time to recover and redirect energy toward new growth.
Growers using [[cannabis-indoor-grow-lighting-systems]] should also raise their lights during the flowering stretch to prevent light burn on the tallest colas.

### Transition to Flowering

The transition from vegetative to flowering is a critical management period that requires careful attention.
When the light cycle is reduced to 12 hours on and 12 hours off for photoperiod varieties, the plant shifts its energy from foliage production to reproductive development.
During the first one to two weeks of this transition, plants often undergo a dramatic stretch where they can double or even triple in height.
The [[cannabis-flowering-stage]] details how the plant develops pre-flowers and then produces bud sites that swell and mature over the following weeks.
Nutrient needs shift dramatically during this transition, with demand for nitrogen decreasing and demand for phosphorus and potassium increasing.
Growers using [[cannabis-living-soil-amendment-schedule]] top-dress methods should apply their flowering amendment blend during this transition period to ensure phosphorus and potassium are available as buds begin to form.

Environmental conditions during the transition should be adjusted to reduce stress and support the hormonal shift.
Temperatures should be slightly cooler than during vegetative growth, ideally 68 to 78 degrees Fahrenheit, with a nighttime temperature drop of at least 5 to 10 degrees to help the plant recognize the seasonal change.
Humidity should begin decreasing from vegetative levels (60 to 70 percent) down toward flowering targets (40 to 50 percent) to reduce the risk of bud rot and powdery mildew as dense flowers develop.
This gradual reduction in humidity, combined with increased airflow from [[cannabis-indoor-ventilation-setup]], creates an environment that supports resin production while minimizing disease pressure.

### Flowering, Harvest, and Curing

The flowering stage itself is typically divided into early, mid, and late phases, each with distinct management priorities.
Early flowering (weeks 1 to 3) is characterized by rapid stretching, the emergence of pistils (white hair-like structures), and the beginning of calyx development.
Mid-flowering (weeks 4 to 6) is when buds begin to bulk up and develop their structure, trichome production becomes visible to the naked eye, and the plant's aroma intensifies significantly.
Late flowering (weeks 7 to 12 depending on variety) is when resin production peaks, trichomes change from clear to cloudy to amber, and the plant approaches harvest readiness.
Indica-dominant varieties typically finish flowering faster (7 to 9 weeks) while sativa-dominant varieties may require 10 to 14 weeks, making variety selection an important consideration for planning your grow schedule.
During late flowering, many growers reduce or eliminate nitrogen entirely and may use finishing products like [[cannabis-knf-ffj-ripeness-progression]] to enhance terpene and cannabinoid production in the final weeks.

Harvest timing is arguably the most impactful decision a grower makes, as it determines cannabinoid potency, terpene profile, and overall effect.
Harvesting too early results in underdeveloped cannabinoids and a racy, incomplete high, while harvesting too late degrades THC into CBN and produces a more sedative effect.
The [[cannabis-harvesting-techniques]] cover methods for determining readiness including trichome observation with a jeweler's loupe, pistil coloration, and overall bud appearance.
After harvest, proper drying and [[cannabis-curing-jar-technique]] is essential for developing smooth flavor, full terpene expression, and optimal potency through controlled moisture release over four to eight weeks.
Rushing the cure is one of the most common mistakes that reduces the quality of otherwise well-grown flower.
Proper curing in airtight glass jars at 60 to 65 percent humidity with daily burping for the first two weeks transforms harsh, green-tasting bud into smooth, aromatic flower with full cannabinoid expression.
