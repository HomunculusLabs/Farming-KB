# How Do I Make Koji at Home for Beginners?
Welcome to the wonderful world of koji! Making koji at home is one of the most rewarding
fermentation projects you can take on. This FAQ walks you through everything — from
gathering supplies to celebrating your first batch. You've got this!

## 1. What Is Koji and Why Should I Make It at Home?
**Koji** is cooked grain (usually rice or barley) inoculated with *Aspergillus oryzae*,
a beneficial mold that produces powerful enzymes. These enzymes break down starches into
sugars and proteins into amino acids — the foundation of umami in Japanese cuisine.
Making koji at home gives you deeper flavor in miso, soy sauce, and amazake; cost savings
versus buying pre-made; creative control over strain and grain; and a connection to over
2,000 years of culinary tradition.

## 2. What Equipment Do I Need to Get Started?
You don't need a professional setup. A beginner-friendly kit includes:
- **Fine-mesh or bamboo steamer** — even steaming without waterlogging
- **Instant-read thermometer** — for grain and incubation temps
- **Shallow tray (~2–3 cm deep)** — wooden or food-safe plastic for incubation
- **Clean damp cloth or paper towels** — covering tray during incubation
- **Spray bottle with clean water** — rehydrating grains during incubation
- **70% isopropyl alcohol** — sanitizing all surfaces and tools
Optional but helpful: a seedling heat mat with thermostat (e.g., Inkbird) to hold steady
temperatures.

## 3. Where Do I Get Koji Spores (Koji-Kin)?
Koji spores (**koji-kin**, 麹菌) are sold as a fine powder from online specialty shops
(Cultures for Health, GEM Cultures), Etsy sellers, and some Japanese grocery stores.
Look for fresh stock and check expiration dates. Store in the freezer in an airtight
container (lasts 6–12 months). Let it reach room temperature before opening to avoid
condensation inside the packet.

## 4. What's the Best Grain to Start With?
**Short-grain white rice (Japonica)** — sushi rice or Calrose — is the best first grain:
predictable, easy to find, and forgiving. Avoid jasmine, basmatin, or long-grain varieties
(they don't hold moisture well). Once comfortable, try pearl barley or brown rice for
different enzyme profiles. Start with 300–500 g of dry rice for your first batch.

## 5. How Do I Prepare and Steam the Rice?
1. **Wash** the rice 3–5 times in cold water until the water runs mostly clear
2. **Soak** in fresh water for 8–12 hours (overnight in the fridge)
3. **Drain** in a strainer for 30–60 minutes; surface should look dry but grains plump
4. **Steam** in a single layer for 45–60 minutes — tender with a slightly firm core
5. **Cool** to 35°C (95°F) before inoculation
**Pro tip:** Rice should be cooked through but not mushy. Too wet = bacteria; too dry =
poor koji growth.

## 6. How Do I Inoculate and Incubate the Rice?
1. **Cool** steamed rice to 35°C (95°F) — hotter will kill the spores
2. **Sanitize** hands and tools with 70% isopropyl alcohol
3. **Dust** koji-kin evenly over the rice (~1 g per 1 kg dry rice — a small pinch).
   Toss gently to distribute
4. **Transfer** to your incubation tray in a layer about 2–3 cm thick
5. **Cover** loosely with a damp (not dripping) cloth
6. **Incubate** (see temperature section below)
After ~12–16 hours, you'll notice grains clumping and a sweet, chestnut-like aroma. Do
your first **mixing (muri)** with sanitized hands to break apart clumps. Mix every
10–12 hours after that to ensure even growth and prevent overheating.

## 7. What Temperature and Humidity Do I Need?
| Stage | Temperature | Humidity |
|-------|------------|----------|
| Inoculation | 35°C (95°F) | ~80% |
| Early growth (0–24h) | 30–33°C (86–91°F) | ~85% |
| Active growth (24–48h) | 28–30°C (82–86°F) | 80–90% |
| Final maturation (48h+) | 25–28°C (77–82°F) | 75–80% |
A heat mat with thermostat is affordable and effective. Place the tray on the mat, drape
a clear plastic bag over it (leave gaps for air exchange), and monitor with your
thermometer. Avoid temps above 35°C (bacterial growth) or below 25°C (too slow).

## 8. How Long Does It Take to Make Koji?
A typical rice koji batch takes **36–48 hours** from inoculation to completion:
- **0–12h:** Spores germinate; not much visible change
- **12–24h:** White mycelium appears; grains clump; sweet aroma begins
- **24–36h:** Vigorous growth; grains fully bound; strong chestnut/cheese aroma
- **36–48h:** Mature koji; even white coverage; flavor at its peak
A few extra hours at lower temperature deepens enzyme development (great for miso).
Stopping earlier gives sweeter results (ideal for amazake).

## 9. How Do I Know When My Koji Is Ready?
**Good signs:** Even white fluffy coverage on all grains; firm, cake-like texture (the
whole tray holds together); sweet, fragrant aroma (chestnuts or mild cheese); grains
tender but easily crushed between fingers.
**Bad signs — discard the batch:** Yellow, green, or black spots (contamination); sour
or ammonia smell (bacterial growth); slippery or slimy texture.
When in doubt, trust your nose. Good koji smells wonderful. Bad koji smells wrong.

## 10. How Do I Store Finished Koji?
**Short-term (up to 1 week):** Refrigerate in an airtight container. Enzymes slowly
continue working, so use promptly for shio koji or amazake.
**Long-term (up to 6 months):** Freeze for 24+ hours, then transfer to airtight bags.
Frozen koji retains most enzyme activity — great for miso and soy sauce.
**Drying (up to 1 year):** Dehydrate at 40°C (104°F) or below until completely dry and
brittle. Store in a cool, dark, airtight jar. Rehydrate by soaking in warm water.

## 11. What Can I Make with My Homemade Koji?
Start with **shio koji** (koji + salt + water) — easiest project, ready in 7–10 days,
amazing as a marinade, seasoning, or soup booster. From there explore **miso** (koji +
soybeans + salt, weeks to years), **amazake** (sweet rice drink), **sake**, **mirin**,
**shoyu** (soy sauce), **garlic shio koji** (incredible on everything), and **koji
butter** (try it on toast or steak). Each project unlocks new dimensions of flavor.

## 12. What Are the Most Common Beginner Mistakes?
1. **Rice too wet** — The #1 cause of failure. Drain thoroughly; steam, don't boil
2. **Poor sanitation** — Clean and sanitize everything; work quickly after cooling
3. **Temperature too high** — Above 35°C (95°F) encourages bacteria and off-flavors
4. **Temperature too low** — Below 25°C (77°F) slows koji, giving contaminants a foothold
5. **Not mixing often enough** — Koji generates heat. Mix every 10–12 hours
6. **Old koji-kin** — Check expiration dates; always store in the freezer
7. **Giving up after one failure** — Even experts have bad batches. Learn and try again!
