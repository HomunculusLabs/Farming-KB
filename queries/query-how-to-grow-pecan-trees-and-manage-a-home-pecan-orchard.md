## How Do I Grow Pecan Trees and Manage a Home Pecan Orchard?

## Short Answer

Growing pecan trees requires patience, deep well-drained soil, and a long-term commitment since trees typically take 6 to 10 years to bear nuts.
Pecans (Carya illinoinensis) are native to the southern and central United States and thrive in USDA hardiness zones 6 through 9.
A successful home orchard starts with choosing the right varieties for your climate, planting them with adequate spacing, and managing pests and nutrients consistently.

## Choosing the Right Pecan Variety

Pecan varieties fall into two main categories: Western and Eastern types.
Western varieties are smaller, thinner-shelled, and more drought-tolerant, making them popular in commercial orchards across Texas and New Mexico.
Eastern varieties produce larger nuts with thicker shells and are better suited to the humid Southeast where pecan scab pressure is high.

Home growers should prioritize disease resistance over nut size.
Varieties like Pawnee, Elliot, and Kanza offer excellent scab resistance and reliable production.
Pollen shed timing matters because pecans are partially self-unfruitful, meaning cross-pollination from a second variety dramatically improves yields.
Plant at least two varieties with overlapping pollen shed periods.

## Site Selection and Planting

Pecan trees need full sun and deep, well-drained soil at least 3 to 4 feet deep.
They are river-bottom trees by nature and perform best in loamy alluvial soils with a pH between 6.0 and 7.0.
Avoid shallow rocky soils, heavy clay that waterlogs, and sites with poor air drainage that encourage fungal disease.

Spacing is critical because mature pecan trees can reach 60 to 100 feet tall with a spread of 40 to 75 feet.

## Pruning and Training

Pecan trees naturally develop a strong central leader, and training should encourage this structure.
Remove competing leaders during the first few years to establish a single dominant trunk.
Lower branches can be retained for the first 5 to 8 years to maximize photosynthesis and trunk caliper.
Gradually raise the canopy by removing the lowest limbs over several seasons, never removing more than 25 percent of live canopy in one year.

Mature trees need periodic pruning to remove dead wood, improve air circulation, and thin crowded interiors.
Good airflow through the canopy is one of the best defenses against fungal diseases like pecan scab.
Prune during dormancy to minimize stress and disease entry points.

## Pest and Disease Management

Pecan scab is the most destructive disease affecting pecan trees, especially in humid climates.
It causes black lesions on leaves, shucks, and nuts, reducing both yield and quality.
Choosing resistant varieties is the first line of defense, followed by sanitation through removing fallen leaves and shucks each autumn.
Cultural practices like thinning crowded trees and maintaining open canopy structure also reduce humidity within the canopy.

Key insect pests include pecan weevil, hickory shuckworm, and aphids.
Pecan weevil larvae destroy the nut kernel from the inside and can devastate an untreated orchard.
Trunk banding with sticky traps catches weevils as they crawl up the tree in late summer.
Aphids excrete honeydew that fuels sooty mold growth on leaves and nuts, reducing photosynthesis.
