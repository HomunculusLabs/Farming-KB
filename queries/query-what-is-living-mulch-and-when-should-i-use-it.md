# What Is Living Mulch and When Should I Use It?

## Short Answer

Living mulch is a ground cover crop grown beneath or between main crops to suppress weeds, retain moisture, build soil, and provide habitat for beneficial insects.
Unlike traditional mulch made from dead material, living mulch is a actively growing plant that provides dynamic, ongoing benefits.
The [[living-mulch-systems-guide]] page provides a comprehensive overview of living mulch techniques for gardens and farms.

## How Living Mulch Works

Living mulch plants form a dense, low-growing carpet that shades the soil surface and prevents weed seeds from germinating.
This living ground cover is arguably the most effective weed suppression strategy available to organic growers.
The [[comparison-mulch-vs-living-mulch]] page compares the advantages and disadvantages of living versus dead mulch.
Living mulches compete with weeds for light, water, and nutrients, effectively suppressing weed growth without herbicides.
The [[permaculture-plants-ground-covers-living-mulches]] resource covers species selection for different climates and purposes.
Active root systems prevent soil erosion and improve soil structure through continuous biological activity.
The constant root growth and dieback cycle feeds soil microorganisms and creates stable soil aggregates.
Living mulches also moderate soil temperature, keeping roots cooler in summer and insulating against frost in winter.
This temperature moderation can extend the growing season for shallow-rooted crops like lettuce and strawberries.
The [[gaias-garden-living-mulches-ground-covers]] page shows how Toby Hemenway integrates living mulches into food forests.
The key advantage over dead mulch is that living mulch continues to grow and adapt throughout the season.

## Living Mulch vs Cover Crops

Living mulch and cover crops serve overlapping but distinct functions in sustainable agriculture.
The [[comparison-living-mulch-vs-cover-crops]] page explains the important differences between these two strategies.
Cover crops are typically grown alone and terminated before the main crop is planted.
Living mulches grow concurrently with the main crop, providing ongoing benefits throughout the growing season.
Cover crops are usually incorporated into the soil as green manure, while living mulches remain in place.
Some plants like white clover can serve as both cover crop and living mulch depending on how they are managed.
The [[living-mulch-systems-guide]] resource provides management strategies for transitioning between these approaches.
Both approaches build soil organic matter and fix nitrogen, but living mulches do so continuously rather than in discrete pulses.

## Best Species for Living Mulch

White clover is the most popular living mulch for vegetable gardens due to its low growth habit and nitrogen fixing ability.
It tolerates foot traffic, competes well with weeds, and fixes between 50 and 200 pounds of nitrogen per acre annually.
The [[white-clover-living-mulch]] page covers the specifics of using white clover beneath garden crops.
Other excellent living mulch species include creeping thyme, sweet woodruff, Corsican mint, and dwarf perennial ryegrass.
Creeping thyme also provides culinary herbs and attracts pollinators, adding another layer of function.
The [[permaculture-plants-ground-covers-living-mulches]] resource lists species for various climate zones and conditions.
For orchards and food forests, comfrey, yarrow, and creeping juniper make excellent living mulch understories.
The [[mollison-designers-living-mulches-ground-covers]] page covers Mollison's recommendations for tropical and temperate species.
Selection should consider shade tolerance, traffic tolerance, nitrogen fixing capability, and compatibility with main crops.
A good living mulch should be persistent enough to suppress weeds but not so aggressive that it outcompetes your crops.
Low-growing, non-aggressive species are preferred to avoid excessive competition with cash crops.

## Using Living Mulch in No-Till Gardens

Living mulch is a natural fit for no-till gardening systems where soil disturbance is minimized.
The [[comparison-living-mulch-vs-cover-crops]] page shows how living mulch supports no-till soil biology.
A permanent living mulch understory eliminates the need to apply new mulch material each season.
This saves enormous amounts of labor and material costs compared to annually spreading wood chips or straw.
Living mulch roots provide continuous carbon inputs to the soil through root exudates and root turnover.
This constant carbon supply feeds the soil food web and maintains the fungal networks that benefit all plants in the system.
The living root network maintains mycorrhizal connections that are disrupted by tillage and bare fallow periods.
Living mulch works particularly well under tall crops like tomatoes, corn, and fruit trees where light reaches the ground.
These tall crops provide the vertical structure while the living mulch manages the horizontal soil surface.
In dense plantings where the main crop canopy closes completely, shade-tolerant living mulch species are essential.
Dutch white clover, sweet woodruff, and dead nettle all perform reasonably well under partial shade conditions.

## Management Challenges

Living mulch can compete with main crops for water and nutrients if not managed properly.
During establishment, living mulch may need to be cut back or suppressed to give main crops a competitive advantage.
Transplanting main crops through a mature living mulch bed gives them a head start over direct-seeded mulch.
The [[living-mulch-systems-guide]] page provides detailed management strategies for balancing competition.
Some living mulch species can become invasive and difficult to remove if they escape the garden.
Always research the invasive potential of any living mulch species before introducing it to your garden or farm.
Excessive living mulch growth can harbor slugs, snails, and other pests in moist, shaded conditions.
The [[comparison-mulch-vs-living-mulch]] page discusses mitigation strategies for common management problems.
Mowing, crimping, or partial tilling between crop rows can suppress overly aggressive living mulch.
A lawn mower set to the highest setting can quickly knock back an aggressive living mulch without killing it.
Selecting the right species and managing its growth is the key to successful living mulch systems.
With proper species selection and management, living mulch can transform a garden into a self-maintaining, weed-free ecosystem.
