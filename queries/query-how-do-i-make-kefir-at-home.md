# How do I make kefir at home?

## Short Answer

Kefir is a fermented milk drink produced by inocating dairy milk with kefir grains, which are symbiotic colonies of bacteria and yeasts (SCOBY).
The process takes 12 to 24 hours at room temperature and requires no special equipment beyond a glass jar, a breathable cover, and live kefir grains.
You can learn more about the broader category in [[fermented-dairy-and-cultured-milk-products]].

## Detailed Explanation

### What are kefir grains?

Kefir grains are gelatinous, cauliflower-like clusters of lactic acid bacteria, acetic acid bacteria, and yeasts embedded in a polysaccharide matrix called kefiran.
They are not cereal grains — the name is a historical carryover from their appearance.
Each grain is a living ecosystem that propagates itself through successive fermentations, growing in mass over time.
The microbial diversity within grains is covered in detail in [[kefir-and-water-kefir-cultivation]].

### Milk kefir vs water kefir

Milk kefir grains ferment dairy milk and cannot be used interchangeably with water kefir grains.
Water kefir grains (sometimes called tibicos) thrive in sugar water, coconut water, or fruit juice.
Both produce probiotic-rich beverages, but milk kefir has a thicker, yogurt-like consistency while water kefir is lighter and more like a carbonated soda.
The preparation methods differ significantly and are described in [[kefir-making]].

### Equipment and ingredients

You only need a few items to start making kefir at home.

- One to two tablespoons of live milk kefir grains.
- One to two cups of whole dairy milk (pasteurized is fine; avoid ultra-pasteurized).
- A clean glass jar (quart size works well).
- A breathable cover such as a coffee filter, paper towel, or cloth secured with a rubber band.
- A plastic or wooden spoon (metal can damage the grains).
- A fine-mesh plastic strainer for separating grains from finished kefir.

Avoid using metal utensils or containers during fermentation, as the acids produced can react with metal surfaces.

### Step-by-step milk kefir process

1. Place the kefir grains in a clean glass jar.
2. Add milk at a ratio of approximately one tablespoon of grains per cup of milk.
3. Cover the jar with a breathable lid to allow gas exchange while keeping out insects and dust.
4. Leave the jar at room temperature (65 to 78°F or 18 to 26°C) for 12 to 24 hours.
5. Gently swirl or agitate the jar once or twice during fermentation to distribute the grains.
6. When the milk has thickened and tastes tangy, strain out the grains using a plastic strainer.
7. The strained liquid is your finished kefir — refrigerate it immediately.
8. Place the grains back into a clean jar with fresh milk to start the next batch.

The entire cycle repeats daily, making kefir one of the most sustainable fermented foods to maintain at home.

### What about water kefir?

Water kefir follows a similar cycle but uses sugar water instead of milk.
Dissolve approximately one-quarter cup of sugar in one quart of water, add water kefir grains, and ferment for 24 to 48 hours.
You can flavor water kefir with fruit, herbs, or ginger during a secondary fermentation.
The principles of microbial fermentation are shared with [[lacto-fermentation-and-food-preservation-methods]].

### How does kefir fermentation work?

The bacteria in kefir grains convert lactose (milk sugar) into lactic acid, which lowers the pH and causes milk proteins to coagulate.
Yeasts produce small amounts of ethanol and carbon dioxide, contributing to kefir's characteristic slight fizz.
The interplay between these microorganisms is similar to the dynamics described in [[fermentation-microbiology-for-brewers]].
This dual fermentation by bacteria and yeasts distinguishes kefir from yogurt, which relies solely on lactic acid bacteria.

### Factors affecting fermentation speed

Several variables influence how quickly your kefir ferments.

- **Temperature:** Warmer rooms speed up fermentation; cooler rooms slow it down.
- **Grain-to-milk ratio:** More grains relative to milk produces faster, tangier kefir.
- **Milk type:** Whole milk produces thicker kefir; low-fat milk produces thinner results.
- **Agitation:** Swirling the jar distributes grains and speeds fermentation slightly.
- **Fermentation time:** Longer fermentation produces a tangier, more acidic drink.

These factors are consistent with the fermentation principles discussed in [[fermented-foods-and-beverages]].

### Flavoring your homemade kefir

Plain kefir is versatile on its own, but many people enjoy adding flavors after fermentation.
Mix in fresh or frozen fruit, honey, vanilla extract, or herbs like mint and basil after straining the grains.
For a fizzy result, bottle the flavored kefir in a sealed container and leave it at room temperature for a second fermentation of 12 to 24 hours.
This secondary fermentation builds carbonation naturally, similar to the process used in [[ale-vs-lager-fermentation]] for brewing.
Avoid adding flavorings before straining the grains, as sugars and fruit acids can disrupt the grain ecosystem.

### Troubleshooting common kefir problems

Several issues can arise during regular kefir production.
If your kefir separates into thick curds and clear whey, it has over-fermented — shorten the fermentation time or use more milk per tablespoon of grains.
If the kefir tastes excessively yeasty or develops an unpleasant odor, the grains may need a gentle rinse in spring water (never chlorinated tap water).
Grains that stop growing or produce thin, weak kefir may be stressed from extreme temperatures or contaminated jars.
Always use clean, residue-free glass jars to prevent unwanted microbial competition, as emphasized in [[agar-culture-technique]] for any culturing work.

### Storing and caring for kefir grains

Kefir grains are resilient but benefit from consistent care.
If you need a break from daily fermenting, store grains in fresh milk in the refrigerator for up to two weeks.
For longer storage, rinse the grains and dehydrate them at low temperature, then rehydrate when ready to resume.
Grains that are neglected or starved may take several batches to recover full fermentation vigor.

## Key Points

- Kefir grains are a living SCOBY of bacteria and yeasts that ferment milk into a tangy, probiotic-rich drink, as explained in [[kefir-and-water-kefir-cultivation]].
- The basic process requires only grains, milk, a glass jar, and a breathable cover — no special equipment.
- Fermentation takes 12 to 24 hours at room temperature before straining and refrigerating.
- Water kefir is a dairy-free alternative using sugar water and different grains, covered in [[kefir-making]].
- Fermentation speed depends on temperature, grain-to-milk ratio, and milk fat content.
- Kefir is part of a broad family of fermented foods including [[kombucha-brewing]], [[vinegar-making]], and [[miso-making]].
- Grains can be stored in the refrigerator for short breaks or dehydrated for long-term preservation.
