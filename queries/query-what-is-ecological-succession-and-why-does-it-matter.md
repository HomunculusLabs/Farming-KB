# What Is Ecological Succession and Why Does It Matter?

Ecological succession is one of the foundational concepts in ecology,
describing the directional, predictable process by which biological
communities change over time following a disturbance or the creation
of new habitat. Below are answers to the most commonly asked questions
about succession, its mechanisms, and its practical applications.

## What is ecological succession?

Ecological succession is the process by which the species composition
of a biological community changes over time in a more or less
predictable sequence. After a disturbance — whether a volcanic
eruption, glacier retreat, wildfire, or abandonment of agricultural
land — the organisms present gradually shift from early-colonizing,
fast-growing species to more complex, stable assemblages. Succession
operates at multiple scales, from microscopic biofilms on a newly
exposed rock surface to the reforestation of a landscape after a
major fire. The concept was first formalized by Henry Cowles in 1899
and later developed by Frederic Clements, who viewed it as a
superorganism progressing toward a stable endpoint.

## What is the difference between primary and secondary succession?

Primary succession occurs on surfaces where no soil previously existed,
such as newly formed volcanic rock, glacial moraines, or land exposed
by retreating glaciers. Because there is no seed bank or organic
matter, the process begins with the slow weathering of rock and the
arrival of lichens and microbes. Primary succession can take
centuries or millennia to produce mature communities. Secondary
succession, by contrast, occurs on sites where an existing community
has been disturbed — by fire, flooding, logging, or farming — but
where soil and often a seed bank remain intact. Because soil, organic
matter, and residual organisms are already present, secondary
succession proceeds much faster, often restoring forest cover within
decades.

## What are pioneer species and why are they important?

Pioneer species are the first organisms to colonize a barren or
newly disturbed habitat. In primary succession these are typically
lichens, cyanobacteria, and mosses that can survive on bare rock with
minimal nutrients. In secondary succession, pioneer species are often
fast-growing herbaceous plants, grasses, and shrubs that rapidly
cover exposed soil. Pioneers are ecologically critical because they
begin the process of soil formation (in primary succession), fix
atmospheric nitrogen, reduce erosion, moderate microclimatic
conditions, and create organic litter that makes the environment
more hospitable for later species. Without pioneer species, the
successional trajectory could not begin.

## How long does ecological succession take?

The timescale of succession varies enormously depending on the type
of succession, the environment, and the definition of a "final"
community. Primary succession on volcanic lava or glacial till can
take hundreds to thousands of years before a mature forest develops.
Secondary succession is considerably faster; a burned forest may
regenerate its canopy within 50 to 150 years, and grassland recovery
after grazing abandonment can occur in just a few decades. Soil
development, moisture availability, and the regional species pool
are among the key factors that accelerate or slow the process. It
is worth noting that succession is rarely linear — disturbances,
climate variability, and species interactions can reset or alter the
trajectory at any point.

## What is a climax community and is it really the "end point"?

The climax community concept, championed by Clements, posits that
succession culminates in a stable, self-perpetuating community that
remains in equilibrium with the prevailing climate. In this classical
view, a given region has a single climax type — for example,
temperate deciduous forest in eastern North America. Modern ecology
has largely moved away from this rigid idea. The "climax" is now
understood as a dynamic mosaic rather than a fixed endpoint.
Disturbances are frequent enough that many landscapes exist in a
shifting steady state of patches at different successional stages,
a concept known as the "shifting mosaic steady state." Climate change
further complicates the picture, as the environmental conditions that
once defined a climax may no longer exist.

## How does succession relate to climate change?

Climate change is altering successional dynamics worldwide in several
ways. Rising temperatures and shifting precipitation patterns change
which species are suited to colonize disturbed sites, potentially
redirecting successional pathways toward novel community types. More
frequent and intense wildfires can repeatedly reset succession, keeping
some ecosystems locked in early-successional states. Thawing permafrost
is triggering rapid primary succession in formerly frozen areas while
simultaneously releasing stored carbon. In addition, species are
migrating poleward and to higher elevations faster than the communities
they leave behind can reorganize, creating "no-analog" assemblages
with unknown ecological properties. Understanding succession is
therefore essential for predicting ecosystem responses to climate
change.

## How can understanding succession help with ecological restoration?

Ecological restoration — the practice of assisting the recovery of
degraded ecosystems — relies heavily on succession theory. Restoration
practitioners use knowledge of natural successional pathways to decide
which species to introduce, when to introduce them, and what abiotic
conditions to modify. For example, planting nitrogen-fixing pioneer
species can accelerate soil development on degraded mine sites. In
reforestation projects, early-successional species are often planted
first to provide shade and improve soil, creating conditions for
later-successional canopy trees. Assisted natural regeneration
leverages existing successional processes by removing barriers such as
invasive species or grazing pressure, allowing natural succession to
proceed. Succession models also help set realistic timelines and
expectations for restoration outcomes.

## What are the main models of succession (facilitation, tolerance, inhibition)?

Connell and Slatyer (1977) proposed three mechanistic models that
remain the standard framework for understanding successional
dynamics. The **facilitation model** holds that early species modify
the environment in ways that make it more suitable for later species —
for instance, nitrogen-fixing plants enriching soil for subsequent
trees. The **tolerance model** suggests that species replacement is
driven by differences in resource use and competitive ability, not by
direct interactions; later species simply tolerate the conditions
created by earlier ones and eventually outcompete them for light or
nutrients. The **inhibition model** proposes that early occupants
actively resist colonization by later species through competition or
allelopathy, and succession advances only when those early species
are damaged or die. Real ecosystems typically involve a mix of all
three mechanisms operating simultaneously.
