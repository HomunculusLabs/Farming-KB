# What Is Agroforestry and How Can I Start

## Short Answer

Agroforestry is the intentional integration of trees and shrubs with crops and/or livestock on the same land, creating productive systems that generate income from multiple sources while building soil, sequestering carbon, and enhancing biodiversity. Common agroforestry practices include alley cropping, [[mollison-designers-silvopasture-integrated-tree-livestock|silvopasture]], forest farming, riparian buffers, and food forests. These systems can be adapted to any scale from a backyard to a large farm and represent one of the most promising strategies for sustainable food production.

## Why Agroforestry Matters

Agroforestry represents one of the oldest forms of agriculture, yet it has gained renewed attention as modern farming grapples with soil degradation, climate change, and economic instability. Unlike monoculture systems that extract from the land, agroforestry mimics natural ecosystems by stacking productive elements vertically and temporally.

Research shows that agroforestry systems can sequester 2 to 10 times more carbon than equivalent conventional agriculture while producing comparable or greater total yields when all products are counted. The [[holmgren-food-forests-agroforestry|food forests and agroforestry]] framework developed by David Holmgren shows how these systems produce food, fiber, fuel, and forage simultaneously while requiring fewer external inputs.

Economically, agroforestry diversifies income streams. When one crop fails due to weather or market conditions, other products from the same land can compensate. This economic resilience is one of the strongest arguments for transitioning from monoculture to agroforestry, particularly for small-scale farmers and homesteaders who cannot afford the risk of single-crop failure.

## Alley Cropping

Alley cropping is one of the most accessible entry points for new agroforesters. In this system, rows of trees or shrubs are spaced widely enough to allow crops or pasture between them. The tree rows are typically planted on contour for water management benefits.

The [[leucaena-alley-cropping-systems|leucaena alley cropping systems]] demonstrate how nitrogen-fixing trees can provide both fodder and soil fertility for inter-row crops. The tree roots access nutrients from deep soil layers and deposit leaf litter on the surface, cycling minerals into the topsoil where crops can access them.

Common alley cropping combinations include walnuts or chestnuts with annual vegetables, honey locust with grains, and fruit trees with grazing livestock. The key is selecting tree and crop species whose root systems, light requirements, and nutrient needs are complementary rather than competitive. In the early years before tree canopy closes, full-sun crops can be grown between the rows.

## Silvopasture

[[mollison-designers-silvopasture-integrated-tree-livestock|Silvopasture]] integrates trees with pasture and livestock on the same land. This practice provides shade that reduces heat stress on animals, dropped leaves and fruit that supplement forage, and timber or nut crops as a secondary yield from the same acreage.

Silvopasture systems can be established by planting trees into existing pasture or by introducing livestock into established woodlands. The former approach is more common and easier to manage, as it allows you to control tree spacing and species selection from the start. Tree spacing of 30 to 50 feet allows enough light for productive pasture between the rows while providing meaningful shade for livestock.

Compared to open pasture, silvopasture systems typically maintain 80 to 90 percent of pasture productivity while adding tree crops worth $500 to $2,000 per acre annually at maturity. The combined livestock and tree income often exceeds the income from either system alone.

## Forest Farming

Forest farming involves cultivating high-value crops under the canopy of existing forests. This requires the least initial investment of any agroforestry practice because the tree layer already exists. It is particularly well-suited to properties with existing woodlots that are not being actively managed for timber.

Common forest-farmed crops include mushrooms (shiitake, oyster, lion's mane on logs), ginseng and goldenseal as understory medicinal herbs, ramps and fiddlehead ferns as spring vegetables, and maple syrup from sugar maple trees. The [[food-forest-design-guide|food forest design]] principles apply here: select understory crops that match the light levels, soil conditions, and moisture regimes of your specific forest type.

## Riparian Buffers

Riparian buffers are strips of trees and shrubs planted along waterways to filter runoff, stabilize stream banks, and provide wildlife corridors. In agroforestry, these buffers can be designed to produce marketable crops like willow (for basketry or bioenergy), elderberry (for fruit and medicine), and nut trees that tolerate periodic flooding.

Beyond their productive value, riparian buffers provide critical ecosystem services. They filter 85 to 95 percent of nitrogen and phosphorus from agricultural runoff before it reaches waterways, stabilize stream banks against erosion, and provide shade that keeps water temperatures cool enough for fish and aquatic organisms.

## Climate Considerations

Agroforestry practices vary significantly by climate. In tropical regions, multistrata systems with dozens of species stacked in canopy, understory, shrub, herb, and ground layers can produce year-round harvests. In temperate climates, simpler systems with fewer layers are more practical.

Cold-climate agroforesters should focus on hardy nut trees (chestnut, hazelnut, black walnut), fruit trees adapted to their zone, and winter-hardy understory crops like elderberry, currant, and mushroom cultivation on logs. The dormant season limits year-round production but also reduces management demands.

## Economic Planning

Plan your agroforestry system with a 10 to 20 year time horizon. Most tree crops take 5 to 10 years to reach meaningful production, so short-term income from inter-row crops, grazing, or annual vegetable production bridges the gap until tree crops begin bearing.

Keep records of yields, labor hours, and input costs for every component of your system. This data helps you identify which elements are most profitable and which need adjustment. Many agroforestry systems reach peak profitability 15 to 25 years after establishment as tree crops reach full production.

## Tools and Resources

Basic agroforestry requires standard farming tools: a tractor or walk-behind tiller for initial site preparation, planting bars or augers for tree planting, pruning tools, and irrigation equipment for the establishment phase. No specialized equipment is needed for most small to medium-scale systems. Hand tools suffice for plantings under an acre.

Extension services, permaculture design courses, and agroforestry associations provide technical assistance and networking opportunities. Visiting established agroforestry farms in your region is one of the best ways to learn what works in your specific climate and soil conditions.

## Getting Started

Getting started with agroforestry requires careful observation and planning. Begin by mapping your property, identifying existing trees, water flows, and microclimates. Note which areas receive full sun, partial shade, and deep shade at different times of year.

The [[food-forest-plant-palette|food forest plant palette]] and [[forest-garden-seven-layers|seven-layer forest garden model]] provide frameworks for selecting complementary species. Start small with a single practice like a [[backyard-food-forest-design|backyard food forest]] or an alley cropping trial, observe the results over several seasons, and expand gradually.

## Windbreaks and Shelterbelts

Windbreaks are rows of trees and shrubs planted to protect crops, livestock, and buildings from wind damage. In agroforestry, windbreaks are designed to produce useful crops while providing their protective function. Multi-row windbreaks with a mix of evergreen and deciduous species provide year-round protection and diverse yields.

Properly designed windbreaks reduce wind speed for a distance of 10 to 15 times their height. A 30-foot-tall windbreak protects crops up to 300 to 450 feet downwind. This wind reduction decreases evaporation, increases crop yields, and reduces livestock heat stress during summer.

## Key Points

- Agroforestry combines trees with crops and/or livestock for diversified, resilient production
- [[leucaena-alley-cropping-systems|Alley cropping]] spaces tree rows to allow crops between them
- [[mollison-designers-silvopasture-integrated-tree-livestock|Silvopasture]] integrates livestock with tree plantings
- Forest farming grows high-value understory crops beneath existing tree canopy
- Riparian buffers protect waterways while producing useful crops
- Start with observation and mapping, then implement one practice at a manageable scale
- Use [[forest-garden-seven-layers|seven-layer design]] to stack yields vertically
