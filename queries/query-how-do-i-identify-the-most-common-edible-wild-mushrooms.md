# How Do I Identify the Most Common Edible Wild Mushrooms?

## Short Answer
The most common edible wild mushrooms include chanterelles, morels, porcini,
and chicken of the woods. Accurate identification requires careful attention to
cap shape, gill structure, [[comparison-spore-print-collection-vs-spore-syringe-production]] color, habitat, and season. Always
consult the [[mushroom-toxicity-identification-warnings]] before

## Chanterelles: Golden Delicacies of the Forest

Chanterelles, detailed in the [[morel-mushroom-guide]] and are among
the most distinctive mushrooms due to their unique honeycomb-like cap. The cap
is covered with pits and ridges that create a pattern resembling a brain or
wasp nest. When sliced lengthwise, true morels are completely hollow from the
base of the cap through the stem, with the cap being attached directly to the
stem at its base rather than hanging freely.

Morels fruit almost exclusively in spring (March through May, depending on
region) and are often found in areas disturbed by fire, near dead or dying
elm trees, ash, tulip poplar, and old apple orchards. The most dangerous
look-alike is the [[bloomfield-false-morel-gyromitrin-volatile-toxin-cooking-risk]] (Gyromitra esculenta), which has a brain-like,
wrinkled cap that is more irregular and less pitted than a true morel. Most
critically, false morels are solid or chambered inside rather than hollow.
The  covers boletes, which are identified by their
distinctive pored underside rather than gills. Instead of blade-like gills,
boletes have a spongy surface of tiny tubes (pores) on the underside of the
cap. Porcini (Boletus edulis) are the most prized members of this group,
featuring a brown cap that can range from pale tan to deep reddish-brown, a
thick solid white stem that is often club-shaped and slightly bulbous at the
base, and a mild, nutty flavor prized in Italian and French cuisine.

Porcini are mycorrhizal associates of conifers and some hardwoods, particularly
spruce, pine, and birch. They fruit from late summer through autumn and are
found in both coniferous and mixed forests. When identifying boletes for the
table, always check that the flesh does not turn vivid blue when cut and verify
that there is no bright red or orange coloration on the pore surface, which can
indicate toxic species like Boletus satanas (the devil's bolete). The pore
surface of edible porcini starts white, ages to yellow-green, and should never
be vividly colored.

## Key Points
- Chanterelles have false gills, golden color, and apricot aroma; watch for
  false chanterelle look-alikes with true gills and no apricot scent
- Morels must be completely hollow when sliced; false morels are solid or
  chambered and contain the dangerous toxin gyromitrin
- Boletes have pores instead of gills; avoid any with vivid red or orange
  pore surfaces and check for blue-staining reactions when cut
- Chicken of the woods grows as bright orange shelves on hardwood trees; never
  harvest from conifer hosts to avoid gastrointestinal issues
- Always follow [[mushroom-toxicity-identification-warnings]] before consuming any wild
  mushroom species
