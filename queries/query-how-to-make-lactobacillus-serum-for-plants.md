# How to Make Lactobacillus Serum for Plants
## Short Answer
Lactobacillus serum (LAB serum) is a natural probiotic culture made by fermenting rice wash water with milk to selectively breed Lactobacillus bacteria. It is a core input in Korean Natural Farming and JADAM methods, used as a soil inoculant, compost accelerator, foliar spray, and odor suppressant. Making it at home costs nearly nothing using just rice, water, milk, and a glass jar.
## Detailed Explanation
### What Is Lactobacillus Serum?
Lactobacillus serum is one of the most versatile inputs in natural farming systems. Lactobacillus bacteria are lactic acid bacteria (LAB) that produce lactic acid as a metabolic byproduct. This acid lowers pH, suppresses harmful microorganisms, breaks down organic matter, and makes nutrients more available to plants.
In Korean Natural Farming, LAB serum is considered essential for maintaining soil health. It is used in virtually every aspect of crop production, from seed treatment to post-harvest storage. For a full overview of how it fits into a broader fertility program, see the [[bionutrients-guide]].
LAB bacteria are naturally present on the surfaces of grains, in soil, and in the digestive tracts of animals. They are among the most widespread and beneficial microorganisms in agricultural systems worldwide.
The rice-and-milk fermentation method simply creates conditions where these bacteria outcompete all others, giving you a concentrated, pure culture that can be applied wherever beneficial microbes are needed.
### Why Use Lactobacillus Serum?
There are several compelling reasons to incorporate LAB serum into your growing practice. Once you master the basic technique, you will find yourself reaching for it constantly throughout the growing season.
- **Cost effective:** The ingredients cost pennies per batch, making it one of the cheapest biological inputs available.
- **Easy to produce:** No special equipment or sterile laboratory conditions are required.
- **Broad spectrum:** It benefits soil, plants, compost, and animal systems simultaneously.
- **Proven results:** Decades of use in Korean Natural Farming demonstrate consistent improvements in crop health and yield.
- **Multipurpose:** A single batch can serve soil, foliar, compost, and animal applications simultaneously.
### Materials Needed
You only need basic kitchen supplies to make LAB serum at home. Everything is inexpensive and widely available at any grocery store.
| Item | Quantity | Notes |
|------|----------|-------|
| White rice | 1 cup | Any standard white rice; brown works poorly |
| Water | 2-3 cups | Non-chlorinated is best |
| Whole milk | 10 cups | Pasteurized, not ultra-pasteurized |
| Glass jar | 1 large | 1 gallon or larger recommended |
| Cloth or paper towel | 1 piece | For covering the jar loosely |
| Straining cloth or coffee filter | 1 piece | For final serum separation |
### Stage 1: Ferment the Rice Wash Water
The fermentation process exploits the fact that Lactobacillus bacteria are present on all grains, including rice. The starchy wash water serves as a growth medium that supports a wide range of microbes before we narrow the population down to LAB in stage two.
Follow these steps to create the initial diverse microbial culture:
1. **Wash the rice.** Place one cup of white rice in a bowl and wash it thoroughly in two to three cups of water. Agitate vigorously with your hands until the water becomes cloudy and starchy.
2. **Collect the wash water.** Pour the cloudy rice wash water into your glass jar. The rice itself can be cooked and eaten normally. The starchy liquid provides sugars that feed the diverse microorganisms living on the rice grain surface.
3. **Cover and wait.** Cover the jar loosely with a cloth or paper towel secured with a rubber band. This allows gas exchange while keeping out dust and insects. Place it in a warm spot at room temperature (70-80°F / 21-27°C).
4. **Ferment for 2-3 days.** During this time, bacteria and yeasts from the rice grain multiply in the sugary liquid. The mixture will develop a slightly sour smell and may form a thin foam on the surface. It is ready when it smells distinctly sour, similar to a weak vinegar.
### Stage 2: Selective Milk Fermentation
The second step selectively isolates Lactobacillus by adding milk to the fermented rice wash. Milk contains lactose sugar, which Lactobacillus bacteria preferentially ferment while most other microorganisms cannot utilize it.
1. **Combine with milk.** Add milk at a ratio of roughly one part fermented rice wash to ten parts milk. For example, add one cup of rice wash liquid to ten cups of milk. Stir gently to combine without introducing too much air.
2. **Choose the right milk.** Fresh raw milk works best, but pasteurized whole milk also produces good results. Do not use ultra-pasteurized (UHT) milk, as the extreme heat treatment destroys beneficial bacteria and enzymes needed for proper fermentation.
3. **Cover and ferment.** Cover loosely again and leave at room temperature for five to seven days. Do not stir or disturb the jar during this phase. The bacteria need still conditions to form clean layers.
4. **Watch for separation.** The mixture will separate into three distinct layers:
   - **Top layer:** solid curd of milk proteins and fats
   - **Middle layer:** clear yellowish liquid (this is the serum)
   - **Bottom layer:** sediment and debris
### Common Problems and Troubleshooting
Even a simple fermentation can sometimes go wrong. Here are the most common issues growers encounter and how to address them:
- **No separation after 7 days:** The room may be too cold. Move the jar to a warmer location (70-80°F) and wait a few more days.
- **Foul or rotten smell:** Contamination likely occurred. Discard the batch and start over with cleaner jars and fresh ingredients.
- **Mold on the surface:** A small amount of white mold is usually harmless, but colored mold (green, black, pink) means the batch is compromised and should be discarded.
- **Very little serum yield:** This can happen if the rice wash was too dilute. Use less water next time to create a starchier wash.
- **Curd does not form solidly:** The milk may have been too old or ultra-pasteurized. Always check the label and use the freshest milk available.
### Harvesting the Serum
The clear yellow liquid between the curd and sediment is the lactobacillus serum, rich in LAB populations and lactic acid. Harvest it carefully to avoid contamination from the other layers.
- Skim off and discard the solid curd layer from the top.
- Carefully pour off the clear yellow serum, leaving the sediment behind.
- Strain the serum through a fine cloth or coffee filter for additional clarity.
- Transfer the finished serum to a clean glass jar for storage.
The [[bionutrients-lab-lactic-acid-bacteria-serum]] guide provides detailed ratios and troubleshooting tips for each stage of this process.
### Storage and Shelf Life
Proper storage keeps your LAB serum viable for months or longer. Glass containers are strongly preferred over plastic, which can leach chemicals and is less airtight.
- **Room temperature:** store in a sealed glass jar for up to 6 months.
- **Refrigerated:** extends shelf life to approximately one year.
- **Avoid direct sunlight:** UV light kills beneficial bacteria over time.
- **Check for spoilage:** discard if the serum develops a putrid or rotten smell rather than a clean sour aroma.
A good batch of LAB serum will smell tangy and pleasant, like yogurt or weak pickling brine. The color should be pale yellow to amber. If it smells rotten or like vomit, something went wrong during fermentation and you should start over.
### Application Rates
Dilution rates depend on how you plan to use the serum. These are starting points; observe your plants and adjust as needed for your specific conditions.
| Application | Dilution Ratio | Frequency |
|-------------|---------------|-----------|
| Soil drench | 1:20 (serum to water) | Every 1-2 weeks |
| Foliar spray | 1:1000 (serum to water) | Every 2-3 weeks |
| Compost accelerator | 1:50 (serum to water) | As needed when turning |
| Odor control | 1:100 (serum to water) | Spray manure piles weekly |
For foliar application, spray in the early morning or late evening to avoid leaf burn. Adding a small amount of surfactant (a few drops of mild soap) can help the serum spread evenly across leaf surfaces.
The [[beneficial-indigenous-microorganisms-bim]] page explains how LAB serum can be combined with indigenous microorganism (IMO) preparations for enhanced soil inoculation.
### How LAB Serum Fits Into Natural Farming
LAB serum is not a standalone product. It works best as part of an integrated system of biological inputs that support plant and soil health holistically.
- **As a compost accelerator:** Spraying diluted serum onto compost piles speeds decomposition by boosting bacterial populations and lowering pH to favorable levels. This complements vermicomposting methods described in [[query-how-to-start-a-worm-compost-bin]].
- **As an odor control agent:** It suppresses ammonia-producing bacteria and reduces foul smells in manure piles and livestock operations. Many small-scale livestock keepers spray pens weekly.
- **As a plant health tonic:** Foliar applications populate leaf surfaces with beneficial bacteria that compete with pathogens for space and nutrients, creating a protective biofilm.
- **In bokashi systems:** LAB serum is an essential ingredient in the bran-based inoculant that ferments kitchen scraps anaerobically. See the [[bokashi-compost-method]] for full instructions on preparing bokashi bran with LAB serum.

For a broader comparison of farming approaches that use LAB serum, see [[query-jadam-vs-korean-natural-farming]] and [[query-how-to-make-korean-natural-farming-inputs]].
## Key Points
- LAB serum is made in two stages: ferment rice wash water for 2-3 days, then add milk and ferment 5-7 more days
- The clear yellow liquid that separates from the milk curd is the usable serum
- Dilute 1:20 for soil drenching and 1:1000 for foliar spraying
- Use pasteurized whole milk but never ultra-pasteurized, which lacks needed enzymes
- Store in glass at room temperature for months or refrigerate for up to a year
- Functions as compost accelerator, odor suppressant, soil inoculant, and foliar tonic
- Raw milk produces the best results, but standard pasteurized milk works well too
- Always strain the final serum and discard the curd and sediment layers
