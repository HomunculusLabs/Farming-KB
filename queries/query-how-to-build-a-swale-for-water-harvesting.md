# How to Build a Swale for Water Harvesting
## Short Answer
A swale is a level ditch-and-berm system built on contour across a slope to capture, slow, and infiltrate surface water runoff into the soil. Building one involves marking contour lines with an A-frame level, digging a shallow ditch on contour, and piling the excavated soil on the downhill side to form a berm that holds water until it soaks into the ground.
## Key Points
- Swales are passive water harvesting earthworks that capture runoff and recharge groundwater
- They must be built perfectly level on contour to prevent dangerous water concentration at one end
- An A-frame level is the simplest tool for marking accurate contour lines across a slope
- Swales work best on slopes between 2 and 15 percent grade
- Typical dimensions are 1 to 3 feet deep and 2 to 4 feet wide, scaled to catchment area
- The berm should be compacted and planted immediately with deep-rooted perennials
- Swales concentrate water infiltration at specific points, unlike keyline design which distributes broadly
- They integrate well with other water harvesting elements like ponds, rain tanks, and greywater systems
## Detailed Explanation
### What Is a Swale?
Swales are foundational earthworks in [[query-what-are-the-permaculture-principles]], serving as passive water harvesting systems that transform runoff into groundwater recharge. Unlike drainage ditches that channel water away, swales are specifically designed to stop water flow, spread it across the landscape, and sink it into the soil profile.
A swale consists of two components working together. The first is a level ditch (the swale itself) excavated along a contour line. The second is a berm (mound) formed from the excavated soil placed on the downhill side.
When rain falls, water flows downhill, hits the swale, and pools in the level ditch. The water then slowly percolates into the soil over hours or days, recharging groundwater and making moisture available to plants on the berm and surrounding area long after the rain event has passed.
This passive system requires no pumps, pipes, or mechanical parts. Once constructed, a swale works continuously through every rainfall event, gradually improving soil moisture levels and reducing the need for supplemental irrigation over time.
### When Are Swales Appropriate?
Not every site is suitable for swale construction. Slope gradient is the most important factor to evaluate before planning your earthworks.
| Slope Gradient | Suitability | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Less than 2% | Poor | Water moves too slowly; minimal runoff to capture |
| 2% to 15% | Ideal | Effective capture and infiltration without blowout risk |
| Greater than 15% | Dangerous | High water velocity can overwhelm and breach the berm |
Walk the site during and after rainstorms to observe existing water flow patterns, identify erosion channels, and determine where water naturally concentrates. The [[faires-swales-and-water-conservation]] guide recommends installing the first swale near the top of the slope.
Additional swales should be spaced at vertical intervals determined by three key factors:
- **Soil type** — sandy soils infiltrate quickly, allowing closer spacing
- **Slope gradient** — steeper slopes need closer spacing to catch water before it gains speed
- **Expected rainfall intensity** — areas with heavy rainfall events need more swales
### Tools and Materials
Gathering the right tools before starting will make the construction process smoother and more accurate. Most of these items are readily available or can be fabricated on site.
1. **A-frame level** — for marking contour lines (can be built from scrap lumber)
2. **Laser level or water tube level** — alternatives for larger properties
3. **Measuring tape and marking flags or stakes** — for laying out the contour line
4. **Shovel, mattock, or excavator** — depending on swale size and scale
5. **Wheelbarrow** — for moving excavated soil to the berm
The [[swale-design-construction]] provides detailed instructions for constructing and using an A-frame level, as well as guidance on choosing the right excavation method for your property scale.
### Step-by-Step Construction
#### Step 1: Mark the Contour Line
Marking contour lines accurately is essential because a swale must be perfectly level to function properly. If the swale slopes even slightly, water will flow to one end, accumulate, and potentially overflow or breach the berm.
The simplest tool for finding contour lines is an A-frame level, a triangular frame with a plumb bob or spirit level attached. Place the A-frame legs on the ground, adjust until level, mark two points at the same elevation, then leapfrog the frame along the slope to trace a continuous level line.
Mark the line clearly with flags or stakes so you can reference it during excavation. Double-check your work by walking the line and looking for any obvious deviations.
#### Step 2: Excavate the Ditch
Dig the ditch along the marked contour line. A typical swale for a home garden or small homestead is 1 to 3 feet deep and 2 to 4 feet wide, though dimensions should be scaled to the catchment area above and expected rainfall volumes.
For larger properties, a small excavator or walk-behind trencher can speed the work considerably. However, hand-digging gives you more control over the shape and depth, and it compacts the surrounding soil less than heavy machinery.
If using machinery, take care not to scrape the subsoil too smooth. A rough bottom surface helps slow water movement and promotes infiltration rather than creating a channel that directs flow.
#### Step 3: Build and Compact the Berm
Place all excavated soil on the downhill side to form the berm. Compact it by walking on it or tamping to prevent erosion. The [[gaias-garden-swales-and-berms-construction]] guide emphasizes that the berm is just as important as the ditch.
Shape the berm with a slight rounded crown to shed water that overtops it during extreme rainfall events. A flat-topped berm can allow water to pond on top and erode through.
Avoid placing rocks, roots, or debris in the berm, as these create weak points where water can channel through during heavy flows.
#### Step 4: Plant Immediately
The berm should be planted immediately with deep-rooted perennial plants, fruit trees, or berry bushes. These plants will stabilize the soil and benefit from the consistent moisture that the swale provides.
Bare soil on a newly built berm is highly vulnerable to erosion from the first rainfall. Even a temporary cover crop of annual grasses can protect the berm while perennial plants establish.
Good berm planting choices include:
- **Fruit trees** — apple, pear, plum, and citrus thrive on the well-drained berm shoulder
- **Nitrogen fixers** — leguminous trees and shrubs improve soil fertility over time
- **Dynamic accumulators** — comfrey and yarrow mine deep minerals and cycle them to the surface
- **Ground covers** — clover, vetch, or creeping thyme protect the soil surface from erosion
### Swales vs. Keyline Design
Swales differ from keyline design in their approach to water management. Understanding the distinction helps you choose the right technique for your property.
| Feature | Swales | Keyline Design |
|---|---|---|
| Placement | Exact contour lines | Parallel to keypoint contours |
| Water strategy | Stop and sink at specific points | Distribute from valleys to ridges |
| Best scale | Small to medium properties | Large agricultural properties |
| Equipment | Hand tools or small machinery | Subsoiler or keyline plow |
| Disturbance | Moderate soil excavation | Minimal soil disturbance |
The [[comparison-keyline-vs-swale]] page explains that swales are better suited for smaller properties with defined slopes where concentrated water infiltration is desired. Keyline design works best on larger agricultural properties with varied terrain.
For more on the keyline approach, see [[query-how-does-keyline-design-work-in-permaculture]].
### Integrating with a Broader Water Strategy
Swales rarely exist in isolation on a well-designed property. They work best as part of a comprehensive water management plan that captures, stores, and distributes water across multiple systems.
- **Ponds** — can be positioned below swale outflow points to store excess water
- **Rain tanks** — capture roof runoff for supplemental irrigation during dry periods
- **Greywater systems** — direct household wastewater into mulch basins near swale plantings
- **Gabions and check dams** — slow water in drainage channels above swale systems
The [[water-catchment-permaculture]] resource describes how swales can be integrated with these and other water harvesting elements for a full property-scale water management plan.
### Planting the Swale System
Planting the berm and swale bottom transforms the earthwork into a productive growing system. The berm, being well-drained and elevated, is ideal for fruit trees, perennial vegetables, and herbs.
The swale bottom, which stays moist longer, supports moisture-loving plants like comfrey, berry bushes, and willow. These plants help filter any sediment that accumulates in the ditch and prevent clogging.
Avoid planting large trees directly in the swale bottom, as their roots can eventually block water flow and compromise the structure. Instead, place trees on the berm crest or just below it where they can access moisture without interfering with the ditch.
The [[swale-design]] page recommends planting a dense polyculture of complementary species on the berm to maximize the productive use of the harvested water while root systems stabilize the earthwork against erosion.

### Common Mistakes to Avoid
- **Building off contour** — even a slight slope causes water to concentrate and overflow at one end
- **Skipping the berm** — without a berm, water simply flows through the ditch and continues downhill
- **Not planting immediately** — bare berms erode quickly and undermine the entire structure
- **Over-sizing for the catchment** — a swale that is too large may never fill, wasting effort and space
- **Under-sizing for heavy rain** — a swale that is too small will overflow and potentially cause erosion damage
- **Ignoring existing drainage patterns** — building a swale across a natural watercourse can cause failure during large storms
Always test your design with a moderate rainfall event before relying on it during extreme weather.
