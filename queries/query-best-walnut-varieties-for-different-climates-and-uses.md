# What Are the Best Walnut Varieties for Different Climates and Uses?

## 1. Best English/Persian Walnut Varieties for Mild/Warm Climates (Zone 8–10)

The mild-winter, long-summer climates of USDA zones 8–10 are ideal for English (Persian) walnut cultivation. These regions encompass California's Central Valley, the Mediterranean basin, parts of the southeastern U.S., and similar climates in Australia, Chile, and South Africa.

**Chandler** is the dominant commercial variety for California and Mediterranean climates worldwide. It offers excellent yield potential, late leafing (which helps avoid spring frost damage), and good resistance to walnut blight. Its kernels are light-colored and plump, commanding premium prices in both domestic and export markets. **Howard** and **Tulare** are close runners-up, both well-suited to USDA zones 8–10 with high-quality kernels and reliable production year over year.

**Serr** performs well in warmer inland valleys thanks to its lower chill requirement (~500 hours), making it a good choice for areas with milder winters where other varieties may not receive adequate dormancy. **Vina** thrives in California's Central Valley and similar climates, producing large, flavorful nuts with a slightly rounded shell. **Hartley** is a legacy variety still found in many older California orchards — it produces a large, round nut with good flavor but is earlier-leafing and more blight-susceptible than newer cultivars. All Mediterranean-climate varieties require long, hot growing seasons (180+ frost-free days) for optimal kernel fill and oil development.

**Franquette**, a traditional French variety, remains commercially important in France, Italy, and other European walnut-growing regions. It has an exceptionally high-quality kernel but is very late-leafing and sometimes fails to ripen in shorter-season climates.

## 2. Most Cold-Hardy Varieties for Colder Regions (Zone 5–7)

Growing walnuts in cold climates requires careful variety selection, as most English walnuts suffer significant winter injury below −15°F.

**Chandler** accounts for the vast majority of new commercial walnut plantings in California and is increasingly adopted worldwide, including in Chile, Australia, and Eastern Europe. It is prized for its high yield potential (3,500–5,000 lbs/acre at maturity), late bloom date, thin shell, and excellent light kernel color that meets the highest industry "extra light" grade. **Howard** and **Tulare** complement Chandler in commercial orchards as pollinizers while also being strong producers in their own right.

**Vina** and **Serr** remain common in established California orchards but are rarely planted in new developments due to susceptibility to blight and lower kernel quality relative to newer selections. **Franquette**, a traditional French variety, is still commercially important in France and other European walnut-growing regions where it is valued for its oil quality and traditional market appeal.

UC Davis recommends commercial spacing at approximately 30×30 feet on paradox rootstock (*J. hindsii × J. regia*), with Chandler as the primary variety and Howard or Tulare as the pollinizer at roughly a 9:1 ratio to ensure adequate pollen distribution. Trees typically begin bearing at 4–5 years and reach full production by year 10–12. High-density plantings (e.g., 22×22 feet with eventual tree removal) are also being explored to accelerate early returns.

## 8. Pollination Considerations

Pollination is one of the most commonly overlooked factors in walnut variety selection, and getting it wrong can result in poor or absent crops even with otherwise healthy trees.

English walnuts are **dichogamous** — male catkins (pollen) and female flowers (pistils) mature at different times on the same tree. They are classified as either **protandrous** (pollen sheds before female flowers are receptive) or **protogynous** (female flowers are receptive before pollen sheds). **Chandler** and **Serr** are protogynous; **Howard** and **Tulare** are protandrous. This complementary timing is why commercial orchards deliberately interplant these types — a protandrous variety releases pollen just as a protogynous variety's flowers become receptive, and vice versa.

Most English walnuts are partially self-fruitful but yield 20–40% more with a compatible cross-pollinator. Wind is the primary pollen vector, so orchard layout and prevailing spring wind directions should be considered when placing pollinizer rows. **Black walnuts** are mostly wind-pollinated and generally self-compatible, though outcrossing improves nut set and kernel fill. **Heartnuts** are self-fruitful but benefit from cross-pollination for heavier, more reliable crops.

For home gardeners planting a single tree, **Chandler**, **Pedro**, or **Heartnut** are the best choices since they produce the most reliable crops without a second tree, though adding a compatible pollinator is always recommended for optimal production.

## 9. Butternut Varieties and Their Uses

Butternut (*Juglans cinerea*), also called white walnut, produces rich, buttery-flavored nuts that are prized for baking, candy-making, and confectionery. The oil content is high (~60%) and the flavor is distinctly sweeter and more complex than that of black walnut, with none of the astringency. However, wild butternut is threatened by **butternut canker** (*Ophiognomonia clavigignenti-juglandacearum*), a fatal fungal disease that has devastated native populations across eastern North America since its discovery in 1967. The species is now listed as endangered in many states.

There are few named cultivars in commercial trade due to the species' threatened status. **Craxezy** and **Kenworthy** are selections from canker-resistant parent trees and show some tolerance to the disease, though they remain difficult to source. **Buartnut** (butternut × heartnut hybrid) is an increasingly popular choice for northern growers — it combines the rich butternut flavor with heartnut's superior cold hardiness, compact habit, and resistance to butternut canker. Several named buartnut cultivars exist, including **Buartnut 1** and **Mitchell**. Butternut wood is also valued for woodworking, carving, and fine furniture due to its light color, satin-like luster, and excellent workability.

## Rootstock Considerations

Rootstock choice significantly affects tree performance, disease resistance, and longevity. **Paradox rootstock** (*Juglans hindsii × J. regia*) is the standard for commercial English walnut orchards in California — it provides vigorous growth, tolerance to wet soils, and resistance to crown gall and nematodes. **Northern California black walnut** (*J. hindsii*) rootstock was historically common but is being phased out due to susceptibility to the walnut twig beetle and thousand cankers disease complex.

For colder climates, **Juglans nigra** (black walnut) rootstock provides excellent cold hardiness and deep, well-anchored root systems, making it the preferred choice for Carpathian walnut and heartnut grafting in zones 5–6. **J. regia seedling** rootstock is used in areas where paradox is not well-adapted; it produces smaller, slower-growing trees but is more cold-hardy than paradox.

Home growers should ensure their chosen rootstock is compatible with their intended scion variety and matched to local soil conditions (drainage, pH, nematode pressure). Consult your local extension office for rootstock recommendations specific to your region.
