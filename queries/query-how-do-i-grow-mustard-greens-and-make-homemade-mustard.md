# How Do I Grow Mustard Greens and Make Homemade Mustard?

## Short Answer

Mustard is a versatile [[brassica-juncea]] crop that delivers both flavorful greens and pungent seeds for homemade condiment making.
The plant thrives in cool weather and matures quickly, making it one of the easiest brassicas to grow in home gardens.
You can harvest tender leaves for salads and stir-fries within 30 days, then let mature plants produce seeds for grinding into prepared mustard.
The same glucosinolate compounds that give mustard its signature bite also make it a powerful [[biofumigation-and-brassica-cover-crops-for-pest-management]] ally in crop rotation.
Growing your own mustard closes the loop from garden to kitchen in a single season.

## What Varieties Should I Grow?

Mustard belongs to several species within the Brassicaceae family, each with distinct culinary uses.
[[brassica-juncea]] (brown mustard) is the most common species for both greens and seed production, producing fiery leaves and the pungent seeds used in Dijon-style mustards.
[[sinapis-alba]] (white mustard) produces milder, yellow seeds ideal for classic yellow mustard and pickling spice blends.
[[sinapis-arvensis]] (charlock or wild mustard) grows aggressively as a volunteer and produces sharp, spicy seeds favored in whole-grain mustard recipes.
The [[brassica-gardening-guide]] covers variety selection in depth, recommending Asian types like Mizuna and Red Giant for greens and Brown 649 for seed production.
For a dual-purpose harvest, choose a [[brassica-juncea]] variety bred for both leaf quality and seed yield.

## How Do I Grow Mustard Greens?

Mustard greens are among the fastest crops you can grow, reaching harvest size in just 25 to 40 days from sowing.
Direct-sow seeds ¼ inch deep in early spring or late summer, spacing rows 12 to 18 inches apart.
The [[growing-spinach-and-mustard-greens]] guide recommends succession planting every two weeks for a continuous supply of tender young leaves.
Mustard prefers full sun and well-drained soil with a pH between 6.0 and 7.5, consistent with the general [[brassica-growing-guide]] recommendations.
Keep the soil consistently moist but not waterlogged, as drought stress causes bitterness in the leaves.
Flea beetles and aphids are the primary pests, which you can manage using row covers or the organic techniques in [[growing-brassicas-organically]].
Harvest outer leaves first to encourage continued growth, or cut the entire plant when it reaches 6 to 8 inches tall.

## How Do I Grow Mustard for Seed?

If your goal is homemade mustard condiment, you need mature seed pods rather than tender leaves.
Allow a portion of your mustard crop to bolt and flower, which happens naturally as temperatures warm in late spring.
The [[solomon-growing-brassicas-guide]] notes that mustard seed matures 90 to 120 days after sowing, depending on variety and climate.
Yellow flowers give way to thin seed pods that turn from green to tan to brown as they dry on the stalk.
Harvest seed stalks when the lower pods begin to split, then hang them upside down in paper bags to finish drying.
Each pod contains 6 to 12 seeds — a single healthy [[brassica-juncea]] plant can yield several tablespoons of seed.
Store dried seeds in airtight glass jars away from light and heat until you are ready to make mustard.

## How Do I Make Homemade Prepared Mustard?

The basic process for making mustard is remarkably simple: soak seeds, grind them, and mix with liquid and seasonings.
The [[food-fermentation-guide]] covers the principles of enzymatic breakdown that give prepared mustard its characteristic flavor and heat.
Start by soaking whole mustard seeds in water, vinegar, or wine for 24 to 48 hours to soften the seed coat and activate myrosinase enzymes.
These enzymes convert glucosinolates into isothiocyanates — the pungent compounds responsible for mustard's sinus-clearing bite.
Grind the soaked seeds to your preferred texture: coarse for whole-grain mustard, smooth for Dijon-style, or somewhere in between.
Add [[vinegar-making]] quality vinegar or wine as the primary liquid, along with salt and any aromatics like garlic, tarragon, or honey.
Freshly made mustard tastes extremely bitter at first — it needs to rest in the refrigerator for 3 to 7 days while the flavors mellow and blend.

## What About Fermented Mustard?

Fermented mustard uses [[lacto-fermentation-and-food-preservation-methods]] instead of vinegar to achieve its tangy profile.
Pack ground mustard seeds with a small amount of salt and water into a jar, then let [[lactobacillus]] bacteria produce lactic acid over several days.
The [[fermented-vegetables-guide]] describes the same technique used for sauerkraut and kimchi, applied here to a condiment.
Fermented mustard has a deeper, more complex flavor than vinegar-based versions, with beneficial probiotic bacteria intact.
A 2% salt concentration is sufficient for mustard fermentation because the natural antimicrobial properties of mustard seed help prevent spoilage.
The [[fermented-foods-and-beverages]] overview notes that fermented mustards keep well in the refrigerator for several months.

## How Does Mustard Benefit My Garden Beyond Food?

Mustard is not just a food crop — it is a workhorse in the sustainable garden.
The glucosinolate-rich plant tissues, when chopped and incorporated into soil, release compounds that suppress soil-borne pathogens and nematodes.
The [[biofumigation-and-brassica-cover-crops-for-pest-management]] topic explains how mustard cover crops reduce wireworm, root-knot nematode, and Fusarium populations before cash crops are planted.

Mustard also attracts beneficial insects during its flowering stage, serving as an early-season nectar source for pollinators and predatory wasps.

## What About Cooking with Mustard Greens?

Mustard greens are a staple in Southern American, Chinese, and Indian cuisines, each with distinct preparation traditions.
Young raw leaves add a peppery kick to salad mixes and pair well with sweet dressings that balance their natural heat.
The [[growing-spinach-and-mustard-greens]] guide notes that cooking mellows the pungency significantly — a quick sauté with garlic and olive oil transforms sharp greens into a silky side dish.
In Chinese cooking, mustard greens are often pickled or braised with pork, while Indian recipes use them in sarson ka saag with cornmeal bread.
Nutritionally, mustard greens are among the most vitamin-dense vegetables available, rich in vitamins K, A, and C plus cancer-fighting glucosinolates.
A light frost actually improves the flavor of fall-planted mustard greens by converting starches to sugars, making late-season harvests the sweetest.

## What About Mustard in Crop Rotation?

Mustard fits naturally into brassica-based rotation plans but requires careful sequencing to avoid disease buildup.
The [[brassica-gardening-guide]] recommends waiting three to four years between brassica plantings in the same bed to prevent clubroot and black rot.
A smart rotation uses mustard as a biofumigant cover crop before a non-brassica vegetable like tomatoes or beans, capturing both soil-cleansing and nutrient-building benefits.
After mustard biofumigation, wait two to three weeks before planting the next crop to allow the glucosinolate breakdown products to dissipate fully.
The [[comparison-french-marigold-vs-mustard-biofumigation]] analysis shows that alternating mustard with marigold cover crops provides broader-spectrum soil pest suppression than either used alone.
Mustard's deep taproot also breaks up compacted subsoil layers, bringing minerals to the surface for subsequent shallow-rooted crops.

## What Can Go Wrong?

Mustard is generally easy to grow, but a few common problems can reduce your harvest.
Bolting in warm weather is the most frequent complaint — plant early and choose slow-bolting varieties for the longest leaf harvest.
White rust and downy mildew can affect fall crops in humid climates; proper spacing and air circulation minimize these fungal diseases.
Cabbage worms and loopers feed on mustard leaves just as they do on other brassicas, treatable with Bt spray or floating row covers.
If your homemade mustard tastes excessively bitter, extend the resting period in the refrigerator — the harshness fades over the first week.
Seeds that fail to germinate usually indicate soil that is too warm; mustard germinates best below 75°F (24°C).
