# How Do You Make Effective Microorganisms (EM) for Agriculture

## Short Answer
[[effective-microorganisms-em|Effective Microorganisms (EM)]] is a
commercial inoculant developed by Teruo Higa containing lactic acid
bacteria, photosynthetic bacteria, and yeast that can be multiplied at
home using molasses and water. While buying the mother culture is the
most reliable approach, gardeners can also create similar microbial
inoculants from scratch using [[indigenous-microorganisms-imo|indigenous microorganism (IMO)]] collection methods from Korean Natural Farming or
[[jadam-microorganism-solution-jms|JADAM microorganism solution (JMS)]]
techniques, which capture and culture locally adapted microbes.

## Detailed Explanation
Effective Microorganisms, commonly called EM, is a proprietary blend of
beneficial microorganisms first developed by Japanese horticulturist
Teruo Higa in the 1980s. The standard
[[effective-microorganisms-em|EM formulation]] contains three main groups
of organisms: lactic acid bacteria such as [[lactobacillus|Lactobacillus]]
species, yeast such as Saccharomyces cerevisiae, and photosynthetic
bacteria. These three groups work synergistically to suppress pathogens,
cycle nutrients, and improve soil conditions.

To multiply EM at home, you start with a purchased bottle of EM-1 mother
culture. The activation process involves mixing 1 part EM-1 with 1 part
unsulfured blackstrap molasses and 20 parts dechlorinated water in a
clean container. The mixture is fermented anaerobically at approximately
95 to 100 degrees Fahrenheit for 7 to 10 days. During fermentation, the
pH drops from around 6.5 to below 3.8, indicating successful activation.
The resulting liquid, called EM-A or activated EM, can be stored for
several months and diluted at various rates for different applications.
This process is similar to making lactobacillus serum, which is
essentially a simplified version using just lactobacillus bacteria
captured from rice wash water.

For gardeners who prefer a completely free, locally sourced approach,
[[indigenous-microorganisms-imo|IMO from Korean Natural Farming]] offers
an excellent alternative. The IMO method, detailed in resources on
[[cho-imo-collection-bamboo-stump-and-paddy-field|IMO collection methods]], involves placing cooked rice in a natural environment to
capture local microorganisms, then culturing them on a wheat bran
substrate with added sugar. This multi-stage process produces a powerful
microbial inoculant adapted to local conditions, which some practitioners
argue is more effective than imported commercial cultures. The
[[comparison-jadam-vs-imo-microorganism-solutions|comparison between JADAM and IMO methods]] can help growers choose the approach that best
fits their situation.

The [[jadam-microorganism-solution-jms|JADAM Microorganism Solution (JMS)]] is another approach developed by Youngsang Cho as a simplified,
ultra-low-cost alternative to EM. JMS uses leaf mold or forest soil as
the microbial source, boiled potato or sweet potato as food, and sea
salt or [[jadam-sulfur-js|JADAM sulfur]] to suppress unwanted microbes.
The [[jadam-microorganism-solution-culturing|JMS culturing process]] is
designed to be accessible to farmers with minimal resources and has been
widely adopted in Korea and Southeast Asia.

Application methods for all of these microbial inoculants are similar.
They can be applied as soil drenches at 1:500 to 1:1000 dilution, as
foliar sprays at 1:500 dilution during early morning or evening, added
to compost piles to accelerate decomposition, or incorporated into
[[fermented-plant-extract-fpe|fermented plant extract (FPE)]] brewing as
an inoculant. The
[[teaming-with-microbes-compost-tea-and-microbial-inoculants|compost tea and microbial inoculant guide]] provides detailed application rates and
timing recommendations.

It is worth noting that the scientific evidence for EM specifically is
mixed, with some studies showing clear benefits and others finding no
significant effect. The benefits are most consistent in degraded soils
with low biological activity, where introducing any diverse microbial
community provides measurable improvements. In soils with already healthy
biology, the marginal benefit of adding EM or similar products is
smaller. The key insight from
[[hamilton-soil-biology-and-microorganisms|soil biology research]] is
that feeding existing soil microbes through organic matter and root
exudates is often more effective than adding new organisms.

## Key Points
- [[effective-microorganisms-em|EM]] can be multiplied at home using
  EM-1 mother culture, molasses, and water through anaerobic
  fermentation
- [[indigenous-microorganisms-imo|IMO collection]] captures locally
  adapted microbes using cooked rice placed in natural environments
- [[jadam-microorganism-solution-jms|JADAM JMS]] offers an ultra-low-
  cost alternative using leaf mold and simple ingredients
- All methods produce microbial inoculants that can be applied as soil
  drenches, foliar sprays, or compost accelerators
- Results are most consistent in degraded soils; feeding existing
  biology through organic matter is often more impactful
