## How Do I Grow Onions from Seed, Sets, and Transplants?

## Short Answer

Onions can be started three ways: direct seeding, sets (small dormant bulbs), or transplants grown indoors.
Each method has trade-offs in cost, timing, and reliability depending on your climate and goals.
The [[onion-growing-guide]] covers the fundamentals, but choosing the right starting method and variety for your day-length zone is the key to success.

## Understanding Day-Length Categories

Onions are photoperiodic, meaning they form bulbs in response to day length rather than plant size.
Long-day varieties need 14-16 hours of daylight and are suited to northern latitudes above 36°N.
Short-day varieties bulb up at 10-12 hours and perform best in southern regions below 36°N.
Intermediate or day-neutral varieties bulb across a wider range and are the most flexible choice for middle latitudes.
Matching variety to your latitude is the single most important decision in onion growing, as described in [[alliums-growing-guide]].
Planting a long-day variety in the south produces small bulbs or none at all, while short-day types in the north bolt before bulbing.

## Starting from Seed

Growing onions from seed is the most economical method and offers the widest variety selection.
Start seeds indoors 8-12 weeks before your last frost date for long-day types, or in late summer for short-day winter-planted onions.
Sow seeds densely in flats at quarter-inch depth and keep soil around 70°F for reliable germination.
Seed-grown onions tend to store better and produce larger bulbs than set-grown onions because you can select varieties bred for your region.
The [[growing-alliums-organically]] guide recommends trimming seedling tops to 4 inches every few weeks to encourage stocky, vigorous transplants.
Harden off seedlings gradually before planting out once soil temperature reaches 50°F.

## Starting from Sets

Onion sets are small, dormant bulbs grown from seed the previous year and harvested at marble size.
They are the easiest and fastest method for beginners because you skip the seed-starting phase entirely.
Plant sets in early spring as soon as the soil can be worked, pushing each bulb point-up about one inch deep and four inches apart.
The main drawback is limited variety selection, as most sets are generic long-day types not matched to specific regions.
Sets are also more prone to bolting (sending up a flower stalk) than seed-grown or transplant onions, which ruins bulb quality.
Choose sets smaller than a dime for best results, as larger sets are more likely to bolt prematurely.

## Starting from Transplants (Bare-Root Bundles)

Transplants are young onion plants grown from seed in southern nurseries and shipped bare-root in bundles.
They offer a middle ground between sets and seed, providing named varieties without needing indoor seed-starting equipment.
Plant transplants as soon as they arrive, burying the white portion about one inch deep with roots spread outward.
Trim the tops by one-third at planting to reduce transplant shock and encourage root establishment.
The [[solomon-growing-garlic-onions-leeks]] guide emphasizes soaking transplant roots in water for 15 minutes before planting to rehydrate them.
Transplants often produce the largest, most uniform bulbs when planted at the right time for your day-length zone.

## Soil Preparation and Fertilization

Onions are heavy feeders with shallow root systems that need loose, fertile, well-drained soil.
Work in 2-3 inches of compost and a balanced organic fertilizer before planting, as recommended in [[hamilton-bulb-vegetables-onion-family-organic]].
The ideal soil pH for onions is between 6.0 and 7.0, so test and amend with lime if needed.
Avoid fresh manure, which promotes soft, disease-prone bulbs and encourages thrips and maggot problems.
Side-dress with a nitrogen-rich fertilizer every 3-4 weeks during active leaf growth, stopping when bulbs begin to swell.
Raised beds work exceptionally well for onions because they provide the excellent drainage that prevents rot.

## Spacing, Planting, and Intercropping

Proper spacing directly impacts bulb size, with most varieties needing 4-6 inches between plants in rows 12-18 inches apart.
Closer spacing produces smaller bulbs ideal for storage, while wider spacing yields larger but fewer onions.
Onions make excellent companion plants because their sulfur compounds deter many common garden pests.
Intercropping onions with carrots is a classic combination, as each crop repels the other's primary pest (onion fly and carrot fly).
The [[allium-cepa]] entry describes how onions also pair well with beets, brassicas, and lettuce in intensive plantings.
Avoid planting onions near beans or peas, as allium root exudates can inhibit legume growth.

## Watering and Weed Management

Onions have shallow roots and need consistent moisture, about 1-2 inches of water per week throughout the growing season.
Mulch with straw or grass clippings to retain moisture and suppress weeds, but pull mulch back from bulbs as they mature.
Weed carefully by hand because onion roots are easily damaged by hoeing too close to the plants.
Reduce watering gradually once tops begin to fall over, as this signals the bulbs are entering their final ripening phase.
Stop watering entirely 1-2 weeks before harvest to encourage the papery outer skins to cure properly.

## Pest and Disease Management

The most common onion pests are thrips (tiny insects that silver the leaves) and onion maggots (larvae that tunnel into bulbs).
Prevent thrips by maintaining good air circulation and using row covers during early growth stages.
Onion maggots are deterred by crop rotation, avoiding fresh manure, and using floating row covers over young plants.
Downy mildew and botrytis leaf blight are fungal diseases that thrive in humid, crowded conditions with poor air flow.
The [[garlic-cultivation-and-allium-growing-guide]] notes that proper spacing, crop rotation, and resistant varieties are the best defenses against allium diseases.
Remove and destroy any bolted or diseased plants immediately to prevent spread to healthy neighbors.

## Harvesting and Curing

Onions are ready to harvest when 50-80% of the tops have fallen over and the necks feel soft.
Lift bulbs gently with a fork on a dry, sunny day and lay them in the garden to dry for a few hours.
Move onions to a warm, dry, well-ventilated location to cure for 2-3 weeks until the necks are completely dry and the outer skins are papery.
The [[coleman-overwintered-onions-and-early-spring-crops]] guide describes how properly cured onions can store for months in cool, dry conditions.
Cut the dried tops to one inch above the bulb and trim roots before storing in mesh bags or braided strings.
Never store onions near potatoes, as the gases each releases will cause the other to spoil faster.

## What About Growing Onions in Containers?

Onions grow well in containers at least 10 inches deep and 12 inches wide, with good drainage holes.
Use a lightweight potting mix enriched with compost and a slow-release organic fertilizer.
Container-grown onions need more frequent watering than garden-grown ones, often daily in hot weather.
Short-day and intermediate varieties work best in containers because they mature faster and at smaller sizes.
Green onions and scallions are especially productive in containers and can be harvested just 30-60 days from sowing.
The [[coleman-winter-scallion-production-cold-house]] approach shows how scallions can be grown through winter in protected containers.

## What About Leeks and Other Allium Relatives?

Leeks, shallots, and chives follow similar cultural requirements to onions but with important differences.
Leeks have a much longer growing season (120-150 days) and are typically started indoors like onion seed, as described in [[coleman-leek-deep-planting-technique]].
Blanching leek stems by mounding soil or planting in deep trenches produces the prized long white shanks.
Shallots are typically planted as cloves (like garlic) rather than sets or transplants, and each clove produces a cluster of bulbs.
[[hamilton-leek-growing-organic]] recommends hillling leeks progressively every few weeks for maximum blanched length.
Chives and perennial bunching onions are the easiest alliums to grow and return year after year with minimal care.

## Key Points

- Match onion variety to your day-length zone (long-day, short-day, or intermediate) for reliable bulbing.
- Seed-grown onions offer the best variety selection and storage quality but need 8-12 weeks of indoor starting.
- Sets are easiest for beginners but limited in variety and more prone to bolting, as noted in [[onion-growing-guide]].
- Transplants provide named varieties without indoor seed-starting equipment and often produce the largest bulbs.
- Onions are heavy feeders needing fertile, well-drained soil and consistent nitrogen during active leaf growth.
- Proper curing is essential for long storage, as emphasized in [[alliums-growing-guide]].
- Onions make excellent companion plants for carrots, beets, and brassicas due to their pest-deterrent sulfur compounds.
- Crop rotation prevents onion maggot and soil-borne disease buildup, as described in [[comparison-garlic-vs-onion-growing]].
