# What Is Induced Systemic Resistance and How Do Plants Defend Themselves?

## Short Answer

Induced systemic resistance (ISR) and systemic acquired resistance (SAR) are two plant defense mechanisms that activate whole-plant immunity after exposure to a triggering stimulus.
ISR is triggered by beneficial microbes like [[plant-growth-promoting-rhizobacteria]] and [[trichoderma-and-biocontrol-fungi]], while SAR is activated after a localized pathogen infection.
Both mechanisms prime the plant to respond faster and stronger to future attacks, reducing disease severity without the need for chemical pesticides.

## What Is Induced Systemic Resistance (ISR)?

ISR is a state of enhanced defensive capacity triggered by beneficial soil microorganisms colonizing the plant roots.
Unlike constitutive defenses that are always active, ISR primes the plant's immune system so it can respond more quickly when a pathogen arrives.
The rhizosphere microbiome plays a central role, with [[pgpr-mycorrhizal-inoculants-microplant-establishment]] and [[bacillus-subtilis]] among the most effective ISR-inducing organisms.
Once induced, the plant produces elevated levels of defense compounds throughout its tissues, not just at the site of colonization.

## What Is Systemic Acquired Resistance (SAR)?

SAR is activated when a plant survives a localized infection and then develops broad-spectrum resistance in uninfected tissues.
The signaling molecule salicylic acid plays a central role in SAR activation, as described in [[salicylic-acid-plant-defense]].
After the initial infection, a signal travels through the vascular system to distal plant parts, priming them for defense.
SAR provides long-lasting protection, often persisting for weeks to months after the initial trigger.
The jasmonic acid pathway described in [[jasmonic-acid-plant-defense-signaling]] mediates a related but distinct defense response primarily targeting herbivores and necrotrophic pathogens.

## How Do Trichoderma Species Induce Plant Resistance?

[[trichoderma-and-biocontrol-fungi]] are among the most studied ISR inducers in agriculture.
Species like [[trichoderma-harzianum]], [[trichoderma-virens]], and [[trichoderma-atroviride]] colonize roots and trigger ISR through multiple mechanisms.
These beneficial fungi produce elicitor compounds that activate defense gene expression in the host plant.
[[trichoderma-viride]] and [[trichoderma-koningii]] have also demonstrated ISR activity in controlled studies.
The biocontrol activity combines direct pathogen antagonism with this indirect immune-priming effect, as detailed in [[fungal-mycoparasitism-and-biocontrol-interactions]].

## How Do Mycorrhizal Fungi Contribute to Plant Defense?

Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF) induce a form of resistance sometimes called mycorrhiza-induced resistance.
[[mycorrhiza-induced-resistance-defense-priming]] describes how AMF colonization primes plants for stronger defense responses against foliar pathogens.
[[endophytic-mycorrhizal-induced-systemic-resistance]] shows that endophytic and mycorrhizal fungi work synergistically to activate multiple defense pathways.
The mechanisms include improved nutrient uptake, altered root exudate profiles, and direct activation of [[plant-defense-mechanisms]].
[[mycorrhizal-plant-defense]] documents how these symbiotic relationships enhance both constitutive and induced resistance strategies.

## What Role Do PGPR Play in Induced Resistance?

Plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria (PGPR) are key ISR inducers living in the rhizosphere.
These beneficial bacteria colonize root surfaces and internal root tissues, forming a defensive barrier against pathogens.
[[plant-growth-promoting-rhizobacteria]] produce signaling molecules including lipopolysaccharides, siderophores, and volatile organic compounds that trigger ISR.
The production of 2,4-diacetylphloroglucinol (DAPG) by certain Pseudomonas species is particularly effective at priming plant defenses.
[[bacillus-subtilis]] is one of the most commercially important PGPR species used as a biocontrol agent and ISR inducer.
Commercial formulations of Bacillus strains are widely available and can be applied as seed treatments, soil drenches, or foliar sprays.
The [[cannabis-endorhiza-core-microbiome-pseudomonas-rhizobiales]] research demonstrates that even cannabis plants harbor ISR-inducing bacterial communities in their root tissues.
[[symbiotic-fungi-amf-pgpr-inoculants-microplants]] shows that combining PGPR with mycorrhizal inoculants produces synergistic resistance effects.
Co-inoculation strategies that pair bacteria and fungi often outperform single-organism approaches in both greenhouse and field trials.

## What Are the Key Signaling Pathways?

Plant defense signaling involves two major hormonal pathways that can be mutually antagonistic.

| ISR | Jasmonic acid / Ethylene | Necrotrophic pathogens + herbivores | Beneficial microbes |

## What About Defense Priming vs. Direct Activation?

An important distinction in induced resistance is the difference between priming and direct activation of defenses.
Priming does not immediately activate defense genes but instead sensitizes the plant to respond more rapidly when a pathogen is detected.
This is energetically efficient because the plant avoids the metabolic cost of maintaining high defense levels continuously.
[[endophytic-mycorrhizal-induced-systemic-resistance]] describes how endophytes and mycorrhizal partners preferentially use priming rather than constitutive activation.
When a primed plant encounters a pathogen, it mounts a faster and stronger defense response than an unprimed plant would.
Both ISR and SAR involve priming components, though SAR also includes some directly activated defense genes.
This priming mechanism is why induced resistance often provides broader-spectrum protection than single-target chemical pesticides.

## What About Combining ISR with Other Biocontrol Strategies?

Induced resistance works best as part of an integrated pest management approach rather than a standalone solution.
Combining ISR-inducing inoculants with [[entomopathogenic-fungi-and-biological-pest-control]] targets both insect pests and plant diseases simultaneously.
[[comparison-trichoderma-vs-bacillus-vs-pseudomonas-biocontrol]] can help growers choose the right biocontrol organisms for their specific crop and climate conditions.
Using cover crops that support beneficial soil microbiomes enhances the natural ISR-inducing capacity of the soil food web.
Foliar applications of compost tea deliver both direct antimicrobial compounds and ISR-inducing microorganisms to leaf surfaces.
[[comparison-silicon-vs-calcium-in-plant-defense-and-structural-support]] shows that mineral nutrition can also strengthen plant defenses alongside biological ISR inducers.
Rotating between different biocontrol products prevents pathogen adaptation and maintains diverse ISR-triggering communities.

## How Can Gardeners and Farmers Use Induced Resistance?

Practical applications of induced resistance in agriculture and horticulture are growing rapidly.
Inoculating soil or seed with [[trichoderma-harzianum]] or [[bacillus-subtilis]] products can prime crops before disease pressure arrives.
Using compost tea rich in beneficial organisms supports the [[endophytic-fungi-plant-defense-dighton]] mechanisms that naturally suppress disease.
Maintaining healthy [[fungal-plant-defense]] communities through minimal tillage and organic matter additions sustains ISR-inducing microbiomes.
Applying products based on [[entomopathogenic-fungi-biocontrol-mycopesticides]] can simultaneously control pests and trigger ISR in treated plants.
For cannabis growers specifically, [[trichoderma-forest-green-mold-guide]] offers species-level guidance on selecting the right Trichoderma strain.
Timing matters: applying ISR inducers early in the growing season, before disease pressure builds, produces the best results.
In greenhouse environments, regular applications every 2-4 weeks maintain the primed state throughout the crop cycle.
Outdoor growers can reduce application frequency but should reapply after heavy rain events that wash away soil-surface inoculants.

## Key Points

- ISR and SAR are distinct but complementary plant immune priming mechanisms activated by different triggers.
- [[plant-growth-promoting-rhizobacteria]] and [[trichoderma-and-biocontrol-fungi]] are the primary ISR-inducing organisms used in agriculture.
- [[salicylic-acid-plant-defense]] mediates SAR, while [[jasmonic-acid-plant-defense-signaling]] mediates ISR and anti-herbivore defenses.
- [[mycorrhiza-induced-resistance-defense-priming]] shows AMF contribute to induced resistance alongside bacteria and biocontrol fungi.
- Gardeners can harness induced resistance through microbial inoculants, compost teas, and practices that support beneficial soil microbiomes.
- [[plant-defense-mechanisms]] include both constitutive barriers and these inducible systemic responses that protect the entire plant.
- Combining ISR inducers with other biocontrol agents creates layered defense systems that pathogens struggle to overcome.
