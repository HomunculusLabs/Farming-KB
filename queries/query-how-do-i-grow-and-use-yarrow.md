## How Do I Grow and Use Yarrow?

## Short Answer

Yarrow ([[yarrow-achillea-millefolium]]) is a hardy perennial herb that thrives in poor soil, full sun, and neglect.
It serves triple duty in the garden as a [[companion-planting|companion plant]], [[dynamic-accumulators|dynamic accumulator]], and [[beneficial-insects-pollinators|pollinator magnet]].
Medicinally, its leaves and flowers are used in [[herbal-tinctures-guide|tinctures]], [[herbal-salves-and-balms|salves]], and teas for wound healing and digestive support.
Even beginner gardeners can succeed with yarrow, as it tolerates drought, poor soil, and minimal care.

## Detailed Explanation

### What Is Yarrow and Why Should I Grow It?

Yarrow ([[yarrow]]) is a member of the Asteraceae family native to temperate regions across the Northern Hemisphere.
Its fern-like foliage and flat-topped flower clusters make it easy to identify in the wild and attractive in garden beds.
The species name *Achillea* refers to the Greek hero Achilles, who reportedly used yarrow to treat battlefield wounds.
Modern permaculture designers value yarrow for its ability to attract [[beneficial-insects-guide|beneficial insects]], accumulate nutrients, and thrive without irrigation.
Its deep taproot mines minerals from subsoil and brings them to the surface via [[chop-and-drop-mulching|chop-and-drop mulching]].

### How Do I Plant and Establish Yarrow?

Yarrow grows readily from seed, division, or transplant and tolerates a wide range of soil types.
Direct sow seeds in early spring by pressing them into the soil surface without covering, as they need light to germinate.
Space plants 12 to 18 inches apart in a location receiving at least six hours of direct sun.
Yarrow prefers well-drained soil and will struggle in waterlogged or heavily shaded sites.
Once established, it requires virtually no watering, fertilizing, or pest management.
Divide clumps every two to three years in spring or fall to maintain vigor and propagate new plants.

### What Role Does Yarrow Play in Permaculture Guilds?

In a [[companion-planting-guild|companion planting guild]], yarrow serves as a mineral accumulator and insectary plant.
It pairs well with [[comfrey]], [[calendula-cultivation-and-medicinal-uses|calendula]], and [[chamomile-cultivation-and-uses|chamomile]] in a medicinal herb guild.
Yarrow's flat flower heads provide landing pads for [[beneficial-insects-and-biological-pest-control|predatory insects]] like hoverflies and lacewings.
Its strong scent confuses pest insects, functioning as a [[companion-planting-deterrents|companion planting deterrent]].
Plant yarrow near fruit trees in a [[permaculture-dynamic-accumulator-plants|food forest guild]] to cycle nutrients upward.
The chopped foliage makes excellent [[dynamic-accumulator-chop-and-drop-mulching|mulch]] rich in potassium, calcium, and trace minerals.

### How Do I Harvest and Dry Yarrow?

Harvest yarrow flowers when they are fully open but before they begin to brown or fade.
Cut the top third of the flowering stalk with clean scissors, including a few inches of leafy stem.
For leaf harvest, pick young leaves throughout the growing season before the plant flowers.
Dry bundles upside down in a warm, well-ventilated area away from direct sunlight.
Store dried yarrow in airtight glass jars away from heat and light for up to one year.
Properly dried yarrow retains its medicinal properties and characteristic bitter aroma.

### How Is Yarrow Used Medicinally?

Yarrow has a long history in traditional herbalism as a [[herbal-tincture-making|wound-healing agent]] and digestive bitter.
The leaves can be applied fresh as a poultice to stop bleeding from cuts and scrapes.
A strong yarrow tea promotes sweating during fevers and supports digestive comfort after heavy meals.
Yarrow-infused oil forms the base of [[herbal-salves-and-balms|healing salves]] when combined with beeswax.
Compared to [[comparison-echinacea-vs-yarrow|echinacea]], yarrow is more astringent and better suited for topical wound care.
Compared to [[comparison-comfrey-vs-yarrow|comfrey]], yarrow excels at stopping bleeding while comfrey excels at tissue repair.

### What About Growing Yarrow in Containers?

Yarrow adapts well to container growing, making it accessible for apartment gardeners and balcony herb gardens.
Choose a pot at least 10 inches deep with drainage holes, filled with a sandy, well-draining potting mix.
Compact cultivars like `Paprika` or `Moonshine` work best for containers, staying under 24 inches tall.
Water container-grown yarrow only when the top inch of soil is completely dry.
Container plants benefit from a light top-dressing of [[dynamic-accumulator-plants-potassium|potassium-rich compost]] in spring.
Move pots to a sheltered location in zones 3 and below for winter protection.

- It functions as a [[dynamic-accumulators|dynamic accumulator]], mining calcium, potassium, and trace minerals from deep soil
- The flowers attract [[beneficial-insects-pollinators|pollinators]] and predatory insects that control garden pests
- Yarrow enhances [[companion-planting-guild|companion planting guilds]] when grown near fruit trees and vegetables

- The plant tolerates drought, poor soil, and neglect once established

- Yarrow leaves make an excellent compost activator, speeding decomposition in both hot and cold piles

### What About Yarrow for Pest Management?

Yarrow's strong aromatic oils make it a useful component of [[query-how-do-i-make-homemade-organic-pest-control-sprays-for-my-garden|organic pest spray]] recipes.
A simple yarrow tea spray, made by steeping fresh leaves in hot water, deters aphids and cabbage worms on brassicas.
The plant's presence in garden borders confuses pests seeking their host plants through chemical camouflage.
Yarrow attracts [[beneficial-insects-guide|ladybugs]], [[query-how-do-parasitoid-wasps-control-pest-populations|parasitoid wasps]], and syrphid flies that prey on common garden pests.
Interplanting yarrow with vegetables reduces pest pressure without requiring any synthetic pesticides.
This approach aligns with [[query-what-is-integrated-pest-management-for-organic-gardens|integrated pest management]] principles for organic gardens.

### What About Using Yarrow in Compost and Compost Tea?

Fresh yarrow leaves and stems are rich in nitrogen, potassium, and copper, making them an excellent compost activator.
Adding yarrow to [[query-how-to-build-a-compost-pile-that-works|compost piles]] speeds decomposition because its nutrient profile feeds thermophilic bacteria.
Yarrow is one of the traditional biodynamic compost preparations, believed to enhance nutrient cycling in finished compost.
A handful of chopped yarrow leaves in [[query-how-to-brew-a-fungal-dominant-compost-tea|compost tea]] adds trace minerals and stimulates microbial diversity.
When combined with [[comfrey-tea-liquid-fertilizer|comfrey tea]], yarrow creates a balanced liquid fertilizer high in both potassium and calcium.
Dry yarrow stalks also serve as carbon-rich browns in the compost pile during late fall cleanup.

### Which Yarrow Varieties Should I Consider?

Common white yarrow (*Achillea millefolium*) is the most medicinally potent and the easiest to find.
Red-flowered cultivars like `Paprika` and `Red Velvet` offer striking garden color with similar ecological benefits.
`Moonshine` produces pale yellow flowers on silver-gray foliage and stays compact at about 18 inches tall.
`Desert Eve` series varieties bloom in shades of red, pink, and gold with excellent heat tolerance.
For wildcrafting and medicine, stick with the straight species rather than ornamental cultivars.
The native North American yarrow tends to be more cold-hardy than European ornamental selections.
