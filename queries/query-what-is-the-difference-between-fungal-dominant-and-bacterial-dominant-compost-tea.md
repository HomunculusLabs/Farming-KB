# What Is the Difference Between Fungal and Bacterial Dominant Compost Tea?

## Short Answer
Fungal dominant compost tea is brewed to multiply beneficial fungi like
mycorrhizal partners and decomposers, using ingredients like humic acids,
kelp, and longer brew times with minimal aeration disruption. Bacterial
dominant tea boosts populations of beneficial bacteria using simple food
sources like molasses and shorter brew times with vigorous aeration. The
choice depends on plant type and growth stage: trees, perennials, and
mature cannabis benefit from fungal teas, while annual vegetables,
seedlings, and early growth stages benefit from bacterial teas.

## Detailed Explanation

Compost tea is a liquid extract created by brewing high-quality compost in
aerated water with food sources that feed beneficial microorganisms. The
resulting liquid is rich in the [[comparison-soil-food-web-vs-bacterial-vs-fungal-soil]] organisms that drive nutrient cycling, disease suppression, and soil
structure formation. However, not all compost teas are the same. The
specific microbial community in the tea depends heavily on the compost
source, the food additives used during brewing, the aeration level, and
the brew duration. Understanding how to steer a brew toward fungal or
bacterial dominance allows growers to match their tea applications to
specific plant needs and growing conditions.

Bacterial dominant teas are the most common type and the easiest to brew
successfully, making them an excellent starting point for growers new to
compost tea production. They use food sources that selectively feed
bacteria, primarily simple sugars like unsulfured blackstrap molasses,
which bacteria can metabolize quickly and efficiently. Other bacterial
food sources include fish hydrolysate, soluble kelp meal, and simple
fruit juices. The brew is heavily aerated with vigorous bubbling to
maintain dissolved oxygen levels above 6 mg/L, which favors aerobic
bacteria over fungi and anaerobic organisms. Brew times are typically 24
to 36 hours. The result is a tea teeming with diverse bacterial species
including Pseudomonas, Bacillus, and Actinomycetes that contribute to
nutrient mineralization, [[aact-disease-suppression-recipes]], and plant growth promotion.

Fungal dominant teas are more challenging to produce but are essential for
certain growing situations where fungal biology is needed. Fungi require
different food sources than bacteria, including more complex compounds like
humic acid, fulvic acid, fish hydrolysate (not fish emulsion, which can
favor bacteria due to its oil content), and oatmeal or oat bran. Critically,
fungal teas require gentle rather than vigorous aeration; too much
turbulence physically damages and shears fungal hyphae before they can
grow to a useful length. Some experienced brewers use no aeration at all
for true fungal teas, relying on occasional gentle stirring instead. Brew
times are longer, typically 48 to 72 hours, to give fungal hyphae time to
grow and multiply. The compost source matters enormously: compost made
with a higher proportion of woody materials, leaf litter, and
[[comparison-leaf-mold-composting-vs-hot-composting-method]]
naturally contains more fungal biomass than compost made primarily from
green materials and manure.

The decision between fungal and bacterial dominance relates to the concept
of [[bacterial-to-fungal-soil-succession]]. In
nature, disturbed soils and early-succession environments tend to be
bacterial dominated, supporting annual plants and fast-growing colonizers
that need readily available nutrients. Mature, undisturbed ecosystems like
forests develop fungal-dominated soils that support perennials and woody
plants through slower, more complex nutrient cycling networks. For
cannabis, the ideal soil microbial balance shifts through the grow cycle.
Early [[cannabis-vegetative-growth-stage]] benefits from
a more bacterial-dominated biology that rapidly mineralizes nitrogen and
other nutrients from organic matter. As plants mature and flowering
approaches, a more fungal-dominated soil supports the complex nutrient
cycling and [[arbuscular-mycorrhizal-network-visualization-anastomosis]] associations
that contribute to [[query-how-to-maximize-terpene-and-resin-production-in-cannabis]] and overall plant health during the demanding flowering phase.

Application methods differ between the two tea types based on the biology
of the organisms involved. Bacterial teas are well suited for foliar
spraying because bacterial cells are small enough to enter leaf stomata
and colonize the phyllosphere, providing [[cervantes-foliar-feeding]] benefits alongside surface-level disease protection. Fungal teas
are more effective as soil drenches where the hyphae can establish in the
root zone and begin building relationships with plant roots through
nutrient exchange networks. For [[living-soil-cannabis]]
cannabis grows, periodic applications of fungal-dominant teas help
maintain the diverse fungal community that makes living soil productive.
In [[aact-applications-guide-ingham]], bacterial teas
are most valuable during the first half of the grow and fungal teas
during the second half, especially in the weeks leading up to and during
the early flowering period.

## Key Points
- Bacterial dominant teas use molasses and vigorous aeration with 24 to 36
  hour brew times for rapid microbial growth
- Fungal dominant teas use humic acids and gentle aeration with 48 to 72
  hour brew times to grow hyphal networks
- Bacterial teas suit annuals, seedlings, and early growth; fungal teas
  suit perennials, trees, and mature cannabis plants
- Compost source matters: woody, leafy compost supports fungi; green,
  manure-based compost supports bacteria
- Bacterial teas work well as foliar sprays; fungal teas are most
  effective applied as soil drenches to the root zone
