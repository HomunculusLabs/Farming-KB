# How do I make kimchi with lacto-fermentation?

## Short Answer

Kimchi is made by salting vegetables, mixing them with seasonings, packing them under their own brine, and letting lactic-acid bacteria acidify the mixture.
The practical kimchi process is covered in [[kimchi-making]], while the underlying preservation biology is explained by [[lacto-fermentation-and-food-preservation-methods]].
For a beginner, the most important controls are salt level, submersion, temperature, clean containers, and frequent tasting.
Kimchi should smell sour, savory, and alive, not rotten, putrid, or moldy.
Once it tastes pleasantly sour, move it to cold storage to slow fermentation.

## Detailed Explanation

### What makes kimchi ferment?

Kimchi ferments because salt-tolerant lactic-acid bacteria convert vegetable sugars into lactic acid.
That acid lowers pH and makes the environment less friendly to many spoilage organisms.
The page [[lactic-acid-bacteria]] explains the microbial group behind this transformation.
The page [[lacto-fermentation-and-food-preservation-methods]] explains why acidification is a preservation strategy.
Kimchi is therefore not preserved by sterility.
It is preserved by creating conditions where the right microbes dominate.
This is why clean handling matters, but total sterilization is not the goal.

### What ingredients do I need?

A basic kimchi needs cabbage or another vegetable, salt, aromatics, chile, and enough liquid to keep everything submerged.
Common additions include garlic, ginger, scallions, radish, and gochugaru.
The page [[kimchi-making]] is the direct reference for kimchi-specific ingredient choices.
Salt draws water from the vegetables and creates the brine that protects the ferment.
Seasonings add flavor and may influence microbial activity, but they do not replace proper brine management.
Avoid iodized salt or heavily anti-caking salts if they create off flavors or cloudy textures.
Use vegetables that are fresh enough to still have crisp structure.

### How much salt should I use?

Salt must be high enough to suppress spoilage while still allowing lactic-acid bacteria to work.
Traditional kimchi often uses a salting step followed by draining and seasoning rather than a single exact brine percentage.
For beginners, consistency matters more than chasing a perfect traditional style.
Weighing vegetables and salt is more repeatable than guessing by handfuls.
The general principles in [[food-fermentation-guide]] help translate between recipes, brines, and vegetable ferments.
Too little salt can lead to mushy textures and spoilage risk.
Too much salt can slow fermentation and make the finished kimchi unpleasant to eat.

### Why must the vegetables stay submerged?

Lacto-fermentation works best when vegetables remain below the brine and away from oxygen.
Exposed pieces can dry, discolor, grow surface yeast, or develop mold.
A fermentation weight, small jar, clean stone, or folded cabbage leaf can hold vegetables under the liquid.
A loose lid or airlock lets carbon dioxide escape without inviting too much oxygen exposure.
The page [[comparison-lacto-fermentation-vs-canning]] is useful because it contrasts living fermentation with sealed heat-processing.
Fermentation jars should be placed on a tray because active kimchi can bubble over.
If the brine level drops, press the vegetables down before adding extra liquid.

### What temperature is best?

Warm temperatures ferment kimchi faster and produce sourness quickly.
Cool temperatures ferment it more slowly and often preserve crunch and aroma longer.
Room-temperature fermentation for a few days followed by refrigeration is a common home pattern.
Taste is a better guide than the calendar because vegetable cut size, salt level, and room temperature all change the pace.
The page [[lacto-fermentation-recipes]] gives useful context for how different ferments move at different speeds.
If the kitchen is very hot, shorten the room-temperature phase.
If the kitchen is cold, expect a slower start and less bubbling.

### How do I know it is working?

Working kimchi usually produces bubbles, brine movement, sour aroma, and gradually sharper flavor.
The vegetables may soften but should not turn slimy or putrid.
A little surface yeast can sometimes appear on ferments, but fuzzy mold, foul odors, and unsafe handling are warning signs.
The species page [[lactobacillus-plantarum]] is relevant because this microbe is commonly associated with vegetable fermentations.
The species page [[lactobacillus-brevis]] is another useful reference for lactic fermentation ecology.
The point is not to identify every microbe in the jar.
The point is to create a brined, acidic, low-oxygen habitat where beneficial fermenters dominate.

### How is kimchi different from sauerkraut?

Kimchi and sauerkraut are both lactic vegetable ferments, but kimchi usually includes more aromatics, chile, and mixed vegetables.
Sauerkraut often relies on finely shredded cabbage and salt with fewer seasonings.
The query page [[query-how-to-make-sauerkraut-at-home]] is useful if you want the simpler cabbage-only comparison.
Kimchi may be eaten younger, fizzier, and more pungent than many sauerkraut styles.
Both foods depend on salt, submersion, and lactic-acid bacteria.
Learning one makes the other easier.
The page [[comparison-vinegar-vs-lacto-fermentation]] also helps distinguish true fermentation from simply adding acid.

### How should I store and use it?

Move kimchi to the refrigerator when it tastes as sour as you like.
Cold storage slows fermentation but does not stop it completely.
Use clean utensils to remove portions so the jar stays stable longer.
Open the jar carefully because carbon dioxide can build pressure.
Older kimchi can be used in cooked dishes when it becomes too sour for fresh eating.
Discard kimchi if it develops persistent mold, rotten odors, or textures that suggest spoilage rather than fermentation.
Good fermentation practice is a combination of recipe, observation, and caution.
