# How to Build a Raised Bed Garden

## Short Answer
A raised bed garden is an elevated frame filled with quality soil that improves drainage, reduces compaction, and extends your growing season. Building one involves choosing a sunny site, constructing a frame from safe materials, filling it with a nutrient-rich soil blend, and planting with intensive spacing techniques.

## Key Points
- Raised beds improve drainage, warm earlier in spring, reduce weeds, and let you create ideal soil regardless of what lies beneath
- Build frames from untreated cedar or redwood at 4 feet wide by 8 feet long and 10 to 12 inches tall
- Fill with a blend of 50% topsoil, 30% compost, and 20% aeration material rather than expensive bagged mixes
- Use intensive grid spacing rather than traditional rows to maximize production per square foot
- Line the bottom with cardboard to suppress weeds and consider hugelkultur wood layers for long-term fertility
- Never use pressure-treated wood or chemically treated materials for food-growing beds
- Orient beds north to south for even light exposure across the entire growing area
- Double-dig beneath the frame to improve root penetration in beds shorter than 12 inches

## Detailed Explanation

### Why Build Raised Beds?
Raised bed gardening is one of the most effective ways to maximize food production in a small space while minimizing the physical demands of traditional ground-level gardening.

By elevating the growing area 6 to 24 inches, raised beds improve drainage in wet climates and warm up earlier in spring for earlier planting. They also reduce weed pressure from surrounding lawn areas and allow you to create ideal soil conditions regardless of what lies beneath.

The [[solomon-raised-beds]] guide explains that raised beds are particularly valuable in areas with heavy clay, rocky, or contaminated soil. You fill them with a custom soil mix rather than trying to amend problematic native ground.

Additional benefits include reduced bending and kneeling for gardeners with mobility limitations, better pest control since slugs and snails struggle to climb into elevated beds, and easier installation of season-extension tools like row covers and hoop houses.

### Site Selection and Planning
Site selection is the critical first step before building any raised bed. Most vegetables and herbs require a minimum of six to eight hours of direct sunlight daily, so observe your yard through a full day before committing to a location.

Avoid low-lying areas where water pools after heavy rain. Also avoid spots near large trees whose roots will compete with your vegetables for nutrients and moisture throughout the growing season.

Orient beds north to south if possible to maximize even light exposure across the entire bed surface. If your yard has a slope, position beds perpendicular to the slope to reduce soil erosion during heavy rains.

The [[biointensive-bed-preparation]] method recommends double-digging the native soil beneath the raised bed frame. This loosens compaction and improves root penetration into the subsoil. This step is optional for beds 12 inches or taller but highly recommended for shallower beds.

Consider proximity to a water source when planning your layout. Position beds within reach of a hose or plan to install drip irrigation from the start. Access to a compost pile nearby also simplifies ongoing top-dressing and maintenance.

### Choosing Construction Materials
Frame construction materials should be safe, durable, and affordable. The table below compares the most common options for home gardeners:

| Material | Lifespan | Cost | Notes |
| Cedar / Redwood | 10-15 years | High | Naturally rot-resistant, best overall choice |
| Pine | 5-7 years | Low | Treat with raw linseed oil to extend life |
| Concrete Blocks | 20+ years | Medium | Heavy but permanent; avoid older blocks with fly ash |
| Galvanized Metal | 15+ years | Medium | Modern look; can heat soil in hot climates |

Avoid pressure-treated lumber, railroad ties, and pallets unless you can verify they are untreated and food-safe. These materials may leach chemicals into your growing soil over time as they weather and break down.

Standard raised bed dimensions are 4 feet wide by 8 feet long and 10 to 12 inches tall. The 4-foot width ensures you can reach the center from either side without stepping on the soil, which prevents compaction that reduces root growth and yields.

The [[raised-beds-vs-in-ground-vs-containers]] page provides a detailed comparison of raised beds against in-ground gardens and container gardening to help you choose the best approach for your space, budget, and growing goals.

### Step-by-Step Construction
Follow these steps to build a basic raised bed from untreated lumber:

1. **Mark the site** with stakes and string at your chosen dimensions (typically 4x8 feet)
2. **Level the ground** by removing sod and flattening the area with a rake
3. **Cut your boards** to length using a hand saw or circular saw
4. **Assemble the frame** using 3-inch exterior-grade screws or pre-made corner brackets
5. **Place the frame** on the leveled site and check with a level in both directions
6. **Line the bottom** with untreated cardboard or several layers of newspaper to smother existing weeds
7. **Fill with soil** using the layered approach described in the next section
8. **Water thoroughly** and let the bed settle for a few days before planting

For corners, use simple butt joints with exterior screws or buy pre-made metal corner brackets from a hardware store. Reinforce tall beds (over 12 inches) with interior stakes or cross-bracing to prevent bowing when the soil becomes wet and heavy.

If gopher or mole pressure is a concern in your area, line the bottom of the bed with half-inch hardware cloth before adding soil and cardboard. This physical barrier prevents tunneling pests from accessing your plant roots from below.

### Soil Filling Strategy
Soil filling is where many new raised bed builders make expensive mistakes. There is no need to fill the entire bed volume with premium bagged soil mix, which can cost hundreds of dollars for a single large bed.

The [[solomon-soil-preparation-bed-prep]] method recommends a cost-effective layered approach. Place cardboard or newspaper at the bottom to suppress weeds, add a base layer of native topsoil or inexpensive bulk soil, then top with 3 to 6 inches of a high-quality compost and topsoil blend.

A popular and proven recipe is:
- 50 percent screened topsoil for mineral content and structure
- 30 percent compost (homemade or bulk purchased) for nutrients and biology
- 20 percent aeration material such as perlite, vermiculite, or coarse sand for drainage

Avoid using pure compost alone as your growing medium. It can hold too much moisture and shrink significantly as it decomposes, leaving your plants with inadequate root support mid-season.

For long-term soil health, see [[query-how-to-build-healthy-garden-soil]] for detailed guidance on building and maintaining living soil ecosystems in raised beds.

### Hugelkultur and Advanced Techniques
The [[holzer-raised-bed-design]] approach, developed by Sepp Holzer, takes raised beds further by incorporating hugelkultur principles. In this method, logs and branches are placed at the bottom of the raised bed before adding soil layers on top.

As the wood slowly decomposes over several years, it releases nutrients, retains moisture, and creates a rich fungal soil ecosystem. This technique is particularly effective in arid climates where water retention is critical and adds long-term soil fertility without annual compost applications.

For a full guide on building hugelkultur beds, see [[query-what-is-hugelkultur-and-how-do-i-build-a-bed]].

Other advanced techniques worth exploring include:
- **Keyhole beds:** a circular raised bed with a compost basket in the center for continuous feeding
- **Wicking beds:** use a water reservoir beneath the soil to reduce irrigation frequency
- **Sheet-mulched beds:** layer organic materials directly on the ground without a physical frame

### Planting and Spacing
Planting in raised beds follows intensive spacing principles rather than traditional row gardening. Plants are spaced closer together in a grid pattern because the improved soil quality means every square foot of the bed is productive growing space.

The [[square-foot-gardening]] method divides beds into one-foot squares, each planted with a specific number of plants based on their mature size:
- **1 per square:** tomato, pepper, eggplant, cabbage
- **4 per square:** lettuce, Swiss chard, marigolds
- **9 per square:** spinach, bush beans, beets
- **16 per square:** carrots, radishes, onions, parsnips

This intensive approach can produce two to five times more food per square foot than conventional row gardening. Succession planting works especially well in raised beds because the soil warms quickly and beds can be replanted immediately after each harvest.

### Maintenance and Seasonal Care
Raised beds require less ongoing maintenance than in-ground gardens, but a few seasonal tasks keep them productive year after year:
- **Top-dress** with 1 to 2 inches of finished compost in early spring before planting
- **Mulch** with straw, leaves, or wood chips to retain moisture and suppress weeds
- **Rotate crops** by plant family each season to prevent disease buildup and nutrient depletion
- **Apply compost tea** every 2 to 4 weeks during the growing season for a microbial boost

For detailed instructions on brewing compost tea, see [[query-how-to-make-compost-tea-for-my-plants]].

At the end of each growing season, remove dead plant material and either compost it or chop and drop it directly onto the bed surface. Add a layer of finished compost and optionally cover with a tarp or heavy mulch to protect soil biology over winter. Avoid leaving bare soil exposed to rain and wind, which erodes nutrients and disrupts beneficial microbial networks.
