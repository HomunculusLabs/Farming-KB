# What Are Permaculture Earthworks and How Do I Plan Them?

## Short Answer

Permaculture earthworks are intentional modifications to the land's shape, contours, and water flow patterns.

They are designed to capture, store, and distribute water across a landscape while reducing erosion and building soil fertility.

Common examples include swales, keyline plowing, ponds, terraces, dams, and berms.

Proper planning starts with careful observation of the site's topography, water shed patterns, and existing vegetation before moving any soil.

## What qualifies as earthworks in permaculture?

Earthworks encompass any large-scale reshaping of the land surface using heavy machinery or manual labor.

In permaculture, the goal is always to slow, spread, and sink water rather than letting it run off and cause erosion.

[[swale]] and [[swale-design-construction]] covers the practical steps from marking contours to final compaction.

On gentle slopes, swales can be spaced 10 to 30 meters apart depending on rainfall intensity and soil type.

On steeper terrain, closer spacing is needed because water accelerates faster and has less time to infiltrate.

Swales are often planted with [[keyline-design]] is a pattern developed by P.A. Yeomans for managing water across large pastoral properties.

It involves identifying the "keypoint" where a valley floor transitions to a steeper convex slope.

Plowing or ripping is done parallel to the contour above the keypoint and slightly off-contour below it.

This off-contour pattern gently directs water from valleys toward ridges, distributing moisture more evenly.

[[comparison-keyline-vs-contour-swale]] shows that keyline ripping causes less surface disturbance than full swale excavation.

## Ponds and dams for water storage

[[backyard-pond-building]] covers smaller-scale [[holzer-pond-construction-and-aquaculture]] suitable for homesteads and food forests.

Ponds should be sited above the highest use point so water can be distributed by gravity.

The dam wall must be built on stable subsoil or clay, never on organic material that will decompose and cause seepage.

[[comparison-terracing-vs-swale]] notes that terraces create usable [[cotter-growing-space-design-fruiting-rooms]] while swales primarily manage water.

Terraces can be bench terraces cut into the hillside or broad-based terraces built up with earth.

On very steep slopes exceeding 15 degrees, terracing may be the only practical option for annual crops.

Steeper slopes are generally better suited to perennial tree crops with [[comparison-constructed-wetlands-vs-bioswales]] shows that [[comparison-constructed-wetlands-vs-bioswales]] treat water quality while bioswales handle volume.

[[permaculture-earthworks-and-water-harvesting]] recommends starting with observation for at least one full year before moving dirt.

## Common mistakes to avoid

Building on fill or unstable soil is the most dangerous earthworks mistake and can lead to dam failure.

Oversizing swales or ponds for the actual catchment area leads to stagnant water and mosquito problems.

Failing to install spillways on dams or ponds means water will cut around the structure during heavy storms.

Not compacting berm soil properly results in rapid settling, erosion, and loss of water-holding capacity.

Ignoring existing drainage patterns often creates new erosion problems downstream of the earthworks.

## Key Points

- Earthworks reshape the land to slow, spread, and sink water across the landscape
- [[swale-design]] are the most accessible earthworks for beginners on moderate slopes
- [[dam-construction]] and [[permaculture-earthworks]] should integrate with plantings and animal systems for maximum benefit
