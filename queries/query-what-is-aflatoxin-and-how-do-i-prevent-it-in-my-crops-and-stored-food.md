# What is aflatoxin and how do I prevent it in my crops and stored food?

## Short Answer

Aflatoxins are highly toxic, cancer-causing compounds produced primarily by the fungi [[aspergillus-flavus]] and [[aspergillus]] species during infection of crops.
They are among the most potent naturally occurring carcinogens known and contaminate staple foods like corn, peanuts, tree nuts, and grains worldwide.
Prevention relies on good agricultural practices, rapid drying after harvest, and proper storage conditions that keep moisture below critical thresholds.

## What Are Aflatoxins?

Aflatoxins are a group of chemically related [[mycotoxins]] produced by certain species of Aspergillus molds, chiefly [[aspergillus-flavus]] and Aspergillus parasiticus.
The discovery of aflatoxins traces back to the infamous [[aflatoxin-turkey-x-disease-1960-discovery-peanut-meal]] outbreak in England, when over 100,000 turkey poults died after eating contaminated peanut meal.
This event catalyzed modern mycotoxin research and food safety regulations worldwide.

There are four major aflatoxin types: B1, B2, G1, and G2, with B1 being the most toxic and most commonly detected.
Aflatoxin B1 is classified as a Group 1 human carcinogen by the International Agency for Research on Cancer.
When dairy animals consume contaminated feed, they metabolize B1 into aflatoxin M1, which then appears in milk and dairy products.

## Which Crops Are Most at Risk?

Aflatoxin contamination is most common in warm, humid climates but can occur anywhere given the right conditions.
The crops most frequently affected include corn (maize), peanuts (groundnuts), cottonseed, tree nuts (especially almonds, pistachios, and walnuts), figs, and dried spices.
Grain crops like sorghum and rice can also be contaminated, particularly when harvested or stored under moist conditions.
Oilseed crops are especially vulnerable because the high fat content provides an ideal growth medium for [[aspergillus-flavus]].

## How Does Contamination Happen?

Contamination can occur in the field before harvest, during harvest, or during storage and transport.
In the field, drought-stressed plants are more susceptible to [[aspergillus]] infection because plant defenses are weakened and damaged kernels provide entry points.
Insect damage to ears, pods, or kernels creates wounds where fungal spores can colonize the tissue.
During harvest, mechanical damage to grains and nuts creates additional entry points for mold growth.
Post-harvest contamination is the most preventable phase and occurs when crops are stored at moisture levels above 12-14 percent with warm temperatures.
The relationship between [[aflatoxins-mycotoxins-food]] and storage conditions is well documented in agricultural science.

## Health Effects of Aflatoxin Exposure

Acute aflatoxin poisoning (aflatoxicosis) can cause liver damage, abdominal pain, vomiting, and in severe cases, death.
Chronic low-level exposure is more common and far more insidious, leading to liver cancer, immune suppression, and stunted growth in children.
Aflatoxin B1 is the most potent liver carcinogen known, and its effects are dramatically worsened in people who also carry hepatitis B virus.
The broader category of [[mycotoxins-and-fungal-toxins-in-food-agriculture-and-health]] includes several other dangerous compounds, but aflatoxins remain the most significant threat to human health among them.
Children are particularly vulnerable because their smaller body weight means higher relative exposure from contaminated food.

## How Do I Prevent Aflatoxin in the Field?

Prevention starts with crop variety selection — choose cultivars bred for resistance to [[aspergillus]] ear rot or pod infection.
Maintain proper plant spacing and irrigation to reduce drought stress, which makes plants vulnerable to infection.
Practice [[mycotoxins-and-human-health]]-aware crop rotation to break disease cycles and reduce soil-borne inoculum.
Use biocontrol agents such as native non-toxigenic strains of [[aspergillus-flavus]] that outcompete toxin-producing strains, a strategy widely used in the southern United States and Africa.
Control insect pests that create entry wounds using integrated pest management or organic methods like [[trichoderma]]-based biocontrol sprays.
Time your harvest carefully — leaving mature crops in the field during warm, wet weather dramatically increases infection risk.

## How Do I Prevent Aflatoxin During Storage?

The single most important factor is drying crops to safe moisture levels quickly after harvest.
For grains, this means reducing moisture below 13 percent within 24-48 hours of harvest using forced-air drying or solar dryers.
Store dried crops in clean, dry, well-ventilated facilities at temperatures below 20°C (68°F) whenever possible.
Use hermetic storage bags or sealed containers that exclude oxygen and prevent moisture migration.
Inspect stored crops regularly for signs of mold, heating, or moisture accumulation, and remove any visibly contaminated material immediately.
Good [[coleman-post-harvest-handling-and-storage]] practices are essential for maintaining grain quality and preventing mycotoxin development.

## What About Testing and Detection?

Commercial test kits using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) can detect aflatoxin levels in grain samples within minutes.
More precise laboratory methods like high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) provide exact quantification for regulatory compliance.
Many countries have strict regulatory limits: the US FDA allows 20 parts per billion (ppb) in food for human consumption and 0.5 ppb of M1 in milk.
If you sell crops commercially, periodic testing by an accredited laboratory is essential to protect consumers and limit liability.
Home gardeners and homesteaders should focus on prevention and discard any visibly moldy grain or nuts rather than relying on testing.

## What About Home Gardeners and Small-Scale Growers?

Home gardeners growing sweet corn, peanuts, or tree nuts should harvest promptly at maturity and not leave crops in the field.
Dry all nuts and grains thoroughly before storage — a simple screen drying rack in a warm, well-ventilated area works well.
Sort through harvested nuts and discard any that show staining, discoloration, or signs of insect damage.
Roasting nuts at high temperature does reduce some aflatoxin but cannot eliminate it entirely — prevention is always better than treatment.
For home-scale grain growers, a small fan-forced dryer can reduce moisture to safe levels in a matter of hours.
Consider using [[comparison-solar-dehydrator-vs-electric-dehydrator]] equipment for reliable and consistent drying results.

## What About Aflatoxin in Animal Feed?

Livestock fed contaminated grain can suffer liver damage, reduced growth rates, and immune suppression just like humans.
Dairy animals are a particular concern because they convert aflatoxin B1 into M1, which contaminates milk and cannot be removed by pasteurization.
Poultry are extremely sensitive to aflatoxin — the original [[bloomfield-aspergillus-biology-and-aflatoxins]] research documented mass turkey deaths from contaminated feed.
Pigs and young animals of all species are more susceptible than mature cattle or sheep.
Clay-based binders (hydrated sodium calcium aluminosilicates) can be added to feed to adsorb aflatoxin and reduce absorption in the gut.
However, binders are not a substitute for clean feed — they only partially mitigate contamination and do not address the underlying cause.

## Key Points

- Aflatoxins are produced by [[aspergillus-flavus]] and related species and are among the most potent carcinogens found in nature.
- The [[aflatoxin-turkey-x-disease-1960-discovery-peanut-meal]] outbreak was the event that launched modern mycotoxin awareness.
- Crops most at risk include corn, peanuts, tree nuts, cottonseed, and dried spices grown in warm humid conditions.
- Drought stress and insect damage in the field increase susceptibility to infection significantly.
- Rapid drying to below 13 percent moisture after harvest is the single most effective prevention strategy.
- Proper storage in dry, cool, well-ventilated conditions prevents post-harvest mold growth.
- Non-toxigenic biocontrol strains of [[aspergillus]] can competitively exclude toxin producers in the field.
- The broader impact of [[mycotoxins]] on agriculture and health makes aflatoxin prevention a global food security priority.
- Children and immunocompromised individuals are most vulnerable to chronic aflatoxin exposure.
- Discard any grain, nuts, or spices that show visible mold growth — aflatoxin cannot be destroyed by cooking or processing.
- Dairy animals convert aflatoxin B1 into M1 in their milk, making contaminated feed a direct food safety hazard.
- Home growers should prioritize rapid drying and careful sorting over testing and detection methods.
- The economic impact of aflatoxin contamination on small-scale farmers in tropical regions is devastating and drives ongoing research into resistant crop varieties.
- Ammonia treatment of contaminated grain can reduce aflatoxin levels significantly but is primarily used for animal feed, not human food.
