## How to Attract and Support Native Bee Pollinators

## Why Do Native Bees Matter?

North America is home to over 4,000 described species of native bees, ranging from the familiar fuzzy bumble bee to tiny, jewel-like sweat bees barely larger than a grain of rice. These bees are essential pollinators for wild ecosystems and agricultural crops alike. Many native bee species are more efficient pollinators than honey bees for specific plants — mason bees, for example, pollinate apples and cherries at rates up to 100× more effectively per individual due to their flower-visiting behavior and pollen-carrying anatomy. Native bees pollinate roughly 80% of flowering plants globally, including many crops that honey bees visit less effectively or not at all (tomatoes, eggplants, blueberries, and squash).

Key native bee groups include:
- **Mason bees (Osmia spp.)** — small, metallic-blue or dark bees that nest in pre-existing cavities; superb orchard pollinators.
- **Leafcutter bees (Megachile spp.)** — cut neat circles from leaves to line their nests; vital for alfalfa and other legumes.
- **Sweat bees (Halictidae family)** — often small, brightly colored, and attracted to human sweat for salt; generalist pollinators.
- **Mining bees (Andrenidae family)** — ground-nesting specialists that emerge early in spring to pollinate fruit trees and spring ephemerals.
- **Bumble bees (Bombus spp.)** — large, social bees capable of buzz pollination (sonication), critical for tomatoes, peppers, and blueberries.

## How Do Native Bees Differ from Honey Bees?

| Feature | Honey Bees (*Apis mellifera*) | Native Bees |
|---------|-------------------------------|-------------|
| Origin | Introduced from Europe (1600s) | Native to North America |
| Social structure | Highly social, large colonies (20,000–60,000) | Mostly solitary; bumble bees are the exception (~50–400) |
| Nesting | Hives in cavities, managed boxes | Ground tunnels, hollow stems, dead wood, bare soil |
| Sting | Dies after stinging; aggressive colony defense | Most solitary species are docile and rarely sting |
| Pollination style | Generalist, recruits nestmates via waggle dance | Often specialist or semi-specialist; more efficient individually |
| Overwintering | Colony survives as a cluster | Most overwinter as dormant adults or pupae in nests |
| Honey production | Yes — stores surplus honey | No — females provision single brood cells, then die |

Understanding these differences is crucial: management strategies for honey bees (hive boxes, smoker, swarm control) do not apply to native bees. Supporting native bees means creating habitat, not managing colonies.

## How Do I Create Nesting Habitat?

Approximately 70% of native bee species are **ground-nesting**, and 30% are **cavity-nesting**. Providing for both types is essential.

### Ground-Nesting Bees
- **Leave patches of bare, well-drained soil** — avoid mulching or covering every inch of ground. A south-facing, slightly sloped area with sandy or loamy soil is ideal.
- **Minimize tilling** — tilling destroys existing nests and overwintering tunnels.
- **Avoid compaction** — don't drive vehicles or place heavy objects on potential nesting areas.
- Many mining bees prefer **south- or east-facing slopes** that warm quickly in spring.

### Cavity-Nesting Bees
- **Bee hotels** can work but must be designed correctly: use untreated wood blocks with drilled holes (3–8 mm diameter, 10–15 cm deep), never deeper holes than wide, and replace liners or blocks annually to prevent disease buildup. Avoid cheap bamboo-only hotels that trap moisture and harbor pests.
- **Dead wood and logs** — leave snags, stumps, and fallen branches; beetles and wood-boring insects create tunnels bees later use.
- **Stem bundles** — gather hollow or pithy plant stems (sunflower, raspberry, elderberry, Joe-Pye weed) and bundle them securely, placing them horizontally or at a slight downward angle to shed rain.
- Position cavity nests at **eye level or above**, facing south or southeast, and protect them from wind and rain.

### Key Nesting Tips
- Provide a diversity of cavity sizes (3–10 mm) to support different species.
- Never use treated wood, paint, or chemicals near nesting sites.
- Replace or clean cavity nests every 1–2 years to reduce pathogens and parasites.

## What Should I Plant for Continuous Bloom?

Native bees need nectar and pollen from early spring through late fall. Aim for **at least three species blooming in each season**:

### Spring (March–May)
- Willow (*Salix* spp.) — early pollen source for mining bees and bumble bee queens
- Wild geranium (*Geranium maculatum*)
- Virginia bluebells (*Mertensia virginica*)
- Serviceberry (*Amelanchier* spp.)
- Bloodroot (*[[echinacea-purpurea]]*)
- Black-eyed Susan (*Rudbeckia hirta*)
- Wild bergamot (*[[agastache-foeniculum]]*)
- Common milkweed (*[[helianthus-maximiliani]]*)
- Asters of many species

**Planting principles:**
- Use **native species** adapted to your region — check local native plant societies for ecoregion-specific lists.
- Plant in **drifts and clusters** (3+ of each species) rather than single specimens to make foraging more efficient.
- Include a mix of flower **shapes and colors** — different bee species have different tongue lengths and preferences.
- Allow some **"weeds"** like clover, dandelions, and violets, which are legitimate bee food.

## How Can I Avoid Harming Bees with Pesticides?

- **Eliminate or drastically reduce** pesticide use — this is the single most impactful action.
- If pesticide use is absolutely necessary: apply **only at night** when bees are not foraging, use **granular formulations** rather than sprays, and choose the **least toxic product** available.
- Avoid **neonicotinoids** (imidacloprid, clothianidin, thiamethoxam) — they persist in soil and plant tissues for months to years, contaminating pollen and nectar.
- **Do not spray blooming plants** — ever.
- Encourage natural pest control through **integrated pest management (IPM)**: tolerate some damage, use physical barriers, and support predatory insects.

## How Do I Provide Water and Mud Sources?

- Set out a **shallow dish** filled with pebbles or marbles and fresh water — bees need landing sites to drink without drowning.
- **Mud sources** are critical for mason bees, which use mud to build partition walls between brood cells. Keep a small patch of moist, bare soil near nesting sites.
- Change water frequently to prevent mosquito breeding.
- A **dripping faucet** or slow hose drip creates an excellent natural water source.

## Cavity-Nesting vs. Ground-Nesting: Quick Reference

| Need | Ground-Nesting Bees (~70%) | Cavity-Nesting Bees (~30%) |
|------|---------------------------|---------------------------|
| Substrate | Bare, well-drained soil | Hollow stems, wood tunnels, holes |
| Sun exposure | South-facing slopes preferred | Southeast-facing, sheltered |
| Soil type | Sandy, loamy, well-drained | N/A |
| Materials needed | None (soil itself) | Drilled wood blocks, stem bundles |
| Key threats | Tilling, compaction, mulch | Cleanliness, moisture, parasites |
| Examples | Mining bees, sweat bees, many Andrena | Mason bees, leafcutter bees, small carpenter bees |

## How Can I Monitor and Identify Native Bees?

- **Observation**: Spend 10–15 minutes watching flowers on warm, calm, sunny mornings (above 55°F / 13°C). Note bee size, color, behavior, and flower preference.
- **Photography**: Use a smartphone or macro lens to capture identifying features — body shape, leg hairs, wing venation, facial markings.
- **iNaturalist**: Submit photos for community identification — this also contributes to citizen science databases tracking bee distributions.
- **Field guides**: Recommended resources include *The Bees in Your Backyard* (Wilson & Carril), *Bees: An Up-Close Look at Pollinators Around the World* (Droege), and the Xerces Society's native bee identification resources.
- **Bee bowls**: Set out small, brightly colored bowls (blue, yellow, white) filled with soapy water to passively sample bee diversity for monitoring purposes.
- **Support bee surveys**: Participate in programs like the Xerces Society's community science projects, Bumble Bee Watch, or state-specific pollinator surveys.

## Quick-Start Checklist

- [ ] Leave bare, undisturbed soil patches for ground-nesting bees
- [ ] Install or create cavity-nesting habitat (stem bundles, drilled wood blocks)
- [ ] Plant native flowers for spring, summer, and fall bloom
- [ ] Eliminate or minimize pesticide use
- [ ] Provide a shallow water source with landing stones
- [ ] Keep a mud patch near cavity nests for mason bees
- [ ] Observe and document bees using iNaturalist or a nature journal
- [ ] Share knowledge with neighbors to create connected pollinator corridors
