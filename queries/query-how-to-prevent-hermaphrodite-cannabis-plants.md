# How to Prevent Hermaphrodite Cannabis Plants

## Short Answer
Hermaphrodite cannabis plants develop both male and female sex organs,
usually in response to environmental stress or genetic predisposition.
Prevention focuses on maintaining stable growing conditions, choosing
genetically stable strains, avoiding light leaks during the dark period, and
inspecting plants regularly during early flowering to remove pollen sacs
before they open. Once a hermaphrodite releases pollen, it can pollinate
your entire crop and ruin sinsemilla flowers.

## Detailed Explanation
Cannabis is normally a dioecious species, meaning individual plants are
either male or female. However, cannabis has evolved the ability to develop
both male and female reproductive organs on the same plant as a survival
mechanism when environmental conditions threaten reproduction. In a grow
room, this stress response is problematic because any pollen released by a
hermaphrodite plant will fertilize nearby female flowers, causing them to
produce seeds instead of the premium seedless buds (sinsemilla) that growers
want. Understanding [[cannabis-sex-determination-floral-biology]] is
essential for preventing and managing hermaphroditism.

The two main types of hermaphroditism in cannabis are true hermaphroditism
and rodelization. True hermaphrodites develop distinct male pollen sacs
alongside female pistils, often at the nodes or in the crotches between
branches. Rodelization occurs when a female plant that has gone unpollinated
for an extended period begins producing male flowers in a last-ditch effort
to self-pollinate and reproduce before dying. Both types produce viable
pollen, but true hermaphroditism is more common and usually triggered by
environmental stress. The [[cannabis-hermaphrodite-prevention-and-management]]
page provides detailed identification guides with photos showing the
difference between normal female calyx development and early male pollen sac
formation.

Genetics play a significant role in hermaphrodite tendency. Some cannabis
strains are genetically predisposed to herming, especially certain
sativa-dominant hybrids and unstable crosses. When selecting genetics for
your grow, prioritize strains from reputable breeders with a track record of
stability. Feminized seeds, while convenient, carry a higher risk of
hermaphroditism than regular seeds because the feminization process involves
stressing a female plant to produce male flowers, and this tendency can be
passed to offspring. The [[cannabis-pre-flowering-and-sexing]] guide explains
how to identify plant sex early and how to evaluate genetic stability.

Light stress is the single most common trigger for hermaphroditism in
indoor grows. Cannabis plants use the dark period as a hormonal signal to
remain in flowering mode. Even brief light interruptions during the dark
cycle, such as a leaking door crack, a timer malfunction, or an accidental
light switch flip, can disrupt this hormonal balance and trigger male flower
development. Ensuring complete darkness during the dark period is
non-negotiable for preventing hermaphrodites. The [[cannabis-light-burn]]
page covers how light stress manifests and how to prevent light leaks in
your grow space.

Temperature stress is another major contributor. Temperatures above 85
degrees Fahrenheit or below 60 degrees Fahrenheit during flowering can push
plants into hermaphrodite mode. Rapid temperature fluctuations, such as
turning off exhaust fans at night and letting the room heat up, are
particularly problematic. Consistent temperature control throughout the
entire dark period is essential. The [[cannabis-plant-stress-types-and-recovery]]
page details the full range of environmental stresses that can trigger
hermaphroditism, including nutrient stress, root stress from overwatering or
underwatering, physical damage from training, and pest or disease pressure.

Nutrient imbalances, particularly nitrogen excess during flowering, can also
contribute to herming. High nitrogen levels signal the plant to continue
vegetative growth rather than fully committing to flower production, creating
hormonal confusion that may result in male flower development. Following a
proper feeding schedule that reduces nitrogen and increases phosphorus and
potassium during flowering reduces this risk.

Regular inspection during the first three weeks of flowering is critical for
catching hermaphrodites early. Check every branch node and sugar leaf
junction for the small, round, yellowish pollen sacs that distinguish male
flowers from the white, hairy pistils of female flowers. If caught early,
individual pollen sacs can be carefully removed with tweezers, though this
is a temporary fix and the plant may continue producing more. For plants
that show widespread hermaphrodite development, removal from the grow space
is the safest option to protect the rest of your crop from pollination.

## Key Points
- Maintain complete darkness during the dark period to prevent
  light-stress-induced hermaphroditism
- Choose genetically stable strains and be cautious with feminized seeds
  that may carry herming tendencies
- Keep temperatures consistent between 65 and 80 degrees Fahrenheit
  throughout flowering
- Inspect plants daily during the first three weeks of flowering for early
  pollen sac development
- Remove heavily hermaphrodite plants immediately to protect the rest of
  your crop from pollination
