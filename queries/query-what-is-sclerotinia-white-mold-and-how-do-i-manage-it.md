# What Is Sclerotinia White Mold and How Do I Manage It Organically?

## Short Answer

Sclerotinia white mold, caused primarily by the fungus [[sclerotinia-sclerotiorum]], is a devastating fungal disease that attacks over 400 plant species including beans, tomatoes, lettuce, and brassicas.
It thrives in cool, moist conditions and produces hard, black survival structures called sclerotia that can persist in soil for five years or more.
The disease manifests as cottony white mycelial growth on stems, leaves, and fruit, eventually causing wilting, rotting, and plant death.
Management requires an integrated approach combining cultural practices, biological controls, and environmental manipulation because once established, white mold is extremely difficult to eradicate.

## Detailed Explanation

### What Causes White Mold?

The primary pathogen behind white mold is [[sclerotinia-sclerotiorum]], a necrotrophic ascomycete fungus in the family Sclerotiniaceae.
A closely related species, [[sclerotinia-rolfsii]], causes similar symptoms in warmer climates and is sometimes called southern blight.
Both species produce sclerotia — compact masses of hardened mycelium that serve as long-term survival structures in soil.
Understanding [[fungal-sclerotia-and-resting-structures]] is key to grasping why this disease is so persistent and difficult to eliminate.
When conditions are right, sclerotia germinate either by producing mushroom-like apothecia that release airborne spores or by erupting directly into mycelial growth near the plant base.
Airborne ascospores travel on wind currents and infect plants through dying flower petals, wounded tissue, or senescing leaves.

### Crops Most Susceptible

White mold has an exceptionally broad host range, making crop rotation challenging as a standalone strategy.
The most commonly affected vegetables include beans (especially soybeans and snap beans), tomatoes, peppers, lettuce, cabbage, broccoli, carrots, and celery.
Flowers such as sunflowers, zinnias, and dahlias are also highly susceptible to infection during humid weather.
In permaculture systems, many common food-forest understory plants including brassicas and legumes can harbor the disease.
The entity page for [[sclerotinia-sclerotiorum]] documents its full host range and pathogenicity mechanisms in detail.

### Environmental Conditions That Favor White Mold

Cool temperatures between 60 and 70°F (15–21°C) combined with high humidity above 95% create ideal infection conditions.
Extended periods of leaf wetness lasting 16 hours or more dramatically increase infection rates on susceptible tissue.
Dense plant canopies that trap moisture and restrict airflow are particularly vulnerable to white mold outbreaks.
Poorly drained soils and areas with standing water after rain promote both sclerotia germination and mycelial spread.
Managing the growing environment with principles from [[plant-disease-management-in-organic-gardens]] helps reduce the conditions that trigger outbreaks.

### Prevention Through Cultural Practices

Wider plant spacing is the single most effective cultural practice because it improves air circulation and reduces canopy humidity.
Using raised beds improves drainage and keeps the root zone drier, reducing conditions favorable for sclerotia germination.
Crop rotation is valuable but must avoid all susceptible species for at least three to four years to allow sclerotia to degrade naturally.
Detailed guidance on effective rotation strategies can be found at [[crop-rotation-vegetable-garden]] and [[crop-rotation-principles]].
Removing and destroying infected plant debris immediately prevents sclerotia from returning to the soil.
Drip irrigation keeps foliage dry by delivering water directly to the root zone, a practice detailed in [[drip-irrigation-and-microirrigation]].
Using [[cervantes-mulch-outdoor-moisture-conservation]] techniques strategically can help, but avoid excessive mulch directly against stems where moisture accumulates.

### Biological Control Agents

Several biological control organisms show genuine efficacy against Sclerotinia species by directly parasitizing sclerotia in the soil.
[[coniothyrium-minitans]] is a mycoparasitic fungus specifically marketed for white mold control that attacks and destroys sclerotia over time.
[[trichoderma-harzianum]] and other Trichoderma species act as both mycoparasites and inducers of plant defense responses.
The [[beneficial-fungi-trichoderma-and-mycorrhizae]] concept page explains how these organisms compete with pathogens for resources and space.
[[bacillus-subtilis]] produces antibiotic compounds that suppress Sclerotinia growth and can be applied as a foliar spray or soil drench.
Commercial biofungicide products containing these organisms should be applied preventively before disease onset for maximum effectiveness.
The principles behind [[biodiversity-of-fungi-plant-health-pathogens-biocontrol]] demonstrate why diverse soil microbial communities naturally suppress pathogens.

### Organic Sprays and Treatments

Copper-based fungicides provide moderate protection against white mold when applied preventively to foliage before infection.
[[cervantes-copper-micronutrient-management]] discusses the careful balance needed because excess copper can accumulate in soil and harm beneficial organisms.
Neem oil, detailed in [[cervantes-neem-oil-organic-pest-control]], has antifungal properties that can slow early infections when caught promptly.
Compost tea applications may suppress white mold through microbial competition, as discussed in [[compost-tea-specific-disease-management]].
Potassium bicarbonate sprays create an alkaline surface on leaves that inhibits spore germination of many fungal pathogens.
Regular applications of [[query-how-to-make-and-use-plant-based-foliar-sprays-for-pest-and-disease-prevention]] can help maintain a protective microbial layer on foliage.

### Managing Outbreaks After Detection

Once white mold is detected, immediately remove and bag the entire infected plant including surrounding soil to capture scattered sclerotia.
Do not compost infected material because home composting temperatures may not be sufficient to kill sclerotia.
Spray neighboring plants with a preventive fungicide to protect them from spreading mycelium and airborne spores.
Reduce irrigation frequency and improve ventilation using fans in greenhouse or high-tunnel environments.
Soil solarization during hot summer months can reduce sclerotia populations in the top few inches of soil.
For severe infestations, consider the approach in [[query-how-do-i-prevent-and-manage-plant-diseases-organically]] for a full integrated response.

### Long-Term Soil Management

Building soil health through regular additions of organic matter encourages diverse microbial communities that naturally suppress Sclerotinia.
Incorporating cover crops from non-susceptible families, such as small grains or grasses, helps break the disease cycle.
The relationship between soil biology and disease suppression is explored in [[plant-disease-ecology-and-organic-management]].
Mycorrhizal fungi, as described in [[arbuscular-mycorrhizae-pathogen-protection]], can improve plant vigor and disease resistance.
Avoiding excessive nitrogen fertilization prevents the lush, soft growth that white mold easily colonizes.
Regular soil testing and balanced fertility keep plants healthy enough to resist or tolerate moderate disease pressure.

White mold management fits within an integrated approach that combines all available tools rather than relying on any single method.

## Key Points

- Sclerotinia white mold attacks 400+ plant species and persists in soil for 5+ years via hardened sclerotia
- Cool temperatures (60–70°F) and high humidity above 95% trigger infection through airborne spores or direct mycelial growth
- Wider spacing, drip irrigation, and improved airflow are the most effective preventive cultural practices
- Biological controls including [[coniothyrium-minitans]] and [[trichoderma-harzianum]] parasitize and destroy sclerotia in soil
- Copper fungicides, neem oil, and compost tea offer moderate organic protection when applied preventively
- Remove and destroy infected plants immediately — never compost material with white mold
- Rotate to non-susceptible crops (grains and grasses) for at least three to four years
- Build diverse soil microbial communities through organic matter additions for long-term suppression
