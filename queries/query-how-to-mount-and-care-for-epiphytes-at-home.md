## How to Mount and Care for Epiphytes at Home

## What Is Mounting?

Mounting means growing an epiphyte on a piece of wood, bark, or stone instead of in a pot — mimicking how these plants grow in nature. Mounted plants receive superior [[cannabis-root-rot-prevention-and-treatment]]. They also make stunning naturalistic displays. The tradeoff is that mounted plants dry out faster and require more frequent watering than potted equivalents.

## Choosing the Right Epiphyte for Mounting

Not all epiphytes are equally suited to mounting. Here's what works best:

**Excellent candidates:**
- Tillandsia (all species) — require mounting; they cannot grow in soil
- Staghorn ferns (*Platycerium*) — naturally grow on tree trunks
- *Vanda*, *Ascocenda*, and vandaceous orchids — prefer bare-root or mounted culture
- Orchids with thick roots: *Cattleya*, *Dendrobium*, *Oncidium*, *Bulbophyllum*
- Small orchids: *Masdevallia*, *Dracula*, *Pleurothallis*
- Bird's nest fern (*[[asplenium-nidus]]*) — smaller specimens
- *Rhipsalis* and other epiphytic cacti
- Dischidia and Hoya species with climbing/vining habits

**Possible but challenging:**
- *Phalaenopsis* — can be mounted but need more frequent watering than most growers expect
- Large bromeliads (*Aechmea*, *Neoregelia*) — heavy; need sturdy mounts
- *Anthurium* and *Philodendron* — work best with a moisture-retaining pad

## Selecting Mount Materials

### Cork Bark

The gold standard for mounting. Portuguese cork bark is widely available, attractive, naturally water-resistant, and lasts 5-10+ years. It has a rough texture that roots grip easily. Available in flat plaques, tubes, and irregular chunks. Medium-to-thick pieces (1-2 cm) are ideal — thin cork dries too fast.

### Tree Fern Fiber

Tree fern (*Dicksonia* or *Cyathea*) fiber panels are excellent moisture retainers. They hold water longer than cork, making them better for moisture-loving species. However, they decompose faster (2-4 years) and can be harder to source sustainably. Tree fern totems (cylindrical) work well for climbing aroids and orchids.

### Driftwood

Natural, attractive, and free if you live near water. Must be thoroughly cleaned (boil or bake) to remove salt, algae, and potential pathogens. Hardwoods (oak, maple, manzanita) last longer than softwoods. Avoid treated lumber or wood from polluted areas. Crevices and rough bark provide good root attachment points.

### Other Options

- **Coconut husk**: Cheap and sustainable; good moisture retention but less aesthetic.
- **Grape wood/vine**: Light and natural-looking; decomposes in 2-3 years.
- **Cedar shakes or planks**: Durable and rot-resistant; may release oils some plants dislike.
- **Stone/rock**: Lava rock, pumice, or rough slate work for some species. Drill or wire-wrap for attachment.
- **Wire mesh baskets**: Not technically mounting, but provide the same root-air exposure. Great for *Vanda* and *Paphiopedilum*.

## Tools and Supplies Needed

- Mounting material (cork bark, driftwood, etc.)
- Plant to mount
- Sphagnum moss (small amount for moisture pad)
- Fishing line, thin wire, or nylon pantyhose strips for tying
- Non-toxic waterproof adhesive (E6000, hot glue for temporary hold, or liquid nails)
- Wire cutters or scissors
- Pliers
- Optional: decorative moss for aesthetics (preserved sheet moss)

## Step-by-Step Mounting Process

### Step 1: Prepare the Mount

Clean the mounting surface. For driftwood, scrub with a stiff brush and boil for 30 minutes, then air dry completely. For cork, brush off any loose debris. If using a moisture pad, soak sphagnum moss in water for 15 minutes, wring out until damp (not dripping), and place a golf-ball-sized mound where the plant will sit.

### Step 2: Prepare the Plant

Remove the plant from its current pot or medium. Gently rinse off old potting material from the roots. Trim away any dead or rotten roots (brown, mushy) with sterilized scissors. Leave healthy roots (firm, white or green, with visible velamen) intact. For Tillandsia, no root preparation is needed.

### Step 3: Position the Plant

Place the plant on the mount with the roots spread across the surface. The growing point (crown) should face upward and outward. Position the plant so it will grow in a natural direction — generally angled slightly outward, not pointing straight up or down. For orchids, orient the plant the same way it was growing in its pot.

### Step 4: Secure the Plant

**Fishing line method** (preferred — invisible, strong, easy to remove later): Wrap fishing line tightly around the plant and mount in a figure-8 pattern, crossing over the root mass. Make at least 3-4 wraps. Tie off securely. The line will eventually be hidden by [[soil-moisture-management-and-root-growth]].

**Wire method**: Use thin aluminum or copper wire. Wrap around roots and mount, twisting ends to secure. Less aesthetic but very secure for heavy plants.

**Adhesive method**: Apply a small dab of E6000 or hot glue to the base of the plant (avoid getting adhesive on roots). Press against the mount and hold for 30-60 seconds. Glue is supplementary — use fishing line or wire for actual [[comparison-silicon-vs-calcium-in-plant-defense-and-structural-support]]. Hot glue is temporary and will release after 6-12 months, by which time the plant should have attached naturally.

### Step 5: Attach a Hanger

Drill a small hole or screw an eyehook into the back/top of the mount. Use wire, chain, or fishing line to create a hanger. For large mounts, use heavy-duty picture hanging wire rated for the weight.

### Step 6: Initial Care Period

For the first 2-4 weeks, keep the mounted plant in a sheltered location with high humidity (70-90%). Mist daily. Water the sphagnum pad whenever it begins to dry. Do not fertilize during establishment. Once new root growth is visible attaching to the mount, the plant has successfully established and can be moved to its permanent location.

## Watering Mounted Plants

Mounted plants dry faster than potted plants. As a general rule, water 2-3 times more frequently than you would the same plant in a pot.

- **Submerge method** (best for Tillandsia and bare-root orchids): Fill a bucket or sink with room-temperature water. Submerge the entire mount for 10-20 minutes. Remove and let drain completely. Do this 1-3 times per week depending on species and conditions.
- **Drench method** (for mounts with sphagnum): Hold the mount under running water or pour water over the root area until saturated. Let drain. Repeat when the moss approaches dryness.
- **Misting**: Use a spray bottle to mist daily between thorough waterings. Misting alone is not sufficient — mounted plants need deep soaking regularly.

**Critical rule**: Never let water sit in the crown of a mounted plant (especially orchids and bromeliads). Always mount with the growing tip angled slightly downward so water drains away.

## Humidity Management

Indoor environments are often too dry for mounted epiphytes (typical home humidity is 30-40%; most epiphytes prefer 50-80%). Solutions:

- **Humidity trays**: Fill shallow trays with pebbles and water; set mounts above the waterline. Effective for small collections.
- **Room humidifiers**: The most reliable solution for larger collections.
- **Grouping**: Cluster mounted plants together to create a shared microclimate of elevated humidity.
- **Bathrooms and kitchens**: Naturally higher humidity; great locations for mounted epiphytes if light is adequate.
- **Glass cases or terrariums**: Ideal for miniature mounted orchids and Tillandsia. Provide ventilation to [[liquid-fertilizer-from-weeds]] applied during regular watering. For mounted orchids: ¼ strength balanced fertilizer (20-20-20) once per week during active growth, reduced to monthly in winter. For Tillandsia: Submerge in fertilizer solution (¼ strength) for 30 minutes once or twice per month. Avoid urea-based fertilizers — epiphytes absorb nitrates and ammonium more readily.
