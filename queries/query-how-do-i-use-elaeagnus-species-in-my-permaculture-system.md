# How do I use Elaeagnus species in my permaculture system?

## Short Answer

Elaeagnus species — including [[elaeagnus-umbellata|autumn olive]], [[elaeagnus-multiflora|goumi]], and [[elaeagnus-angustifolia|Russian olive]] — are versatile nitrogen-fixing shrubs that serve multiple functions in [[permaculture]] design.
They produce edible berries, fix atmospheric nitrogen through a [[actinorhizal-symbiosis-frankia|symbiotic relationship with Frankia bacteria]], provide wildlife habitat, and act as effective windbreaks and hedgerow plants.
Their ability to thrive in poor soils makes them excellent [[pioneer-species-in-permaculture-design|pioneer species]] for land rehabilitation and food forest establishment.
Choosing the right species for your climate and goals is key to maximizing benefits while minimizing invasiveness risks.
This guide covers species selection, guild integration, propagation, and management strategies for permaculture systems.

## The Three Main Elaeagnus Species

[[elaeagnus-umbellata|Autumn olive (E. umbellata)]] is the most widely naturalized species, producing abundant red-speckled berries high in lycopene and antioxidants.
It grows aggressively and can reach 3-5 meters tall, making it useful for rapid site establishment but requiring management to prevent unwanted spread.
[[elaeagnus-multiflora|Goumi (E. multiflora)]] is the preferred choice for most permaculture plantings because it stays more compact at 1.5-2.5 meters and produces sweeter, tastier fruit.
[[elaeagnus-angustifolia|Russian olive (E. angustifolia)]] is the largest of the three, reaching 6-10 meters, and is best suited for [[windbreak-design-and-configuration|windbreaks]] and [[living-fences-and-hedgerows|hedgerows]] in cold, dry climates.

## Nitrogen Fixation and Soil Building

All Elaeagnus species form root nodules hosting [[frankia|Frankia actinomycetes]], which convert atmospheric nitrogen into plant-available forms through [[actinorhizal-symbiosis-frankia|actinorhizal symbiosis]].
This differs from legume nitrogen fixation, which relies on rhizobia bacteria.
A mature Elaeagnus shrub can fix 50-200 kg of nitrogen per hectare per year, significantly boosting soil fertility for neighboring plants.
The nitrogen-rich leaf litter makes them excellent companions in [[food-forest-design|food forest design]], where they feed [[dynamic-accumulator-plants-nitrogen|nitrogen-accumulating guild partners]].
Use [[chop-and-drop-mulching|chop-and-drop techniques]] to cycle the nitrogen directly into the soil around fruit trees and other crop plants.
This technique pairs well with [[cover-crop-multifunction-design|multifunction cover crop designs]] to build soil rapidly on degraded sites.

## Edible Berries and Human Use

Goumi berries are the most palatable, with a tart-sweet flavor suitable for fresh eating, jams, and wines.
Autumn olive berries are more astringent but are exceptionally high in lycopene — up to 17 times more than tomatoes.
Russian olive produces small, mealy fruits that are edible but less desirable, typically used for wildlife forage rather than human consumption.
All species can be processed into fruit leather, jelly, or fermented beverages.
The berries also attract birds and other wildlife, supporting [[bird-habitat-creation|bird habitat]] goals in your permaculture design.
In Asian cuisine, goumi berries are traditionally dried and used in teas and medicinal preparations.
The astringency of autumn olive berries decreases after a frost, making late-season harvests more palatable for fresh eating.
Processing berries into juice concentrates the lycopene content, creating a nutrient-dense functional food.

## Food Forest Guild Design

Elaeagnus species fit naturally into the shrub layer of [[forest-garden-seven-layers|forest garden designs]].
Plant them as [[apple-tree-guild-design-species-combinations|companion shrubs in apple tree guilds]] where their nitrogen fixation directly benefits the fruit tree.
In [[backyard-food-forest-design|backyard food forests]], goumi works well as a mid-story plant between tall nut or fruit trees and ground-level herbs.
Their dense branching provides shade for shade-tolerant understory plants and creates [[companion-planting-guild|companion planting guild]] microclimates.
Combine Elaeagnus with [[dynamic-accumulators|dynamic accumulator plants]] and [[companion-planting|companion planting]] strategies for a self-fertilizing guild.
Follow [[guild-design-methods|guild design methods]] to position Elaeagnus on the sunny edge of tree canopies where they receive enough light to produce berries.

## Windbreaks, Hedgerows, and Living Fences

[[elaeagnus-angustifolia|Russian olive]] is particularly valuable for [[windbreak-design-and-strategy|windbreak design]] in cold, arid regions due to its drought tolerance and rapid growth.
The dense, thorny branches of most Elaeagnus species make them effective [[living-fences-and-hedgerows|living fence]] components that contain livestock and exclude deer.
For [[coastal-windbreak-systems-and-plant-wind-tolerance|coastal windbreaks]], autumn olive tolerates salt spray and poor sandy soils.
Use them as nurse plants in [[alley-cropping|alley cropping]] systems where they shelter young crop trees until the canopy closes.
In [[silvopasture-systems|silvopasture designs]], Elaeagnus hedges provide livestock shelter and emergency forage during drought periods.

## Land Rehabilitation and Degraded Sites

Elaeagnus species excel at [[soil-building-permaculture|soil building]] on degraded, compacted, or nutrient-poor sites.
They tolerate pH ranges from 5.5 to 8.0, making them suitable for both acidic mine spoils and alkaline prairie soils.
As [[pioneer-species-for-land-reclamation|pioneer species for land reclamation]], they prepare the ground for more demanding crop plants by building organic matter and fixing nitrogen.
They pair effectively with [[hugelkultur|hugelkultur beds]] on severely degraded land, providing the initial nitrogen input to jumpstart decomposition.
Their deep root systems stabilize slopes and prevent [[ecological-succession-in-permaculture-design|erosion during early succession stages]].

## What About Propagation and Establishment?

Elaeagnus species can be propagated from seed, hardwood cuttings, or layering, with stratification improving germination rates for autumn olive and Russian olive.
Hardwood cuttings taken in late winter root reliably for goumi, making it the easiest species to propagate at home.
Space plants 1.5-3 meters apart for hedgerow plantings and 3-5 meters apart in [[food-forest-layers-and-species|food forest layers]].
Young plants benefit from mulch and occasional watering during their first year but become remarkably drought-tolerant once established.
They begin producing fruit within 2-4 years from seed and even sooner from vegetatively propagated stock.
Avoid planting near septic drain fields, as the aggressive root systems can invade pipes.

## What About Integration with Other Permaculture Systems?

Elaeagnus integrates well with [[agroforestry-and-alley-cropping|alley cropping]] layouts where rows of shrubs alternate with crop alleys.
In [[silvopasture-systems-design-and-management|silvopasture management]], Elaeagnus hedges divide pastures into paddocks while providing shade and emergency fodder.
Combine them with [[cover-crops-and-green-manures|green manure cover crops]] during establishment for rapid soil improvement on degraded sites.
The flowers provide early-season nectar for pollinators, complementing [[pollinator-gardening|pollinator garden]] plantings that bloom later in the season.
Use Elaeagnus as the shrub layer in [[coppice-and-pollard-systems|coppice systems]] where periodic cutting provides biomass for mulch or fuel.
Their dense growth also supports [[companion-planting-and-polycultures|polyculture designs]] by creating protected microclimates for tender herbs and vegetables.

## What About Invasiveness Concerns?

Autumn olive ([[elaeagnus-umbellata|E. umbellata]]) is considered invasive in many parts of eastern North America and several US states have banned its sale.
Goumi ([[elaeagnus-multiflora|E. multiflora]]) is generally not considered invasive and is the safest choice for most permaculture plantings.
Russian olive ([[elaeagnus-angustifolia|E. angustifolia]]) is invasive in riparian areas of the western US but well-behaved in drier eastern sites.
Mitigate spread risk by harvesting berries before birds distribute them, selecting non-invasive goumi cultivars, and monitoring for volunteer seedlings.
Check your local noxious weed list before planting any Elaeagnus species.
When in doubt, choose cultivated goumi varieties which are widely available from specialty nurseries.

## Key Points

- Elaeagnus species fix nitrogen through [[actinorhizal-symbiosis-frankia|Frankia symbiosis]], unlike legumes that use rhizobia bacteria.
- [[elaeagnus-multiflora|Goumi]] is the best choice for most permaculture systems due to its compact size, tasty fruit, and low invasiveness risk.
- Use [[chop-and-drop-mulching|chop-and-drop]] on pruned branches to cycle nitrogen back into the soil for neighboring plants.
- Elaeagnus fits the shrub layer in [[forest-garden-layers|forest garden designs]] and works well in [[guild-design-species-interactions|guild plantings]] with fruit trees.
- Dense, thorny growth makes them effective [[living-fences-and-hedgerows|living fence]] and [[windbreak-design-establishment|windbreak]] components.
- They are excellent [[pioneer-species-nuclear-reforestation|pioneer plants]] for [[soil-building-ecological-garden|soil building]] on degraded land.
- Autumn olive berries contain up to 17 times more lycopene than tomatoes, making them a nutrient-dense food crop.
- Always check local regulations regarding [[elaeagnus-umbellata|autumn olive]] and [[elaeagnus-angustifolia|Russian olive]] before planting.
- Propagate goumi easily from hardwood cuttings in late winter for the most reliable results.
- Space plants 1.5-3 meters apart for hedgerows and 3-5 meters apart when integrating into [[food-forest-plant-palette|food forest palettes]].
- Early-spring flowers provide critical nectar sources for pollinators before most other shrubs bloom.
