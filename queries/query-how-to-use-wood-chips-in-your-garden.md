# How to Use Wood Chips in Your Garden

## Short Answer
Wood chips are one of the most effective mulching materials for building
long-term soil health. They suppress weeds, retain moisture, moderate soil
temperature, and feed fungal networks as they decompose. Use them as a surface
mulch two to four inches thick around established plants, avoiding direct
contact with stems, and source arborist wood chips that include bark, wood, and
leaves for the best results.

## Detailed Explanation
Wood chips have gained enormous popularity among gardeners and permaculturists
thanks in large part to the work of Paul Gautschi, whose back-to-eden approach
demonstrated that wood chip mulch can transform depleted soil into rich, dark,
fertile ground over time. Unlike finer mulches like shredded bark or sawdust,
arborist wood chips contain a mix of wood pieces, bark, twigs, and often leaves
from tree trimming operations. This diversity in particle size and composition
is what makes them so effective as a
[[cervantes-mulch-outdoor-moisture-conservation]].

The primary benefit of wood chip mulch is moisture retention. A four-inch layer
of wood chips can reduce soil moisture evaporation by up to 70 percent compared
to bare soil. This means less frequent watering, deeper moisture penetration,
and more consistent soil moisture levels that benefit beneficial soil organisms.
During hot summer months, the mulch layer also keeps root zones significantly
cooler, protecting shallow roots from heat stress. In winter, wood chips
insulate the soil against freezing temperatures, reducing frost damage to
perennial root systems.

Weed suppression is another major advantage. A properly applied wood chip mulch
layer blocks sunlight from reaching weed seeds in the soil surface, preventing
germination of most annual weeds. Perennial weeds with established root systems
may push through thin mulch layers, but a thick application of four to six
inches will suppress even aggressive species over time. The key is to apply
mulch thickly enough to block light while still allowing air and water to
penetrate. Compacted or matted wood chips can create a water-repellent barrier,
so replenish the layer annually as the older material breaks down.

The decomposition process of wood chips is what makes them uniquely valuable for
soil building. As wood chips break down, they feed fungal networks in the soil
rather than bacterial communities. This fungal-dominated decomposition pathway
produces humic and fulvic acids that improve soil structure, increase
water-holding capacity, and create stable humus. Over several years, a
wood-chip-mulched garden develops a dark, crumbly top layer of humus that is
rich in organic matter and teeming with beneficial fungi. This aligns with the
[[comparison-soil-food-web-vs-bacterial-vs-fungal-soil]] spectrum, where woody
materials favor fungal dominance that benefits perennial plants and trees.

There is a persistent myth that wood chips rob nitrogen from the soil as they
decompose. While it is true that the microorganisms breaking down wood chips
require nitrogen to do their work, this nitrogen is drawn from the mulch layer
itself, not from the soil beneath. As long as wood chips are used as a surface
mulch and not tilled into the soil, nitrogen tie-up is not a concern for
established plants. The decomposition interface between the wood chips and the
soil surface is where the magic happens: fungal hyphae bridge the gap,
transporting nutrients between the decomposing mulch and plant roots.

For vegetable gardens, wood chips work best around established perennials, fruit
trees, and shrubs. They can also be used in annual vegetable beds, but some
growers prefer to remove the chips at planting time and replace them after
seedlings are established. Very fine seeds like lettuce and carrots may struggle
to germinate through a thick chip layer. In these cases, leave a small circle of
bare soil around the planting area and apply chips between rows. For
[[comparison-sheet-mulching-vs-chop-and-drop]] enthusiasts, wood chips complement
green mulch materials by providing long-lasting ground cover that breaks down
slowly.

Avoid common mistakes when using wood chips. Never pile them directly against
tree trunks or plant stems, as this creates a moist environment that promotes
bark rot and fungal disease. Leave a two to three inch gap between the mulch and
any woody stem. Do not use wood chips from diseased trees, particularly trees
killed by oak wilt, Dutch elm disease, or other vascular pathogens, as these can
spread to healthy plants. Black walnut chips should also be avoided because they
contain juglone, a natural compound toxic to many garden plants. For
permaculture applications, wood chips integrate well with
hugelkultur|Hugelkultur beds and [[sheet-mulching-guide]] as part
of a broader soil-building strategy.

## Key Points
- Apply wood chips two to four inches deep as a surface mulch, never tilling
  them into soil
- Leave a gap between mulch and plant stems to prevent rot and disease
- Source mixed arborist wood chips containing bark, wood, and leaves for best
  results
- Wood chips feed fungal soil networks and build long-term soil humus
- Nitrogen tie-up is a myth for surface-applied chips, but avoid using diseased
  or walnut wood
