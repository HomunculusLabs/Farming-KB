# How Do I Make Tempeh at Home?

## Short Answer

Tempeh is a traditional Indonesian fermented food made by inoculating cooked soybeans with the mold [[rhizopus-oligosporus]] and incubating them until a firm, white mycelium cake forms.
Making tempeh at home requires only dried soybeans, a tempeh starter culture, vinegar, and a warm incubation space maintained around 30°C (86°F) for 24 to 48 hours.
The process is straightforward but demands attention to hygiene, proper bean preparation, and temperature control to ensure successful fermentation.

## Detailed Explanation

### What Is Tempeh?

Tempeh originated in Indonesia and is one of the oldest fermented soybean foods in the world, with records dating back several centuries.
Unlike tofu, which is made from coagulated soy milk, tempeh uses whole soybeans bound together by a dense mat of [[rhizopus]] mycelium.
The fermentation process partially breaks down the soybeans, making their protein, vitamins, and minerals more bioavailable than in unfermented beans.
Tempeh is a cornerstone of [[miso-tempeh-and-soybean-fermentation]] traditions across Southeast Asia and has gained worldwide popularity as a nutritious [[soybean]]-based protein source.

### Ingredients and Equipment

You need dried [[soybean|soybeans]], white vinegar, and tempeh starter containing [[rhizopus-oligosporus]] spores.
A large pot for boiling and a steamer for dehydrating the beans are essential, along with perforated ziplock bags or banana leaves for shaping.
An incubation chamber such as a dehydrator, oven with a light on, or a warm spot near a heater is needed to maintain 29–32°C (85–90°F).
A thermometer is critical because temperatures above 35°C can kill the mold, while temperatures below 25°C slow fermentation dramatically.
Unlike [[koji-and-amylase-fermentation]], which uses Aspergillus oryzae, tempeh relies on Rhizopus species that thrive in slightly warmer tropical conditions.

### Step-by-Step Process

First, soak the soybeans in water for 8 to 12 hours or overnight until fully hydrated and plump.
Next, dehull the beans by rubbing them between your hands or using a mechanical splitter, then float the hulls off in water — dehulling is important because hulls contain compounds that inhibit mycelium growth.
Boil the dehulled beans for 30 minutes until tender but not mushy, then drain and spread them on a clean towel to surface-dry.
Add one tablespoon of white vinegar per kilogram of beans to lower the pH and prevent unwanted bacterial contamination during the early stages of fermentation.
Once the beans are cooled to below 35°C and surface-dry, sprinkle the tempeh starter evenly over them and mix thoroughly to distribute the spores.
Pack the inoculated beans into perforated bags about two to three centimeters thick, ensuring even distribution and avoiding air pockets.
Place the packed bags in your incubation space at 30°C with moderate humidity, and within 24 to 48 hours the white mycelium will knit the beans into a solid cake.

### Common Problems and Troubleshooting

Black or dark gray patches usually indicate sporulation from temperature spikes or insufficient ventilation — the tempeh is still edible but may taste bitter.
Slime or foul odors signal bacterial contamination, often caused by wet beans, poor hygiene, or incubation temperatures above 38°C.
Incomplete mycelium coverage with bare spots typically means the beans were too wet, the starter was old or under-applied, or airflow was inadequate.
Yellowing tempeh is normal near the end of fermentation and often indicates the cake is ready to harvest.
These issues mirror common challenges in other [[fermented-foods-and-beverages]], where cleanliness and environmental control determine success.

### What About Tempeh Made from Other Beans?

While traditional tempeh uses soybeans, you can make it from chickpeas, black beans, lupini beans, or even a mix of grains and legumes.
Non-soy tempeh generally has a different texture and nutritional profile, often with less protein density per gram.
Some producers mix soybeans with grains like [[soybean-meal]] byproducts or locally available legumes for flavor variation.
The [[rhizopus-oligosporus]] strain is versatile enough to colonize most cooked legumes, though incubation times may differ slightly from standard soy tempeh.

### What About Temperature and Climate Considerations?

Tropical and subtropical climates are naturally suited for tempeh production, requiring little or no artificial incubation.
In temperate or cold climates, maintaining 30°C reliably is the primary challenge and usually requires a dedicated incubation setup.
Seasonal temperature fluctuations mean summer tempeh may finish in 24 hours while winter batches need closer to 48 hours.
Compared to [[koji-culture-and-fermentation]], which ferments at a cooler 25–30°C, tempeh demands the upper end of that range consistently.

### What About Storing and Using Homemade Tempeh?

Fresh tempeh keeps in the refrigerator for three to five days and freezes well for up to three months when sliced and vacuum-sealed.
Before eating, steam or blanch the tempeh for ten minutes to deactivate enzymes and improve digestibility — this is standard practice in Indonesian cuisine.
Pan-fry, grill, crumble into stir-fries, or marinate sliced tempeh in soy sauce and maple syrup for a versatile main dish.
Homemade tempeh often has a richer, nuttier flavor than store-bought versions because it is fresher and uses fewer processing steps.
Tempeh can also be dehydrated for long-term storage, similar to techniques described for [[comparison-dehydration-vs-lacto-fermentation]].

### What About Tempeh Nutrition Compared to Other Plant Proteins?

A 100-gram serving of tempeh provides approximately 19 grams of protein, making it one of the most protein-dense plant foods available.
The fermentation process increases the bioavailability of iron, calcium, and B vitamins, particularly B12 in some commercially produced tempeh.
Tempeh contains all nine essential amino acids, giving it a complete protein profile comparable to animal sources but without cholesterol.
The mycelium also produces natural antioxidants and vitamin K2 during fermentation, nutrients largely absent from unfermented [[soybean]] products.
Compared to [[fermented-dairy-and-cultured-milk-products]], tempeh is dairy-free, lower in fat, and suitable for vegan and lactose-intolerant diets.
The fiber content of tempeh supports gut health, and the fermentation process reduces oligosaccharides that cause gas from raw beans.

### What About Scaling Up Tempeh Production?

Home-scale tempeh makers typically produce one to two kilograms per batch using ziplock bags or banana leaf wrappers.
Small commercial operations use stainless steel trays in climate-controlled incubation rooms for consistent, larger batches.
Consistent airflow is critical at scale — crowded trays restrict oxygen and can cause uneven mycelium growth or contamination hot spots.
Sanitation protocols become increasingly important as batch size grows, similar to scaling considerations in [[fermentation-microbiology-for-brewers]].
Many community-scale producers source starter cultures from specialized suppliers to ensure reliable spore viability and consistent results.
Automation with temperature controllers, humidity sensors, and forced-air circulation can dramatically improve yield and quality at commercial scales.

## Key Points

- Tempeh is made by fermenting cooked, dehulled [[soybean|soybeans]] with [[rhizopus-oligosporus]] at 30°C for 24 to 48 hours.
- Proper bean dehydration and a slightly acidic pH (using vinegar) are essential to prevent contamination.
- [[rhizopus]] mycelium binds the beans into a firm, nutritious cake rich in bioavailable protein and B vitamins.
- [[miso-tempeh-and-soybean-fermentation]] represents a broader family of traditional Asian fermentation techniques.
- Hygiene, temperature control, and airflow are the three pillars of successful tempeh production.
- Dehulling soybeans before fermentation is critical because the hulls contain compounds that inhibit mycelial growth.
- Fresh homemade tempeh can be refrigerated for up to five days or frozen for three months without significant quality loss.
- Homemade tempeh differs from [[comparison-tempeh-vs-tofu|tofu]] in texture, flavor, and nutritional profile due to whole-bean fermentation.
- Compared to [[comparison-tempeh-vs-miso|miso]], tempeh is faster to make and requires no salt or long aging period.
- Tempeh can be made from legumes other than soybeans, though protein content and texture will vary.
- Tempeh production at home is cost-effective, typically yielding two to three times the quantity of store-bought for the same price.

### What About the Cultural Origins of Tempeh?

Tempeh has been a dietary staple in Java, Indonesia for centuries, with some historical accounts dating it to the 12th or 13th century.
The traditional process used wild [[rhizopus]] spores from banana leaves or hibiscus leaves as natural wrappers and inoculation surfaces.
Indonesian home cooks developed the technique as a way to preserve and enhance the nutritional value of harvested soybeans in tropical conditions.
The word "tempeh" likely derives from the Javanese term for fermented food, reflecting its deep integration into regional culinary traditions.
Unlike [[comparison-wild-fermentation-vs-commercial-starter-culture|wild fermentation]], modern tempeh production typically uses purified starter cultures for consistency.
Tempeh spread globally in the 20th century through Indonesian diaspora communities and was embraced by the Western natural foods movement in the 1970s.
