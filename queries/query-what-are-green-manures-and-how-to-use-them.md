# What Are Green Manures and How to Use Them

## Short Answer
Green manures are crops grown primarily to improve soil health rather than for
harvest. They are typically incorporated into the soil while still green,
adding organic matter, fixing atmospheric nitrogen, suppressing weeds,
preventing erosion, and breaking pest cycles. Green manures are a cornerstone
of organic and sustainable farming, providing many of the benefits of synthetic
fertilizers and herbicides through natural biological processes.

## Detailed Explanation

### Green Manures vs. Cover Crops
Green manures and cover crops are closely related terms that are often used
interchangeably, but they have a subtle distinction. A cover crop is any crop
grown to protect and improve soil between cash crop cycles, while a green
manure specifically refers to a crop that is intentionally turned into the
soil while still green and succulent to add organic matter and nutrients. In
practice, most cover crops serve as green manures when they are terminated and
incorporated. The [[solomon-cover-crops-green-manure-guide]] provides
comprehensive variety recommendations and management guidelines for home
gardeners and small-scale farmers.

### Nitrogen Fixation: Growing Your Own Fertilizer
The nitrogen-fixing function is the most celebrated benefit of leguminous
green manures. Plants in the legume family, including clover, vetch, peas,
beans, alfalfa, and lupine, form symbiotic relationships with Rhizobium
bacteria that convert atmospheric nitrogen gas into ammonium, a form plants
can use. When the legume green manure is terminated and decomposes, this
biologically fixed nitrogen becomes available to the subsequent crop. The
[[urban-permaculture-nitrogen-fixing-green-manures]] approach integrates
nitrogen-fixing green manures into urban garden rotations, demonstrating that
even small garden spaces can significantly reduce their dependence on imported
nitrogen fertilizers.

### Building Soil Organic Matter
Beyond nitrogen fixation, green manures build soil organic matter. When a dense
stand of green manure is terminated and decomposes, it adds substantial
quantities of carbon to the soil in the form of plant residues, root exudates,
and associated microbial biomass. This organic matter improves soil structure,
water-holding capacity, cation exchange capacity, and biological activity. The
[[teaming-with-microbes-cover-crops-soil-health]] research shows that cover
crop roots are particularly effective at building soil carbon because they
exude sugars and amino acids that feed soil microorganisms, which in turn
produce glues and humic substances that stabilize soil aggregates.

### Weed Suppression
Weed suppression is another major benefit. A dense green manure canopy shades
the soil surface, preventing weed seed germination. Many green manures,
particularly fast-growing species like buckwheat, annual ryegrass, and mustard,
outcompete weeds through rapid establishment and dense foliage. Some green
manures also exhibit allelopathic properties, releasing biochemical compounds
that inhibit weed seed germination. This natural weed suppression reduces or
eliminates the need for cultivation or herbicides between cash crop cycles,
saving labor and protecting soil structure from disturbance.

### Pest and Disease Management
Green manures also play a role in pest and disease management through crop
rotation effects. By breaking the continuous presence of a particular crop
family, green manures interrupt the life cycles of host-specific pests and
pathogens. Mustard family green manures produce glucosinolate compounds that
act as natural biofumigants when the plant tissue is incorporated into the
soil, suppressing soil-borne pathogens like nematodes and Fusarium fungi. The
[[soil-nutrient-cycling]] process is enhanced because green manure roots explore
different soil horizons than the preceding cash crop, bringing nutrients from
deeper layers to the surface.

### Choosing the Right Green Manure
Selecting the right green manure depends on your goals, timing, and climate.
For nitrogen fixation, clovers (crimson, red, white, and berseem) and vetches
(hairy vetch, common vetch) are top choices. For rapid biomass production and
weed suppression, buckwheat excels in warm weather and winter rye dominates in
cool conditions. For deep soil penetration and compaction breaking, daikon
radish (tillage radish) sends a massive taproot several feet deep that
decomposes in winter, leaving channels for water infiltration and root
penetration. For drought tolerance, cowpeas and sorghum-sudangrass perform in
hot, dry conditions.

### Termination Timing and Methods
Termination timing affects the balance between biomass production and nutrient
release. Green manures terminated at the flowering stage offer the best
combination of maximum biomass production and sufficient nitrogen content.
Earlier termination yields less organic matter but releases nitrogen faster
because younger tissue has a lower carbon-to-nitrogen ratio. Later termination
produces more biomass but may tie up nitrogen temporarily as the higher C:N
ratio material decomposes. Incorporation methods include mowing and tilling,
crimping with a roller-crimper for no-till systems, or simply mowing and
leaving the residue on the surface as mulch.

### Living Mulches: A Special Case
Living mulches, such as [[white-clover-living-mulch]], are a special category
of green manure that grows concurrently with the cash crop rather than between
crop cycles. White clover interplanted between vegetable rows provides ongoing
nitrogen fixation, weed suppression, and soil protection while the main crop
grows. Managing the competition between the living mulch and the crop requires
attention to mowing height and timing, but the long-term soil benefits of
continuous living root systems are substantial, particularly for building
mycorrhizal networks that benefit the entire garden ecosystem.

### Integrating Green Manures into Garden Planning
For home gardeners, the simplest integration is to plant a green manure in any
bed that would otherwise sit empty between crops. After harvesting summer
vegetables, sow a winter cover of crimson clover or winter rye. Before spring
planting, terminate the cover crop and allow two to three weeks for
decomposition before direct seeding. In crop rotation, replace every third or
fourth bed with a legume green manure to maintain soil nitrogen levels without
importing any fertilizer.
