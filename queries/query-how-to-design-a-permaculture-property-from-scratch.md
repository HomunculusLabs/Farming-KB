# How to Design a Permaculture Property from Scratch

## Short Answer
Designing a permaculture property begins with extended observation of the land's patterns over at least one full year, followed by mapping sectors for sun, wind, water flow, and wildlife corridors.

Elements are then organized into zones based on frequency of human use, with intensive gardens and daily-use elements placed near the home and orchards and food forests placed farther away. Water management through earthworks like swales and keyline design is typically installed first, followed by access paths, shelterbelts, and finally planting systems.

## Detailed Explanation

Permaculture property design is a systematic process that transforms a piece of land into a productive, self-sustaining ecosystem modeled on natural patterns. Unlike conventional landscaping or farming that imposes a pre-determined layout regardless of site conditions, permaculture design starts with deeply understanding the unique characteristics of your specific site.

The [[permaculture-design]] framework provides the methodology, while the [[permaculture-beginners-guide-observation-and-mapping]] covers the critical first step that many beginners are tempted to skip.

### Observation and Site Analysis

Observation should be the longest phase of your design process, and patience here pays dividends for decades. The [[permaculture-beginners-guide-observation-and-mapping]] recommends spending at least one full year observing your property through all four seasons before making any permanent changes.

During this observation year, track where water flows during rain events and where it pools, where frost pockets form on cold mornings, which areas receive the most sun at different times of year, where prevailing winds come from and where they are blocked or funneled, what native plants are already thriving and where, and what wildlife uses the land.

The [[hemenway-garden-design-process-observation-and-visioning]] method adds a visioning component where you articulate your goals and values before beginning the design process. This visioning step ensures that your design serves your actual needs rather than following a generic template.

Taking photos at the same locations each month creates a visual record of seasonal changes that is invaluable during the design phase. Simple tools like a compass, measuring tape, and notebook are all you need to begin documenting your site's patterns.

Creating a base map of your property is one of the most useful exercises during observation. Sketch the boundaries, existing structures, large trees, water features, and any other permanent features. This map becomes the canvas on which you layer your sector analysis, zone layout, and planting plans.

Talking to longtime neighbors about historical weather patterns, previous land use, and seasonal variations can reveal information that months of personal observation might miss. Local knowledge about frost dates, flooding history, and soil conditions is invaluable.

### Zones and Sectors

Once you understand your site's natural patterns, the sector analysis organizes this information into overlays on your property map. The [[faires-zone-and-sector-planning]] method maps sectors for sun, wind, water, fire risk, wildlife, noise, pollution, and views.

Each sector represents an energy flow that crosses your property from a particular direction. For example, the winter sun sector tells you where to place cold-sensitive elements, the wind sector informs shelterbelt placement, and the water sector guides swale and pond positioning.

The [[permaculture-design]] provides the most comprehensive treatment of this topic and explains how zones and sectors interact to create an efficient and resilient design.

After sectors are mapped, the zone system organizes your design by frequency of human interaction and maintenance needs. The [[permaculture-beginners-guide-zones-and-sectors]] explains the five zones in detail.

Zone 1 is the area immediately surrounding your home, typically within 30 feet, for intensive annual gardens, culinary herbs, salad greens, propagation areas, and anything you visit daily. Zone 2 extends to about 100 feet and contains less intensive perennial plantings, small fruit trees, berry bushes, compost bins, and small animal housing like chicken coops.

Zone 3 is for larger-scale production including orchards, grazing areas, and staple crops that need weekly attention. Zone 4 is for managed forestry, foraging, and large-scale grazing that needs monthly or seasonal attention. Zone 5 is left as wild habitat for wildlife, observation, and ecological study.

The zone system is not rigid and can be adapted to your specific property shape, size, and personal habits. A key principle is that zones can be non-concentric: a well-placed herb garden next to the kitchen door is Zone 1 even if your property's Zone 1 boundary extends in a different direction.

### Water and Access Infrastructure

Water management is typically the first physical element to install because water flows downhill and all other design elements must work with and around the water system. The [[keyline-design]] approach, developed by P.A. Yeomans and adapted for permaculture by Bill Mollison, uses contour-based cultivation and shallow keyline plowing to distribute water evenly across a landscape.

Keyline design prevents erosion, reduces runoff, and hydrates the deep soil profile. For smaller properties, the [[faires-water-harvesting-and-swales]] guide covers swale construction: shallow ditches dug on contour that catch runoff water and allow it to slowly infiltrate into the soil.

The [[backyard-water-harvesting]] resource addresses roof water collection through gutters, downspouts, and storage tanks, which can provide thousands of gallons of irrigation water annually. Start water management from the highest point on your property and work downhill.

Ponds and greywater systems can supplement rainwater harvesting and provide additional water storage. Even small ponds serve multiple functions including irrigation storage, wildlife habitat, fire protection, and aesthetic beauty.

Access design is another foundational element that should be established early because pathways, roads, and gateways define how you move through the property and influence where elements are placed for convenience. The [[permaculture-beginners-guide-observation-and-mapping]] notes that well-designed access reduces the energy cost of maintaining your property.

Main paths should be wide enough for wheelbarrows and follow the most natural routes between frequently visited areas. Secondary paths can be narrower stepping-stone trails. Every time you find yourself taking a shortcut across the lawn, that shortcut should become an official path.

Consider how water moves along paths during heavy rain and design drainage accordingly. Paths that double as swales or divert water to planting beds serve dual functions, a core permaculture principle of each element performing multiple functions.

### Planting Systems and Soil Building

After infrastructure is in place, planting design follows the patterns established by zones, sectors, and water management. The [[food-forest-design]] approach creates multi-layered perennial food systems modeled on natural forest ecosystems, using canopy trees, understory trees, shrubs, herbaceous plants, ground covers, root crops, and vines stacked vertically to maximize production per square foot.

The [[food-forest-layers-and-species]] resource provides plant lists for each layer. The [[homestead-permaculture-design]] integrates food forests with annual gardens, animal systems, water systems, and infrastructure into a cohesive whole.

The [[permaculture-beginners-guide-food-forests-introduction]] is an excellent starting point for understanding how perennial food systems fit into a broader permaculture design. Combined with the [[phytoremediation]], which covers techniques for building the soil health that all other systems depend on, these resources provide a solid foundation for anyone starting their permaculture journey from an empty or degraded property.

Soil building should begin immediately, even before planting. Sheet mulching, cover cropping, composting, and chop-and-drop mulching build organic matter and soil biology from the surface down. Healthy soil is the foundation upon which every other permaculture system depends, and investing in soil health from the start dramatically accelerates the establishment of productive planting systems.

Start small rather than trying to implement your entire design at once. Begin with Zone 1 and the water systems closest to your home, then expand outward as you gain experience and confidence. A well-managed small area is more productive and educational than a poorly maintained large one.

Design is an iterative process, not a one-time event. Your plan will evolve as you learn more about your land, your climate, and your own preferences. The best permaculture designs are living documents that grow and change alongside the systems they describe.

## Key Points
- Begin with thorough observation and mapping per [[permaculture-beginners-guide-observation-and-mapping]] before making any physical changes
- Use [[permaculture-beginners-guide-zones-and-sectors]] to organize elements by frequency of use and energy efficiency
- Install water management systems like [[keyline-design]] and [[faires-water-harvesting-and-swales]] before any planting
- Design [[food-forest-design]] systems in lower zones for long-term food production with minimal maintenance
- [[homestead-permaculture-design]] integrates all elements into a cohesive, self-sustaining property plan
- [[faires-zone-and-sector-planning]] maps energy flows (sun, wind, water, wildlife) that cross your property
- [[permaculture-design]] provides the most comprehensive zone and sector methodology
- [[backyard-water-harvesting]] captures roof runoff for garden irrigation
- [[phytoremediation]] covers the soil health foundation all systems depend on
- Access paths should follow natural movement patterns to reduce maintenance energy
- Soil building with sheet mulching and composting should begin before any planting is done
- Monthly photo documentation of seasonal patterns provides invaluable design reference data
- Start with Zone 1 and expand outward as experience grows rather than implementing the entire design at once
- Each element in the design should perform multiple functions, following core permaculture design principles
