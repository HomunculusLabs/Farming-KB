## How to Build a Cob Oven for Outdoor Cooking

## Short Answer

A cob oven is an earthen oven made from clay, sand, and straw that can reach temperatures over 800 degrees Fahrenheit, making it ideal for baking bread, roasting vegetables, and cooking pizzas.

Building one costs under fifty dollars in materials and can be completed in a single weekend with basic [[natural-building-materials-guide]] known to humanity, consisting of clay-rich subsoil mixed with sand and straw. It is fireproof, inexpensive, and can be sourced directly from your property in many regions.

Unlike brick ovens, cob ovens do not require mortar or fired bricks, making them accessible to anyone regardless of construction experience.

The [[comparison-windbreak-vs-thermal-mass]] of a cob oven is what makes it such an exceptional cooking tool.

Once heated, the dense earthen walls retain heat for hours, allowing you to bake multiple batches of bread from a single fire.

This slow, even heat distribution produces crusty artisan loaves that rival any professional bakery.

## Gathering Your Materials

You will need three main ingredients: clay subsoil, sharp sand, and straw.

The clay subsoil can usually be dug from your yard, often found below the topsoil layer at a depth of 30 centimeters or more.

A simple test involves filling a jar with soil and water, shaking it, and watching the layers settle.

Sand should be sharp or builder-grade sand rather than fine beach sand.

The angular edges of sharp sand create better interlocking within the cob mix.

Straw serves as the fiber reinforcement that prevents cracking as the oven dries and heats.

Fresh straw works better than hay because it contains fewer seeds that could sprout in the finished oven.

Additional materials include a base of urbanite (broken concrete pieces), firebricks for the oven floor, and newspaper or sawdust as insulation.

Most of these can be scavenged for free from construction sites or local recycling centers.

## Building the Foundation

A solid foundation keeps your oven stable and raises it to a comfortable working height.

Many builders use a platform of stacked urbanite or stone rubble mortared together.

The foundation should be roughly 120 centimeters in diameter and raised to about hip height for ergonomic loading.

Create a level surface on top of the foundation using a thin layer of sand and cement.

This provides the base for your firebrick floor, which should be laid tightly together in a circular or square pattern.

Firebricks can withstand repeated heating and cooling cycles without cracking, unlike standard bricks.

## Forming the Oven Dome

The oven dome is built over a temporary form made from damp sand.

Shape the sand into a dome roughly 45 to 60 centimeters in diameter and 35 to 45 centimeters tall.

Cover the sand form with wet newspaper so you can tell the sand from the cob when you carve out the interior later.

Mix your cob by combining one part clay subsoil with roughly three parts sharp sand, then fold in straw until the mixture holds together when compressed.

The ideal consistency is similar to modeling clay.

It should hold its shape when formed into a ball and dropped from waist height without cracking.

Apply the cob mixture over the sand form in layers roughly 8 to 10 centimeters thick.

Start from the base and work upward, smoothing each layer before adding the next.

The first layer, called the thermal layer, should be solid cob without insulation.

This is the layer that absorbs and radiates heat.

## Adding Insulation and the Outer Shell

Once the thermal layer dries for a day or two, add an insulation layer of sawdust, [[perlite]], or lightweight clay-straw mix.

This layer traps heat inside the oven and reduces fuel consumption.

It should be roughly 10 to 15 centimeters thick and can be applied more loosely than the thermal layer.

The final outer shell protects the insulation from weather and physical damage.

This layer uses the same cob mix as the thermal layer but is thinner, around 5 centimeters.

## The Door and Chimney

Cut a doorway into the front of the oven once the cob has dried enough to hold its shape.

The door opening should be roughly 60 percent of the oven interior height, which creates the right ratio for efficient combustion and heat retention.

A wooden door carved to fit the opening helps seal in heat during baking.

A small chimney opening at the top of the door arch improves draft during the initial firing phase.

The chimney helps draw smoke out during the fire-building stage before the oven is sealed for baking.

## Curing and First Fires

Curing is the most critical step in building a cob oven.

The thick earthen walls must dry slowly to prevent cracking.

Start with small fires for short periods, gradually increasing duration and intensity over the course of a week or two.

Rushing this process can cause steam to build up inside the walls, leading to cracks or structural failure.

During curing, watch for developing cracks and patch them with wet cob mix while the oven is still warm.

Hairline cracks are normal and can be filled with a clay-sand slip.

## Cooking with Your Cob Oven

After curing, your cob oven is ready for cooking.

Build a hot fire inside using dry hardwood and let it burn until the interior walls turn white or black soot burns off, indicating the oven has reached maximum temperature.

Rake out the coals and sweep the floor clean with a damp cloth.

The oven temperature at this point is ideal for pizza, flatbreads, and quick roasting.
