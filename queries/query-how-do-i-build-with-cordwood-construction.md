# How Do I Build With Cordwood Construction?

## Short Answer

Cordwood construction is a natural building technique where short logs are laid crosswise in a mortar matrix to create thick, highly insulated walls.
The method combines the thermal mass of masonry with the insulation value of wood, producing walls that are typically 12 to 24 inches thick.
It is one of the most accessible [[natural-building]] methods for owner-builders because it requires minimal specialized tools and can use locally sourced or salvaged wood.

## What Is Cordwood Construction?

Cordwood construction, also called stackwall or stovewood building, involves placing log ends perpendicular to the wall face, embedded in a mortar mix.
The logs act as insulation while the mortar provides structural binding and thermal mass.
The result is a striking wall with exposed circular log ends that creates a distinctive, rustic aesthetic.

The technique has deep historical roots in Scandinavia and North America, where settlers built cordwood barns and homes using firewood-length timber scraps.
Modern cordwood building has been refined with better mortar recipes, insulation layers, and engineering standards.
It is closely related to other [[natural-building-techniques]] like [[cob-building]], [[straw-bale-construction]], and [[rammed-earth]].

## What Wood Species Work Best?

The ideal wood for cordwood is dry, dense, and rot-resistant.
Softwoods like cedar, spruce, and pine are commonly used because they dry well and resist cracking.
Hardwoods can be used but tend to shrink more as they dry, potentially creating gaps in the mortar.

All wood must be thoroughly seasoned before building, ideally for at least one year.
Green or wet wood will shrink inside the wall, pulling away from the mortar and creating air leaks.
The logs should be debarked to prevent insect harboring and improve mortar adhesion.

Key considerations for wood selection:
- Cedar and juniper offer natural rot resistance and are lightweight
- Spruce and pine are widely available and affordable
- Oak and other hardwoods are dense but require longer drying times
- Avoid wood with visible rot, insect damage, or excessive checking

## What Mortar Mix Should I Use?

The mortar in cordwood construction serves as both the structural binder and the thermal mass component.
Traditional mixes use a combination of Portland cement, lime, sand, and sawdust or paper fiber.
The sawdust or fiber additive slows curing and reduces shrinkage cracking.

A common recipe is one part Portland cement, one part hydrated lime, and two to three parts sand, with soaked sawdust added to improve workability.
Some builders use [[hempcrete-and-hemp-lime-construction]] principles, substituting hemp hurd for sawdust.
The mortar should be stiff enough to hold the logs in place without sagging.

Proper mortar consistency is critical for preventing air infiltration and ensuring long-term wall integrity.
For more on natural mortar options, see [[earth-plaster-and-natural-finishes]] and [[mollison-designers-lime-and-cement-natural-building]].

## How Does Insulation Work in Cordwood Walls?

Cordwood walls can be built in several configurations to optimize thermal performance.
The simplest approach uses solid cordwood and mortar throughout the wall thickness.
More advanced builds incorporate an insulation layer between inner and outer mortar joints.

Common insulation strategies include:
- A centered layer of perlite or vermiculite between inner and outer mortar beds
- Sawdust mixed with lime as a fill material between the logs
- Rigid foam board or mineral wool as a continuous insulation layer
- Double-wall construction with a full insulation cavity

The insulation value of a 16-inch cordwood wall typically ranges from R-10 to R-20 depending on the wood-to-mortar ratio and any added insulation layers.
This compares favorably to conventional stud walls while providing significantly more [[passive-solar-design]] thermal mass.

## How Does Cordwood Compare to Other Natural Building Methods?

Cordwood occupies a unique niche among natural building methods.
Compared to [[comparison-cob-vs-cordwood]], cordwood provides better insulation but less sculptural flexibility.
Against [[comparison-straw-bale-vs-cordwood-construction]], cordwood offers better fire resistance and lower maintenance.

The method also compares well to [[comparison-timber-frame-vs-cordwood-construction]], where timber framing provides the structural skeleton and cordwood fills the wall panels.
Some builders combine cordwood with [[comparison-earthship-vs-cordwood-construction]] principles, using recycled materials in the foundation.
Others integrate cordwood with [[comparison-hempcrete-vs-cordwood-construction]] mortar for improved moisture regulation.

Each method has strengths suited to different climates, budgets, and aesthetic preferences.

## What Are the Key Steps in Building a Cordwood Wall?

Building a cordwood wall follows a systematic process:

1. Prepare a solid foundation, typically stone or concrete block
2. Mix mortar to the correct consistency
3. Lay a bed of mortar along the wall line
4. Place log ends into the mortar, pressing firmly
5. Leave a small gap between logs for mortar to key in
6. Smooth the mortar joints and clean excess from log faces
7. Repeat courses, checking for level and plumb regularly
8. Allow adequate curing time between courses in hot weather

The process is methodical but does not require advanced masonry skills.
Most owner-builders can achieve good results with practice and patience.
For structural guidance, see [[timber-frame-basics]] and [[roundwood-shelter-building]].

## What About Roof and Foundation Considerations?

Cordwood walls are heavy, requiring a substantial foundation that extends below the frost line in cold climates.
Stone, concrete, or [[slip-form-stone-construction]] foundations are common choices.
The foundation must be wide enough to support the full wall thickness plus any overhang.

Roof design is critical because cordwood walls are vulnerable to prolonged moisture exposure.
Generous roof overhangs of at least 18 to 24 inches protect the wall tops from rain and snow.
A [[permaculture-building-design]] approach often integrates living roofs or water catchment systems.

Proper detailing at the wall-to-roof junction prevents water infiltration, which is the primary cause of cordwood wall failure.
See [[comparison-wattle-and-daub-vs-cordwood-construction]] for contrast in how different methods handle roof connections.

## What About Building in Wet or Cold Climates?

Cordwood construction performs best in dry or moderately humid climates where the walls can breathe and dry out between wetting events.
In very wet climates, extra attention to roof overhangs, splash protection, and wall coatings is essential.
In cold climates, the thermal mass of the mortar can be a liability if not properly insulated from the exterior.

Strategies for challenging climates include:
- Using a vapor-permeable exterior finish to allow drying
- Adding external insulation to prevent condensation within the wall
- Extending roof overhangs further in rainy regions
- Applying a breathable waterproof coating to exposed exterior faces

The [[mollison-designers-passive-solar-building]] principles are especially relevant for cold-climate cordwood structures.
Proper siting to capture winter sun and block prevailing winds dramatically improves comfort and energy performance.

## Key Points

- Cordwood construction uses short log ends set in mortar to create thick, insulated walls
- Cedar, spruce, and pine are the most commonly used wood species
- Mortar mixes typically combine Portland cement, lime, sand, and fiber additives
- Insulation layers between inner and outer mortar beds improve thermal performance
- Generous roof overhangs are essential to protect walls from moisture damage
- The method is accessible to owner-builders with basic construction skills
- Building code compliance varies by jurisdiction and may require engineering review
- Proper wood drying and mortar mixing are critical for long-term wall integrity
