# How do I make homemade vinegar from scratch?

## Short Answer

Homemade vinegar is produced by a two-stage fermentation process where sugars are first converted to alcohol by yeast, then alcohol is converted to acetic acid by Acetobacter bacteria.
You can make vinegar from nearly any fruit, grain, or sugar source including apples, grapes, rice, honey, and even leftover wine.
The process requires minimal equipment and takes anywhere from a few weeks to several months depending on the method and desired flavor complexity.

## Detailed Explanation

### How Does Vinegar Fermentation Work?

Vinegar production involves two sequential microbial transformations.
In the first stage, yeasts consume sugars and produce ethanol through alcoholic fermentation.
In the second stage, acetic acid bacteria (primarily Acetobacter and Gluconobacter species) oxidize the ethanol into acetic acid.
This second stage requires oxygen, which is why vinegar fermentation vessels are designed to maximize air exposure while keeping out fruit flies and contaminants.
The [[effective-microorganisms-em|Effective Microorganisms (EM)]] concept uses similar mixed-microbe principles for soil and plant applications.
Understanding both fermentation stages helps you troubleshoot problems like slow conversion or off-flavors.

### What Ingredients Do I Need?

The simplest vinegars require only a sugar source, water, and a vinegar mother or starter culture.
For fruit vinegars, use overripe or bruised fruit that is still free of mold — apples, grapes, peaches, and berries all work well.
For grain-based vinegars like the ones used in Korean Natural Farming, you start with [[brown-rice-vinegar-brv|brown rice]] as the carbohydrate base.
A mother of vinegar (a gelatinous cellulose mat containing live acetic acid bacteria) speeds up the process significantly.
You can also use raw, unfiltered store-bought vinegar as a starter if you do not have a mother culture available.
The [[vinegar-making|vinegar-making guide]] provides detailed ingredient ratios for various vinegar types.

### What Equipment Is Required?

You need a wide-mouth glass or ceramic vessel that allows maximum surface area for air exchange.
Cover the opening with a breathable cloth or coffee filter secured with a rubber band to allow oxygen in while keeping insects out.
Avoid metal containers because acetic acid corrodes most metals and will create off-flavors.
A glass jar or crock with at least twice the volume of your liquid gives adequate headspace for the process.
Some practitioners use an airlock system for the alcoholic fermentation stage, then switch to open-air for the acetic fermentation.
A simple kitchen thermometer and pH strips help monitor progress, though experienced fermenters often rely on taste and smell alone.

### How Do I Make Apple Cider Vinegar?

Start by chopping organic apples into small pieces, including the cores and peels where wild yeast populations are concentrated.
Place the apple pieces in a glass jar and cover with filtered water, adding one tablespoon of sugar per cup of water to feed the yeast.
Cover with a cloth and stir daily for the first week to introduce oxygen and prevent surface mold from forming.
After two to three weeks, strain out the apple solids and return the liquid to the jar for the acetic fermentation stage.
Let it ferment for an additional four to six weeks, tasting weekly until it reaches your preferred acidity.
The [[cider-making-guide|cider-making guide]] covers the related process of hard cider production, which is essentially the first stage of apple cider vinegar.

### How Do I Make Brown Rice Vinegar (KNF/JADAM Style)?

Korean Natural Farming and JADAM both produce brown rice vinegar as a core input for organic farming.
The [[knf-brown-rice-vinegar|KNF brown rice vinegar]] process involves fermenting cooked brown rice with water in a sealed anaerobic environment.
The [[jadam-brown-rice-vinegar-production|JADAM brown rice vinegar production]] method follows similar principles but with simplified techniques designed for small-scale farmers.
Both approaches rely on the [[jadam-anaerobic-fermentation|JADAM anaerobic fermentation]] framework to convert rice starches through multiple microbial stages.
The resulting vinegar serves as a pH adjuster, nutrient solvent, and foliar spray ingredient in organic farming systems.
The [[cho-brown-rice-vinegar-preparation|CHO brown rice vinegar preparation]] method from Master Cho provides step-by-step instructions for home-scale production.

### How Long Does Vinegar Fermentation Take?

The total time ranges from three weeks for fast fruit vinegars to six months or more for traditional slow-fermented grain vinegars.
Alcoholic fermentation typically takes one to three weeks depending on temperature and sugar concentration.
Acetic acid conversion takes two to six weeks after the alcoholic stage completes.
Warm temperatures between 60-80°F (15-27°C) accelerate the process, while cooler temperatures slow it down but can produce more complex flavors.
You can speed up the process significantly by using a vinegar mother or commercial starter culture rather than relying on wild bacteria.
Some traditional methods like the Orleans process take months but yield exceptionally smooth, complex vinegars.

### What Can Go Wrong and How Do I Fix It?

The most common problems are mold growth, slow or stalled fermentation, and unpleasant odors.
White surface mold (kahm yeast) is generally harmless and can be skimmed off without affecting the batch.
Black, green, or furry mold indicates contamination and the batch should be discarded for safety.
If fermentation stalls, check the temperature and ensure adequate oxygen supply during the acetic stage.
A vinegar that smells like nail polish remover (ethyl acetate) has gone too far — it is still safe but may taste harsh.
The [[comparison-vinegar-vs-lacto-fermentation|vinegar vs lacto fermentation comparison]] explains why these two preservation methods behave differently when problems arise.

### What About Using Vinegar for Food Preservation?

Homemade vinegar can be used directly for [[food-preservation-and-storage-methods|food preservation]] alongside other techniques.
However, homemade vinegars vary in acidity and may not reach the 5% acetic acid threshold required for safe canning.
Use a pH meter to verify your vinegar registers at pH 4.0 or below before using it for shelf-stable preservation.
Pickling with homemade vinegar works beautifully for refrigerator pickles and quick-pickle recipes where long-term shelf stability is not required.
The [[canning-and-preserving|canning and preserving]] guide covers safe acidification standards for shelf-stable products.
The [[lacto-fermentation-and-food-preservation-methods|lacto-fermentation and food preservation]] approach offers an alternative acid-based preservation method that does not require vinegar at all.

### What About Fruit and Herb-Infused Vinegars?

Once you have mastered plain vinegar, infusing it with herbs, fruit, or aromatics creates distinctive condiments for cooking.
Add fresh herbs like tarragon, rosemary, or thyme to finished vinegar and let steep for two to four weeks in a cool, dark place.
Berry-infused vinegars made with raspberries or blackberries work beautifully in salad dressings and marinades.
Garlic, chili, and ginger infusions are popular in Asian cuisines and make excellent homemade gifts.
The [[fermented-vegetables-guide|fermented vegetables guide]] describes similar infusion principles for pickled condiments.
Always start with a fully fermented vinegar base before adding infusions to avoid introducing unwanted fermentation variables.

### What About Storage and Shelf Life?

Finished vinegar is self-preserving due to its high acidity and can be stored indefinitely at room temperature.
Keep bottles sealed and out of direct sunlight to preserve flavor quality over time.
A sediment or "mother" may form in the bottom of stored vinegar — this is completely normal and harmless.
You can filter it out through a coffee filter if you prefer clear vinegar, or leave it as proof of live-culture status.
Properly fermented vinegar with pH below 4.0 will not support pathogen growth and is safe for long-term storage.

## Key Points

- Vinegar is made through a two-stage fermentation: sugar to alcohol (yeast), then alcohol to acetic acid (Acetobacter bacteria).
- You can make vinegar from apples, grapes, rice, honey, or any sugar-containing substrate.
- KNF and JADAM systems use [[brown-rice-vinegar-brv|brown rice vinegar]] as a core farming input.
- A vinegar mother or raw vinegar starter significantly speeds up the acetic fermentation stage.
- Always use glass or ceramic containers — never metal — because acetic acid is corrosive.
- Homemade vinegar may not reach safe canning acidity (5%) — always verify with pH testing.
