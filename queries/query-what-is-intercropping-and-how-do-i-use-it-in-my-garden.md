# What Is Intercropping and How Do I Use It in My Garden?

## Short Answer

Intercropping is the practice of growing two or more crops together in the same space at the same time.
It increases total yield per area, reduces pest pressure, and improves soil health by mimicking natural plant communities.
This technique is a cornerstone of [[intercropping-systems]] and is closely related to [[companion-planting]] and [[polyculture-design-evolution]].

## What Is Intercropping?

Intercropping means planting different species in close proximity so they share resources and interact beneficially.
Unlike monoculture planting, intercropping leverages [[comparison-monoculture-vs-polyculture|the advantages of polyculture]] to maximize light capture, nutrient uptake, and biological diversity.
The concept has been practiced for centuries in traditional farming systems such as the [[gaias-garden-three-sisters-guild|Three Sisters]] (corn, beans, and squash) grown by Indigenous peoples of the Americas.
Sepp Holzer used intercropping extensively in his terraced gardens, as documented in [[holzer-intercropping-by-height-suntraps]] and [[holzer-polycultures-catch-crop-combinations]].

## Types of Intercropping

There are several intercropping patterns, each suited to different garden layouts and goals.
**Row intercropping** arranges crops in alternating rows, making harvesting and management straightforward.
**Strip intercropping** uses wider strips of each crop, allowing for mechanized cultivation between strips.
**Mixed intercropping** plants species together without a fixed arrangement, as seen in [[holzer-vegetable-garden-polycultures]].
**Relay intercropping** plants a second crop into a standing first crop before the first is harvested, which overlaps with [[coleman-succession-planting|succession planting]] concepts.
**Understorey intercropping** pairs tall crops with shade-tolerant species below, similar to the canopy layer strategy described in [[canopy-layer-design-food-forests]].

## Benefits of Intercropping

Intercropping offers multiple synergistic benefits that single-crop planting cannot achieve.
**Higher total yield:** Different species use light, water, and nutrients at different levels and times, producing more food per square meter.
**Pest reduction:** Diverse plantings confuse pests and support beneficial insects, a principle explored in [[companion-planting-deterrents]].
**Improved soil health:** Mixed root systems prevent erosion, add organic matter, and support [[interplant-mycorrhizal-linkages|mycorrhizal networks]] that transfer nutrients between species.
**Weed suppression:** Dense canopy coverage from multiple crop layers shades out weeds more effectively than monocultures.
**Risk reduction:** If one crop fails due to weather or disease, the others may still produce a harvest.

## How Plants Interact Below Ground

One of the most fascinating aspects of intercropping is what happens underground.
Plants connected by mycorrhizal fungi can share carbon and nutrients through [[interplant-carbon-nutrient-transfer-mycorrhizal-networks|common mycelial networks]].
Research documented in [[interplant-resource-transfer-mycorrhizal-succession]] shows that older, established plants can nourish younger seedlings through these fungal connections.
[[companion-planting-with-fungi]] explores how deliberate inoculation with mycorrhizal species enhances these underground partnerships.
Nitrogen-fixing legumes intercropped with heavy-feeding crops provide a direct nutrient subsidy, as detailed in [[companion-planting-and-polycultures]].

## Planning an Intercropped Garden

Designing an effective intercropping system requires attention to plant height, root depth, growth rate, and nutrient needs.
Use tall, slow-growing crops (like corn or sunflowers) with fast, low-growing crops (like lettuce or radishes) underneath.
Pair deep-rooted crops with shallow-rooted ones so they access nutrients from different soil layers.
Match heavy feeders (tomatoes, brassicas) with nitrogen-fixing companions (beans, clover, peas).
[[guild-design-species-interactions]] provides a framework for analyzing how different species combinations perform together.
The [[companion-planting-reference]] offers a quick lookup table for compatible and incompatible crop pairings.

## Classic Intercropping Combinations

Several well-tested combinations work reliably in home gardens.
**The Three Sisters** (corn, pole beans, squash) is described in [[gaias-garden-three-sisters-guild]] and remains one of the most productive intercrop systems known.
**Tomatoes with basil and carrots** repels pests and improves flavor while filling different canopy layers.
**Brassicas with legumes** pairs heavy feeders with nitrogen providers, as explored in [[biofumigation-and-brassica-cover-crops-for-pest-management]].
**Fruit tree understories** use [[apple-guild-design-guide|guild design principles]] to stack productive layers beneath canopy trees.
**Cereal-legume intercrops** are widely used in grain production, as documented in [[fukuoka-duck-rice-fish-polyculture|Fukuoka's rice-duck-fish system]].

## Intercropping vs Companion Planting vs Polyculture

These three concepts overlap but have distinct meanings in practice.
Intercropping focuses on the spatial arrangement of crops for combined yield, as discussed in [[comparison-polyculture-vs-intercropping]].
Companion planting emphasizes beneficial interactions between specific plant pairs, covered in [[comparison-companion-planting-vs-intercropping]].
Polyculture is the broadest term, encompassing any multi-species planting system, as explored in [[comparison-companion-planting-vs-polyculture]].
In practice, most productive gardens use all three approaches simultaneously.

## Intercropping in Permaculture Design

Permaculture design embraces intercropping as a fundamental strategy for creating productive, resilient landscapes.
[[evolving-polyculture-design]] describes how plant communities change over time, with early species preparing conditions for later ones.
[[polyculture-design-holzer|Holzer's polyculture approach]] emphasizes observation-based design, letting natural succession guide species selection.
[[agroforestry-and-alley-cropping|Alley cropping]] is a large-scale intercropping system that integrates trees with annual crops.
[[comparison-food-forest-vs-polyculture|Food forests]] are essentially multi-layered intercrops designed to function like natural woodland ecosystems.

## Intercropping in Small Spaces

Even small gardens and raised beds can benefit from intercropping techniques.
Use the [[cervantes-raised-beds-mound-planting|raised bed mound planting]] method to create micro-topography that supports multiple crop layers in a small footprint.
Fast-growing crops like radishes or arugula can be sown between slower crops like cabbage or peppers, harvesting the quick crop before the slow one needs the space.
Vertical intercropping trains climbing plants (beans, cucumbers) up trellises while low crops (lettuce, spinach) grow at the base.
Container gardens can use the same principles by pairing a tall centerpiece plant with trailing or low-growing companions in the same pot.

## What About Intercropping in Greenhouses?

Greenhouse intercropping follows similar principles but with added control over the growing environment.
You can extend the growing season of intercrops by using protected structures, as described in [[coleman-succession-planting-winter-harvest|Coleman's winter harvest methods]].
In heated greenhouses, tropical and subtropical species can be intercropped in layered systems that would not survive outdoors.
Ventilation and airflow become more critical with dense intercropped plantings, so spacing must account for mature plant sizes.
Greenhouse intercropping pairs well with [[backyard-food-forest-design|food forest design]] concepts adapted for indoor environments.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

Avoid intercropping plants with similar resource needs, as they will compete rather than cooperate.
Do not mix crops that require different watering regimes or soil pH levels.
Watch for allelopathic interactions where one species chemically inhibits another's growth.
Keep harvest timing in mind — if you need to pull one crop mid-season, it may disturb its neighbor's roots.
Start with simple two-species combinations before attempting complex [[garden-guilds-and-polyculture-design|multi-layer polycultures]].

## Key Points

- Intercropping grows multiple crop species together in the same space for higher total yield and resilience.
- Underground fungal networks enable [[interplant-mycorrhizal-linkages|nutrient sharing]] between intercropped species.
- Choose plant combinations with complementary heights, root depths, and nutrient needs.
- Classic systems like the [[gaias-garden-three-sisters-guild|Three Sisters]] have been proven over centuries of practice.
- Intercropping is a core strategy in [[holzer-catch-crop-and-polyculture-design|Holzer's permaculture methods]] and [[fukuoka-duck-rice-fish-polyculture|Fukuoka's natural farming]].
- Start simple with proven pairs, then expand to more complex [[guild-design-methods|guild-based designs]].
