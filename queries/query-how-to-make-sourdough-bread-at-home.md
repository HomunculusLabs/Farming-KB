# How to Make Sourdough Bread at Home

## Short Answer
Sourdough bread is made using a naturally fermented dough starter (levain) instead
of commercial yeast. Creating a sourdough starter involves mixing flour and water
and feeding it daily for five to seven days until it becomes a bubbly, active
culture. The resulting bread has a complex tangy flavor, chewy texture, and
superior digestibility compared to commercially yeasted bread.

## Detailed Explanation
Sourdough is the oldest form of leavened bread, dating back thousands of years to
ancient Egypt, where wild yeasts and bacteria in the environment were first
harnessed to make bread rise. Before the development of commercial baker's yeast
in the 19th century, virtually all bread was made using some form of sourdough
fermentation. The modern revival of sourdough baking reflects growing interest in
traditional foodways, gut health, and the superior flavor and texture that natural
fermentation produces.

The foundation of sourdough baking is the starter, also called a levain or
mother culture. A sourdough starter is a living community of wild yeast (primarily
Saccharomyces exiguus) and lactic acid bacteria (Lactobacillus species) that
coexist in a symbiotic relationship within a mixture of flour and water.

Creating a sourdough starter from scratch is straightforward but requires patience
and consistency. Begin by mixing equal weights of flour and water (typically 50
grams of each) in a clean glass jar. Whole wheat or rye flour is recommended for
the initial feedings because their higher mineral and enzyme content supports
faster microbial colonization.

For the first five to seven days, the starter needs to be fed daily with fresh
flour and water. Each feeding involves discarding half of the existing starter and
adding fresh flour and water in equal parts by weight. This 1:1:1 ratio (one part
starter, one part flour, one part water) keeps the microbial population well-fed
and prevents the mixture from becoming overly acidic.

The [[faires-yogurt-and-sourdough-making]] resource provides a practical approach
to creating and maintaining sourdough cultures as part of a broader homesteading

Within three to five days, you should see bubbles forming in the starter, a sign
that wild yeast and bacteria are actively fermenting. The starter is ready to use
when it reliably doubles in volume within four to eight hours of feeding, has a
pleasant tangy aroma, and is bubbly and active on the surface.

Temperature significantly affects fermentation speed. Starters kept at warm room
temperature (75 to 80 degrees Fahrenheit) ferment faster and need more frequent
feeding, while those kept in cooler environments (65 to 70 degrees) ferment more
slowly and develop more complex sour flavors.

The [[comparison-sourdough-vs-wild-yeast-bread]] explores the differences between
maintained sourdough starters and spontaneous wild-yeast fermentation methods.

Once your starter is active, making sourdough bread involves four main phases:
mixing, bulk fermentation, shaping, and baking. A basic sourdough loaf contains
just flour, water, salt, and starter, though many recipes include small amounts
of fat or sweetener for enriched variations.

The mixing phase combines the ingredients into a shaggy dough, which is then
kneaded or stretched and folded to develop the gluten network. Autolyse, a
technique where flour and water are mixed and rested before adding salt and
starter, improves extensibility and flavor development.

Bulk fermentation is the longest phase, typically lasting four to twelve hours at
room temperature. During this time, the wild yeast produces carbon dioxide that
leavens the dough, while the lactic acid bacteria produce organic acids that
contribute flavor and strengthen the gluten. The dough should double in volume
and show visible signs of fermentation activity.

After bulk fermentation, the dough is shaped into a round or oval and placed in a
banneton (proofing basket) for a final rise. Many bakers refrigerate the shaped
loaf overnight, which slows fermentation and develops deeper flavor through cold
retardation.

Baking is typically done in a preheated Dutch oven or covered baker, which traps
steam during the first portion of the bake. This steam allows the bread to expand
fully before the crust sets, producing the characteristic open crumb and crispy,
well-colored crust that sourdough is known for.

## Key Points
- A sourdough starter is a living culture of wild yeast and lactic acid bacteria
  maintained by regular feedings of flour and water, as covered in the
  [[sourdough-bread-guide]]
- Creating a starter from scratch takes five to seven days of daily feeding before
  it is active enough to leaven bread
- Sourdough bread requires only flour, water, salt, and starter, but the long
  fermentation process creates complex flavors superior to commercial yeast
- Bulk fermentation of four to twelve hours develops both the rise and the
  characteristic tangy flavor through microbial activity
- Baking in a covered Dutch oven traps steam for optimal oven spring and crust
  development
