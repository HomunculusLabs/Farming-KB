# How Do I Build With Earthbags?

## Short Answer

Earthbag building uses polypropylene sandbags filled with damp earth, stacked in courses, and tamped solid to create thick, load-bearing walls.

The technique was popularized by architect Nader Khalili in the 1990s as a fast, affordable, and earthquake-resistant construction method.

It requires minimal skilled labor and uses locally available subsoil as the primary building material.

Barbed wire laid between each course provides tensile strength and prevents the bags from sliding.

Once plastered with earthen or lime plaster, earthbag walls are extremely durable, fire-resistant, and thermally massive.

## What Materials Do I Need?

The core materials list for an earthbag project is surprisingly short.

You need polypropylene bags, which are inexpensive and widely available from sandbag suppliers or agricultural feed stores.

The fill material is typically damp subsoil excavated directly from the building site.

A mix of approximately 70% sand and 30% clay is considered ideal for structural earthbag walls.

You will also need four-point barbed wire to place between every course of bags.

Basic tools include a tamper, a coffee can or scoop for filling bags, a bucket, and a level.

Optional but recommended materials include galvanized wire for pinning, nylon string for keeping courses straight, and a sheet-metal slider to help position bags.

## How Do I Prepare the Foundation?

Earthbag buildings need a rubble-trench foundation to keep moisture away from the lower courses.

The trench is dug below the frost line and filled with drainage gravel and a perforated pipe.

Double-bagged courses filled with gravel sit on top of the trench to create a moisture break.

These gravel-filled bags are the first courses laid and they keep wicking moisture away from the earth-filled walls above.

Some builders also pour a reinforced concrete bond beam on top of the rubble trench before stacking bags.

The foundation width should match or slightly exceed the width of your planned walls.

## How Are the Walls Actually Built?

Filling and placing bags follows a rhythmic process once you establish a workflow.

Bags are filled to a consistent height, usually about 75-80% full, then the open end is folded and tucked.

Each bag is laid in a running bond pattern so seams overlap the bags in the course below.

After placing each bag, it is tamped vigorously until the fill compacts and the bag becomes firm.

Barbed wire is laid in two strands along the top of each completed course before the next course begins.

The wire bites into the bags above and below, creating friction that prevents lateral movement.

Door and window openings are formed using temporary wooden frames or built with specialized arch techniques.

## How Do I Handle Openings and Roofs?

Door and window openings in earthbag buildings require careful engineering.

The most common approach is to use poured concrete or wooden lintels above openings to carry the load of bags above.

Arched openings are also popular and can be built using removable wooden formwork.

A reinforced concrete bond beam or a wooden ring beam is cast on top of the wall to distribute roof loads evenly.

Roofing options range from lightweight metal roofing on timber rafters to living green roofs.

The roof overhang should be generous, at least 60 centimeters, to protect the earthen plaster from rain splash.

## What Plaster and Finishing Should I Use?

Earthbag walls must be plastered to protect the UV-sensitive polypropylene bags from sunlight degradation.

Earthen plaster made from clay, sand, and chopped straw is the most common and affordable finish.

Lime plaster is more water-resistant and is recommended for areas with heavy rainfall or high humidity.

Cement stucco is sometimes used but can trap moisture inside the wall and is generally not preferred.

A good plaster job starts with a rough key coat pressed into the bag surface, followed by one or two finish coats.

Mesh such as chicken wire or plastic netting is often pinned to the bags to give the plaster something to grip.

## What Are the Pros and Cons?

Earthbag construction has significant advantages over conventional building methods in the right context.

| Advantage | Disadvantage |
|---|---|
| Extremely low material cost | Very labor-intensive process |
| Uses on-site subsoil | Polypropylene bags degrade in UV light |
| Excellent thermal mass | Limited by local building codes |
| High earthquake resistance | Requires wide walls (typically 40 cm) |
| Fire-resistant when plastered | Plaster maintenance is ongoing |
| Simple to learn | Not widely accepted by lenders |

The technique shines in rural, off-grid, or permaculture homestead settings where labor is available and budgets are tight.

It is less practical in urban areas with strict code enforcement and high labor costs.

## Key Points

- Earthbag building uses polypropylene bags filled with damp subsoil, stacked and tamped into load-bearing walls.
- Barbed wire between courses provides tensile strength and prevents sliding.
- A rubble-trench foundation with gravel-filled first courses protects walls from moisture.
- Walls must be plastered with earthen or lime plaster to protect bags from UV degradation.
- Earthbag construction is extremely low-cost but very labor-intensive.
