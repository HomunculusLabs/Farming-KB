## What Distinguishes Ectomycorrhizal Fungi

Ectomycorrhizal fungi, commonly abbreviated as ECM, are characterized by their external colonization strategy. The
fungal hyphae encase the fine root tips of host plants in a dense tissue called the mantle, which can be 20 to 100
micrometers thick and is often visible to the naked eye as a color change or thickening of the root tip.

Between the root cells, the fungus forms an intricate intercellular network called the Hartig net, named after the
German forest pathologist Robert Hartig who first described it in the 1880s. This is the primary site of nutrient
exchange, where the fungus transfers mineral nutrients and water to the plant in return for photosynthetically derived
carbon compounds, primarily sugars.

## Host Plant Families

Ectomycorrhizal associations are found predominantly among woody plants in temperate, boreal, and some tropical
ecosystems. The most important ECM host families include Pinaceae (pines, spruces, firs, larches, hemlocks, and Douglas-
fir), Fagaceae (oaks, beeches, and chestnuts), Betulaceae (birches and alders), Salicaceae (poplars and willows),
Myrtaceae (eucalypts in Australia), and Dipterocarpaceae (tropical dipterocarp trees of Southeast Asia).

The Pinaceae family is arguably the most ecologically significant ECM host, as pine-dominated forests cover vast areas
of the northern hemisphere. Pines are obligately ectomycorrhizal in most natural settings, meaning they rarely survive
to maturity without ECM associations. Nursery practices for reforestation routinely include ECM inoculation to ensure
successful outplanting.

Oaks and their relatives in the Fagaceae are also heavily dependent on ectomycorrhizal partnerships. Mature oak trees
may associate with 100 or more ECM fungal species simultaneously, creating one of the most diverse mycorrhizal
communities documented in any ecosystem. This diversity provides functional redundancy and resilience against
environmental perturbations.

extraradical mycelium produce substantial quantities of chitin, glomalin, and other recalcitrant compounds that resist
decomposition and contribute to stable soil organic matter pools. Forests dominated by ECM trees tend to accumulate more
soil carbon than those dominated by arbuscular mycorrhizal or non-mycorrhizal vegetation.

Many ECM fungi produce fruiting bodies that serve as critical food resources for forest wildlife. Mammals including
deer, squirrels, chipmunks, voles, and bears consume large quantities of ECM mushrooms, dispersing spores through their

genus Tuber form exclusive ectomycorrhizal associations with specific host trees, primarily oaks and hazelnuts. Truffle

planting the colonized trees in carefully selected soils with appropriate pH, drainage, and mineral composition.
