# How to Use Rock Dust in Your Garden

## Short Answer
Rock dust is a natural soil amendment made from finely ground volcanic, glacial, or
sedimentary rocks that provides slow-release minerals and trace elements to your
garden. Apply it at 10 to 20 pounds per 100 square feet for garden beds, or mix 1
to 2 cups per cubic foot into potting soil. It works best when combined with
compost and biological inputs that help solubilize the minerals for plant uptake.

## Detailed Explanation

Rock dust, also called rock flour, stone dust, or mineral fines, is created by
crushing and grinding various types of rock into a powder or fine grit. The most
common sources are basalt (a volcanic rock rich in silica, iron, calcium, and
magnesium), glacial moraine gravel (a naturally diverse mix of minerals ground by
glaciers over millennia), and azomite (a volcanic ash deposit containing over 70
trace minerals). These materials provide a slow-release source of the major
secondary nutrients and micronutrients that are often depleted in intensively managed
soils. The [[rock-dust-minerals]] page provides a comprehensive comparison of
different rock dust types, their mineral profiles, and recommended application rates.

The primary benefit of rock dust is soil remineralization. Over decades of
cultivation, harvesting, and leaching, agricultural soils lose minerals faster than
they can be naturally replenished. Rock dust reintroduces the full spectrum of
elements that plants evolved to access, including trace minerals like cobalt,
molybdenum, selenium, and vanadium that are absent from most conventional
fertilizers. This broad mineral availability supports not just plant growth but the
entire soil food web, from bacteria and fungi that use minerals in their metabolism
to the larger organisms that depend on them. The [[nutrient-lockout]] page explains
how mineral balance affects nutrient availability to plants and why remineralization
is important.

However, rock dust alone is not a quick-fix fertilizer. The minerals in rock dust
are bound in silicate and oxide mineral structures that are not immediately
water-soluble. Plants cannot absorb them directly until soil microbes, particularly
bacteria and fungi, produce organic acids and enzymes that slowly dissolve the
mineral matrix over months and years. This is why rock dust is best understood as a
long-term soil-building amendment rather than a crop-feeding fertilizer. For maximum
effectiveness, always apply rock dust alongside [[comfrey-multifunctional-guild-plant]],
compost, or other organic matter that feeds the microbial community responsible for
mineral solubilization. The biological approach to making rock dust available is far
more effective than relying on chemical weathering alone.

In cannabis cultivation, rock dust can be mixed into the base soil at 1 to 2 cups
per cubic foot of potting mix. Many living soil growers include rock dust as a
standard component of their soil recipe because it provides a multi-year mineral
reserve that feeds the soil biology and, indirectly, the plants. Rock dust is
particularly valuable for preventing micronutrient deficiencies that can be
difficult to diagnose and correct in living soil systems, such as boron, copper,
manganese, and zinc deficiencies. The [[cannabis-living-soil-guide]] covers how to
integrate rock dust into a complete living soil recipe with proper particle size and
application depth.

For outdoor garden beds and food forests, rock dust can be applied as a top-dress at
10 to 20 pounds per 100 square feet, worked into the top few inches of soil.
Heavier applications (up to 40 pounds per 100 square feet) are sometimes used for
severely depleted soils, but should be split across multiple applications to avoid
temporary nutrient imbalances. Combining rock dust with [[biochar]] creates a
particularly effective soil amendment: the highly porous biochar provides habitat
for the microbes that solubilize the rock dust minerals, while the minerals
themselves charge the biochar's cation exchange sites with a diverse array of
nutrients. This synergy between rock dust and biochar is one of the most powerful
soil-building strategies available to organic growers.

Rock dust also improves soil physical properties in measurable ways. Fine rock
particles help improve soil structure by filling voids between larger particles,
which improves water retention in sandy soils and drainage in clay soils. The
silicate minerals in basalt dust can also contribute to the formation of stable
soil aggregates that resist erosion and compaction, improving root penetration and
aeration. When combined with [[kelp-meal-fertilizer]] for trace minerals and
hormones, and compost for microbial food and organic matter, rock dust forms the
mineral backbone of a complete, self-sustaining soil fertility program that improves
with each season of application.

Application timing matters for rock dust effectiveness. Fall applications are ideal
for outdoor gardens because the winter freeze-thaw cycle and spring microbial
activity help begin breaking down the mineral matrix before the growing season. For
container gardens, rock dust should be mixed into the soil at least 2 to 4 weeks
before planting to allow initial microbial colonization. Rock dust can also be
added to compost piles at a rate of 1 to 2 cups per cubic yard to enrich the
finished compost with additional minerals. The [[cervantes-organic-mineral-amendments]]
page provides specific integration guidance for rock dust in cannabis-specific
growing systems.

## Key Points
- Rock dust provides slow-release minerals and trace elements through microbial
  solubilization over months to years, not instantly like synthetic fertilizers
- Apply at 10 to 20 pounds per 100 square feet for beds, or 1 to 2 cups per cubic
  foot for potting mixes
- Best combined with compost, kelp meal, and biochar for maximum biological activity
  and mineral availability
- [[rock-dust-minerals]] compares basalt, glacial, azomite, and other rock dust
  sources with full mineral analyses
- It is a long-term soil-building amendment; benefits compound over multiple seasons
  as the mineral matrix gradually weathers
