# Why Is [[comfrey]] Controversial?

Comfrey (*Symphytum [[comfrey-symphytum-officinale]]* and related species) is one of the most polarizing plants in both herbal medicine and permaculture. Widely valued for its [[dynamic-accumulator-plants-potassium]] properties, deep taproot, and rapid biomass production, comfrey simultaneously faces significant health and ecological concerns. This page addresses the core controversies factually and in plain language.

---

## What are pyrrolizidine alkaloids (PAs) and which comfrey species contain them?

Pyrrolizidine alkaloids are a family of naturally occurring hepatotoxic compounds produced by many plants as chemical defenses against herbivores. Comfrey—particularly *Symphytum officinale* (common comfrey), *S. asperum* (rough comfrey), and their hybrid *S. × uplandicum* (Russian comfrey)—contains unsaturated PAs, the most toxic subclass within this family.

These include intermedine, lycopsamine, and their acetyl and N-oxide derivatives. Concentrations vary considerably by species, plant part, harvest time, and growing conditions. Roots generally contain the highest PA levels (often 10× leaf concentrations), followed by leaves, with flowers typically the lowest but still detectable.

Wild-harvested common comfrey tends to have higher and more variable PA content than cultivated Russian comfrey varieties, but no comfrey tissue has been shown to be reliably PA-free. Soil conditions, [[nitrogen-availability-in-legumes]], and plant stress all influence PA biosynthesis, making absolute safety claims for any given plant impossible.

---

## Is comfrey actually dangerous to the liver?

Yes, the hepatotoxicity of comfrey PAs is well-established in the scientific literature and is not seriously disputed among toxicologists.

Unsaturated PAs are metabolized by the liver (via [[cytochrome-p450-enzymes-structure-function-pharmacology]] enzymes) into reactive pyrrolic esters that bind covalently to cellular proteins and DNA. This damage causes hepatic veno-occlusive disease (VOD)—a condition in which the small veins of the liver become blocked and fibrotic, leading to cirrhosis, portal hypertension, and potentially acute liver failure.

The evidence base includes:

- **Animal studies:** Rat and rodent models consistently show liver tumors and VOD at sustained doses relevant to human consumption patterns. PA-induced liver tumors are dose-dependent but occur at relatively low cumulative exposures.
- **Human case reports:** Multiple documented cases of VOD following comfrey consumption exist in the medical literature, including fatalities. Most cases involved long-term use of comfrey tea, root preparations, or supplements.
- **Genotoxicity data:** In vitro studies confirm that comfrey PAs are directly genotoxic (DNA-damaging), which is the mechanistic basis for their classification as probable human carcinogens by bodies such as IARC.

The dose-response relationship is not linear and individual susceptibility varies based on genetics, concurrent liver stressors (alcohol, other medications), and duration of exposure. Chronic low-dose exposure carries cumulative risk that may not manifest for years.

## What do regulatory agencies say about comfrey?

Regulatory bodies worldwide have converged on a cautious-to-restrictive stance:

- **FDA (United States):** In 2001, the FDA issued an advisory requesting that dietary supplement manufacturers remove comfrey products from the market. Comfrey is not GRAS (Generally Recognized As Safe) for internal use. It remains legal to grow and sell as an ornamental or horticultural plant, but not as a food or dietary supplement ingredient.
- **EMA (European Union):** The European Medicines Agency's HMPC assessed comfrey in 2011 and concluded that herbal medicines containing unsaturated PAs should not be used internally. Topical use on unbroken skin was considered acceptable under specific conditions (short duration, intact skin, no open wounds), though several EU member states have further restricted even external preparations.
- **BfR (Germany):** Germany's Federal Institute for Risk Assessment has issued multiple opinions on PA-containing plants. Germany has among the strictest PA limits in food and herbal products, with maximum permitted levels in the low microgram-per-kilogram range for ready-to-eat products.
- **TGA (Australia):** The Therapeutic Goods Administration classifies comfrey as a restricted substance. Products containing comfrey must carry warning labels, and internal use in listed medicines is prohibited. Australia has set regulatory limits for PAs in foods and therapeutic goods.

Internal consumption of comfrey is banned or heavily restricted across virtually all major regulatory jurisdictions.

## What about the sterile cultivar Bocking 14—is it safe?

The Bocking 14 cultivar (*Symphytum × uplandicum* 'Bocking 14') is widely promoted in permaculture as a sterile clone that will not spread by seed. This sterility is real and valuable for controlling unwanted [[seed-dispersal-ecology-and-mechanisms]].

However, Bocking 14 is **not** PA-free. Analytical studies have confirmed measurable levels of unsaturated PAs in Bocking 14 leaves and roots, though some reports suggest slightly lower concentrations than common comfrey. The sterility addresses only the invasive spread question, not the toxicological one.

Claims that Bocking 14 is "safe" for internal use are not supported by the available evidence and are contradicted by regulatory assessments across all major jurisdictions. Bocking 14 is excellent for horticultural use (mulch, compost, bee forage) but should not be consumed.

## Is topical comfrey use safe?

Topical application of comfrey to intact, unbroken skin for short periods (days to a few weeks) is generally considered low-risk by EMA and some European regulators. PA absorption through intact skin is significantly lower than oral absorption, and systemic exposure from normal topical use is minimal.

Comfrey-based creams and ointments for muscle and joint pain have a long history in European phytotherapy, and several randomized clinical trials support efficacy for osteoarthritis of the knee and acute back pain. These products typically use purified comfrey extract with controlled PA content.

Caveats remain: avoid applying comfrey to broken skin, open wounds, or large body surface areas, as systemic absorption increases substantially. Long-term daily topical use has not been well-studied. The prudent approach is short-term use on intact skin only, preferably with commercially prepared products whose PA content has been tested.

## Why is comfrey considered an invasive species?

Beyond toxicity, comfrey's aggressive growth habit is a second major axis of controversy. Comfrey spreads primarily through root fragmentation—even very small root pieces can regenerate into new plants. This makes it extremely difficult to eradicate once established where it is not wanted.

In some regions, particularly temperate areas of North America, New Zealand, and parts of Australia, comfrey has naturalized outside cultivation and is listed as an environmental weed. Russian comfrey (*S. × uplandicum*) is particularly vigorous.

The deep taproot (often 1–2+ meters) makes mechanical removal challenging and increases the risk of root pieces breaking off and resprouting. Bocking 14's sterility prevents seed dispersal but does not prevent root-fragment spread if the plant is disturbed. Gardeners should site comfrey thoughtfully, away from waterways, property boundaries, and sensitive native plant areas.

## How does the permaculture community view comfrey?

Comfrey occupies a central and somewhat uncritical place in permaculture practice. It is valued as a dynamic accumulator (drawing potassium, calcium, and other minerals from deep subsoil layers), a nutrient-rich mulch and compost activator, a high-quality bee forage plant, and a companion planting species.

Many permaculture practitioners recommend comfrey freely and dismiss or downplay PA concerns, sometimes citing traditional use or the argument that "our grandparents ate it without problems." This perspective often overlooks that traditional use was not risk-free, that liver damage from PAs can be insidious and slow-developing, and that modern analytical methods have revealed hazards that were invisible to traditional knowledge systems.

The permaculture community's embrace of comfrey is not wrong per se—comfrey has genuine and well-documented horticultural value—but the tendency to minimize or ignore the toxicological evidence creates a credibility gap with health professionals and regulators, and can put well-meaning practitioners at risk.

---

## What is the gap between traditional use and modern safety data?

Comfrey has been used medicinally for centuries in European and Asian herbal traditions, both internally (as teas, decoctions, and eaten greens) and externally (as poultices and salves). The traditional perspective holds that this long history of use validates safety.

Modern toxicology, however, has demonstrated that chronic PA exposure can cause liver damage that is slow, cumulative, and not immediately apparent to the user. Historical users may have experienced subclinical liver damage that went unrecognized and was attributed to other causes. Furthermore, the modern scientific framework accounts for genotoxic carcinogenicity data that traditional systems had no method to detect.

This is not simply "old vs. new" but reflects fundamentally different epistemologies of risk. Traditional knowledge is valuable and should not be dismissed, but it is not a substitute for controlled toxicological investigation.

## Practical risk assessment for gardeners and homesteaders

| Activity | Risk Level | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Growing comfrey as mulch/compost | Very Low | PA transfer to soil and food crops is minimal |
| Handling and cutting comfrey | Very Low | PAs are not volatile; normal garden hygiene sufficient |
| Topical use on intact skin, short-term | Low | Use commercially prepared products; avoid broken skin |
| Long-term daily topical use | Moderate | Insufficient data; not recommended |
| Internal use (tea, tincture, eating leaves) | Moderate–High | Not recommended; cumulative hepatotoxic risk |
| Internal use of root preparations | High | Roots contain highest PA concentrations |
| Planting near waterways or sensitive habitats | Moderate | Consider invasive potential and local regulations |

## Summary

The comfrey controversy exists because the plant occupies a genuine middle ground. It is not a binary "safe" or "dangerous" plant. Comfrey is an excellent horticultural species with real ecological and permaculture benefits, and the risks associated with growing and handling it are low when used appropriately for mulch, compost, and bee forage. At the same time, the hepatotoxicity of its PAs is well-established, internal use poses real cumulative [[health-risks-of-hallucinogenic-mushrooms]], and its invasive potential requires responsible siting. The controversy is perpetuated primarily by advocates who overstate safety and critics who overstate danger. A balanced, evidence-based approach recognizes both the genuine utility and the genuine hazards of this remarkable plant.
