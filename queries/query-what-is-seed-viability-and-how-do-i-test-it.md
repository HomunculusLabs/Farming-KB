# What Is Seed Viability and How Do I Test It?

Seed viability refers to a seed's ability to germinate and produce a healthy seedling under favorable conditions. Not all saved or purchased seeds remain viable forever — each species has a characteristic shelf life, and storage conditions dramatically affect how long seeds stay alive. Testing viability before planting saves time, effort, and growing space.

---

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the difference between seed viability and seed vigor?

**Viability** is a binary measure — can the seed germinate at all? **Vigor** describes how strong, fast, and uniform the resulting seedling is. A seed can be viable (it germinates) but low-vigor (it produces a weak, slow-growing seedling). Both matter, but viability is the baseline you test at home.

### How long do seeds typically remain viable?

Shelf life varies enormously by species when stored under cool, dry conditions:

| Category | Examples | Typical Viability |
|---|---|---|
| **Very short (1–2 years)** | Onion, leek, parsnip, corn salad, pepper (some) | 50–70% by year 2 |
| **Short (2–3 years)** | Pepper, okra, Swiss chard, beans | 50–80% by year 3 |
| **Medium (3–5 years)** | Tomato, carrot, beet, squash, cucumber | 50–80% by year 4–5 |
| **Long (5–8 years)** | Lettuce, radish, turnip, peas | 60–90% by year 5 |
| **Very long (8–15+ years)** | Some brassicas, beans (dry), grains | 50–70% beyond 8 years |

These are rough guidelines. Proper storage can extend all of these significantly.

### What storage conditions maximize seed longevity?

Three factors govern seed lifespan:

- **Temperature:** Seeds store best at cold temperatures. Every 10°F (5.5°C) reduction in storage temperature roughly doubles seed life. Refrigeration (35–40°F / 2–4°C) is good; freezing (0°F / −18°C) is better for dry seeds.
- **Moisture:** Seeds must be dried to low moisture content (below 8% for most species) before freezing. A simple test: if a seed bends, it's too moist to freeze — it will crack. It should shatter or snap.
- **Darkness and stability:** Store seeds in airtight, opaque containers (glass jars with desiccant packets work well). Avoid temperature cycling — a steady 40°F is better than fluctuating between 35°F and 70°F.

For long-term storage, use silica gel desiccant packs inside sealed jars kept in a freezer. Let frozen seeds warm to room temperature inside the sealed jar before opening to prevent condensation.

### How do I do a simple paper-towel germination test?

This is the most common home viability test:

1. **Moisten** a paper towel (not dripping wet — wring it out so it's damp).
2. **Count out** 10 seeds of one variety (10 makes the math easy for percentages).
3. **Arrange** seeds in a single row, spaced apart, on half the towel.
4. **Fold** the towel over the seeds and place it inside a labeled ziplock bag. Leave the bag open slightly for airflow, or seal it to retain moisture (both work — sealing retains more moisture for long-germinating seeds).
5. **Place** the bag in a warm spot appropriate for the species (65–75°F / 18–24°C for most vegetables; warmer for peppers and eggplant at 80–85°F / 27–29°C).
6. **Check daily** for germination. Add a few drops of water if the towel dries out.
7. **Count** germinated seeds at the species' typical germination time (usually 7–14 days). A seed counts as germinated when the radicle (root tip) emerges.

**Calculate viability:** If 7 out of 10 seeds germinate, viability is 70%.

### What if seeds need special conditions to germinate?

Some seeds have requirements that a simple paper towel won't provide:

- **Light-requiring seeds** (lettuce, some herbs, celery): Press them against the towel surface and place the bag where they get indirect light. Don't bury them under multiple layers.
- **Cold-stratification seeds** (many perennials, some herbs): Place the damp paper towel with seeds in the refrigerator (not freezer) for the required period (typically 2–8 weeks) before moving to warmth.
- **Hard-seeded species** (beans, morning glory, lupines, some legumes): Nick the seed coat with a nail file or soak in warm water for 12–24 hours before the germination test.
- **Smoke-water or fire-treated seeds** (some proteas, certain Australian and South African species): These won't germinate on a paper towel without their specific trigger. Research species requirements before testing.

### How do I interpret the germination test results?

| Germination Rate | Recommendation |
|---|---|
| **90–100%** | Excellent — plant at normal spacing |
| **75–89%** | Good — plant slightly thick, thin later |
| **50–74%** | Declining — sow 1.5–2× normal rate, or replace soon |
| **Below 50%** | Poor — use for microgreens/sprouts, or discard and buy fresh seed |
| **0%** | Dead — compost them |

### Are there other ways to test viability without germinating?

Yes, though they're less accurate than a full germination test:

- **Water float test:** Drop seeds in water. Viable seeds typically sink; non-viable seeds often float. This works well for larger seeds (beans, peas, corn) but is unreliable for small seeds. Note: some viable seeds float due to air trapped in the seed coat, so this test has false positives.
- **Cut test:** Carefully cut open a few seeds and inspect the embryo. A firm, white or pale interior indicates viability; dark, mushy, or hollow interiors suggest the seed is dead. This destroys the seed but gives an instant answer.
- **Tetrazolium test:** A chemical stain (2,3,5-triphenyl tetrazolium chloride) turns living tissue red and dead tissue colorless. Used by seed labs for precise results. You can buy tetrazolium chloride online, but it requires careful handling and interpretation.

### Does seed size or appearance predict viability?

Not reliably. Small, shriveled, or discolored seeds *tend* to have lower viability, but plenty of small, unpromising-looking seeds germinate fine. The only way to know is to test. That said, if seeds are visibly moldy, cracked, or have insect damage, they're likely non-viable.

### How often should I test my seed collection?

- **Before each planting season** is the minimum — test seeds you plan to sow.
- **Annually** for long-stored seeds or a large collection.
- **After storage disruptions** (freezer failure, humidity exposure, pest damage).

Keep a simple log with the variety, seed lot (if known), storage date, and germination test results each year. This builds a personal database of how long each variety lasts under your specific storage conditions.

### Can I improve the germination rate of old seeds?

Several pre-treatments can help struggling seeds:

- **Soaking** in water for 12–24 hours rehydrates seeds and can speed germination. Add a drop of liquid kelp or chamomile tea as a mild anti-fungal.
- **Scarification** (nicking, sanding, or acid treatment) breaks through hard seed coats that develop with age.
- **Stratification** (cold, moist treatment) can break dormancy that deepens as seeds age.
- **Gibberellic acid (GA3)** soaking can break deep dormancy in recalcitrant seeds. Use sparingly — too much causes weak, leggy seedlings.

Even with treatments, expect lower vigor from old seeds. Plant them deeper than usual and keep conditions optimal to give them every advantage.

### Should I plant old seeds directly in the garden or start them indoors?

For seeds below 75% viability, starting indoors (or in seedling trays) is smarter than direct sowing. You can monitor germination closely, thin out failures, and replace gaps. Direct-sowing low-viability seed in garden beds wastes space and creates uneven stands. Start them in trays, count what germinates, and only transplant the strong seedlings.

### How do seed banks maintain viability for decades?

Professional seed banks (like the Svalbard Global Seed Vault and USDA's National Plant Germplasm System) use three key practices:

1. **Ultra-dry storage:** Seeds are dried to 3–6% moisture content using controlled drying rooms.
2. **Sub-zero freezing:** Seeds are stored at −18°C (0°F) or colder. At this temperature, most orthodox seeds remain viable for 50–100+ years.
3. **Periodic regeneration:** Seed banks periodically grow out stored accessions, harvest fresh seed, and replace aging stock before viability drops too low.

Home seed savers can approximate these conditions with a home freezer, desiccant, and airtight glass jars — achieving 10–20 year storage for many common vegetables.

### What are "orthodox" vs. "recalcitrant" seeds?

- **Orthodox seeds** can be dried to low moisture and frozen for long-term storage. Most vegetable, grain, and flower seeds are orthodox.
- **Recalcitrant seeds** (also called "intermediate" or "sub-orthodox") are damaged by drying and cannot survive freezing. These include many tropical and subtropical species: avocado, mango, cacao, rubber tree, some citrus, and many oak species. Recalcitrant seeds must be stored moist and cool, and typically last only weeks to months.
